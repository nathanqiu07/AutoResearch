## Abstract

The vast majority of theoretical results in machine learning and statistics assume that the available training data is a reasonably reliable reflection of the phenomena to be learned or estimated. Similarly, the majority of machine learning and statistical techniques used in practice are brittle to the presence of large amounts of biased or malicious data. In this work we consider two frameworks in which to study estimation, learning, and optimization in the presence of significant fractions of arbitrary data.

The first framework, list-decodable learning, asks whether it is possible to return a list of answers, with the guarantee that at least one of them is accurate. For example, given a dataset of $n$ points for which an unknown subset of $\alpha n$ points are drawn from a distribution of interest, and no assumptions are made about the remaining $(1-\alpha)n$ points, is it possible to return a list of $\operatorname{poly}(1/\alpha)$ answers, one of which is correct? The second framework, which we term the semi-verified learning model, considers the extent to which a small dataset of trusted data (drawn from the distribution in question) can be leveraged to enable the accurate extraction of information from a much larger but untrusted dataset (of which only an $\alpha$-fraction is drawn from the distribution).

We show strong positive results in both settings, and provide an algorithm for robust learning in a very general stochastic optimization setting. This general result has immediate implications for robust estimation in a number of settings, including for robustly estimating the mean of distributions with bounded second moments, robustly learning mixtures of such distributions, and robustly finding planted partitions in random graphs in which significant portions of the graph have been perturbed by an adversary.

## 1 Introduction

What can be learned from data that is only partially trusted? In this paper, we study this question by considering the following setting: we observe $n$ data points, of which $\alpha n$ are drawn independently from a distribution of interest, $p^{*}$, and we make no assumptions about the remaining $(1-\alpha)n$ points—they could be very biased, arbitrary, or chosen by an adversary who is trying to obscure $p^{*}$. Our goal is to accurately recover a parameter of interest of $p^{*}$ (such as the mean), despite the presence of significant amounts of untrusted data. Perhaps surprisingly, we will show that in high dimensions, accurate estimation and learning is often possible, even when the fraction of real data is small (i.e., $\alpha\ll 1$). To do this, we consider two notions of successful learning–the list decodable model and the semi-verified model–and provide strong positive results for both notions. Our results have implications in a variety of domains, including building secure machine learning systems, performing robust statistics in the presence of outliers, and agnostically learning mixture models.

The goal of accurate robust estimation appears at first glance to be impossible if the fraction $\alpha$ of real data is less than one half. Indeed, in the $\alpha=\frac{1}{2}$ case, it is possible that the real and fake data are distributed identically, except that the mean of the fake data is shifted by some large amount; in such a case, it is clearly impossible to differentiate which of these two distributions is “right”. Perhaps, however, such symmetries are the only real problem that can occur. It might then be possible to output a short list of possible parameter sets–if $\alpha=\frac{1}{2}$, perhaps a list of two parameter sets–such that at least one is accurate. To this end, we consider a notion of successful learning called list decodable learning, first introduced by Balcan et al. (2008). In analogy with list decodable coding theory, the goal is for the learning algorithm to output a short list of possible hypotheses.

**Definition (List Decodable Learning).**

We say that a learning, estimation, or optimization problem is $(m,\epsilon)$ list decodably solvable if an efficient algorithm can output a set of at most $m$ hypotheses/estimates/answers, with the guarantee that at least one is accurate to within error $\epsilon$.

A central question in this paper concerns which learning problems can be robustly solved in the above sense:

> To what extent are learning problems robustly solvable in the list decodable sense? If the dataset consists of only an $\alpha$-fraction of real data, in what settings is it possible to efficiently output a list of at most $\frac{1}{\alpha}$ or $\operatorname{poly}(\frac{1}{\alpha})$ parameter sets or estimates with the guarantee that at least one closely approximates the solution that could be obtained if one were given only honest data?

The intuition for why strong positive results are obtainable in the list decodable setting is the following. Given a dataset with an $\alpha$ fraction of trusted data, the remaining data might do one of two things: either it can be fairly similar to the good data, in which case it can bias the overall answers by only a small amount, or the adversarial data may be very different from the trusted data. The key is that if a portion of the untrusted data tries too hard to bias the final result, then it will end up looking quite different, and can be clustered out. Given this viewpoint, the question of robust learning in the list decodable model becomes a question of the extent to which an adversary can completely obscure the inherent structure possessed by a dataset drawn from a well-behaved distribution.

Our investigation of robust learning has three motivations. First, from a theoretical perspective, it is natural to ask what guarantees are possible in the setting in which a majority of data is untrusted ($\alpha<\frac{1}{2}$). Is it the case that learning really becomes impossible (as is often stated), or can one at least narrow down the possible answers to a small set? Second, in many practical settings, there is a trade-off between the amount of data one can collect, and the quality of the data. For a fixed price, one might be able to collect either a small and accurate/trusted dataset, or a large but less trusted dataset. It is worth understanding how the quality of models derived from such datasets varies, across this entire range of dataset quality/quantity. Finally, robust learning with $\alpha\ll 1$ provides a new perspective on learning mixtures of distributions—by treating a single mixture component as the real data, and the remaining components as fake data, we can ask to what extent a mixture component can be learned, independently of the structure of the other components. While this perspective may seem to give up too much, we will show, somewhat surprisingly, that it is possible to learn mixtures almost as well under these adversarial assumptions as under stochastic assumptions.

##### Semi-Verified Learning.

When $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$, the list decodable model handles symmetries by allowing the learner to output multiple possible answers; an alternative is to break these symmetries with a small amount of side information. In particular, in many practical settings it is possible to obtain a (sometimes extremely small) verified set of data that has been carefully checked, which could be used to determine which of multiple alternative answers is correct. This motivates us to introduce the following new notion of learnability:

**Definition (The Semi-Verified Model).**

In the semi-verified model, we observe $n$ data points, of which an unknown $\alpha n$ are “real” data reflecting an underlying distribution $p^{*}$, and the remaining $(1-\alpha)n$ points are arbitrary. Furthermore, we observe $k$ “verified” data points that are guaranteed to be drawn from $p^{*}$.

The definition of the semi-verified model is inspired by the semi-supervised model of learning (see e.g. Chapelle et al. (2006)). In semi-supervised learning, one is concerned with a prediction/labeling task, and has access to a large amount of unlabeled data together with a small amount of labeled data; the central question is whether the presence of the unlabeled data can reduce the amount of labeled data required to learn. Analogously, in our robust learning setting, we are asking whether the presence of a large amount of untrusted data can reduce the amount of trusted data required for learning. Clearly the answer is “no” if we make no assumptions on the untrusted data. Nevertheless, the assumption that a significant fraction of that data is drawn from $p^{*}$ seems plausible, and may be sufficient to achieve strong positive results. We therefore ask:

> To what extent can the availability of a modest amount of “verified” data facilitate (either computationally or information theoretically) the extraction of the information contained in the larger but untrusted dataset? What learning tasks can be performed in the above semi-verified setting given $k\ll n$ verified data points? How does the amount $k$ of verified data that is needed vary with the setting, the fraction $\alpha$ of honest data, etc.?

The above definition and associated questions reflect challenges faced in a number of practical settings, particularly those involving large crowdsourced datasets, or datasets obtained from unreliable sensors or devices. In such settings, despite the unreliability of the data, it is often possible to obtain a small verified dataset that has been carefully checked. Given its pervasiveness, it is somewhat surprising that neither the theory nor the machine learning communities have formalized this model; we think it is important to develop an understanding of the algorithmic possibilities in this domain. Obtaining theoretical guarantees seems especially important for designing provably secure learning systems that are guaranteed to perform well even if an adversary obtains control over some of the training data used by the algorithm.

##### Relationships between the models.

The semi-verified and list decodable models can be reduced to each other. Informally, given $m$ candidate outputs from a list decodable algorithm, we expect to be able to distinguish between them with $\mathcal{O}\left(\log(m)\right)$ verified data points. Conversely, if a model is learnable with $k$ verified points then we can output $\mathcal{O}\left(({\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{k}\right)$ candidate parameters in the list decodable setting (since if we sample that many $k$-tuples from the untrusted data, at least one is likely to contain only honest data). For simplicity we state most results in the list decodable model.

##### Our contributions.

We provide results on robust learnability in a general stochastic optimization setting, where we observe convex functions $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$ of which $\alpha n$ are sampled from $p^{*}$, and want to minimize the population mean $\bar{f}=\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[f]$.[^1] Our results are given in terms of a spectral norm bound on the gradients $\nabla f_{i}$. Therefore, we obtain robustness in any setting where we can establish a matrix concentration inequality on the good data — for instance, if the $\nabla f_{i}$ are sub-Gaussian and Lipschitz, or sometimes even with only bounded second moments.

From our general results (discussed in detail in the next section), we immediately obtain corollaries in specific settings, starting with mean estimation:

- Robust mean estimation: When $\alpha>\frac{1}{2}$ we can robustly estimate the mean of a distribution $p^{*}$ to $\ell_{2}$ error $\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$, where $\sigma^{2}$ is a bound on the second moments of $p^{*}$. For $\alpha$ bounded away from $1$, this improves upon existing work, which achieves error either $\mathcal{O}{(\sigma\sqrt{\log(d)})}$ under a $4$th moment bound on $p^{*}$ (Lai et al., 2016) or matches our rate of $\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$ but assumes $p^{*}$ is sub-Gaussian (Diakonikolas et al., 2016). For $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$, which was previously unexplored, we can estimate the mean to error $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma/\sqrt{\alpha}\right)$.

Since our results hold for any stochastic optimization problem, we can also study density estimation, by taking $f_{i}$ to be the negative log-likelihood:

- Robust density estimation: Given an exponential family model $p_{\theta}(x)\propto\exp\left(\theta^{\top}\phi(x)\right)$, we can find a distribution $p_{{\theta}}$ with $\operatorname{KL}\left(p_{\theta^{*}}\ \|\ p_{{\theta}}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}({\frac{\sigma r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}})$, where $\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\theta^{*}}}[\phi(x)]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$ and $r=\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}$.

While density estimation could be reduced to mean estimation (via estimating the sufficient statistics), our analysis applies directly, to an algorithm that can be interpreted as approximately maximizing the log likelihood while removing outliers.

In the list decodable setting, our results also yield bounds for learning mixtures:

- Learning mixture models: Given a mixture of $k$ distributions each with covariance bounded by $\sigma^{2}$, and with minimum mixture weight $\alpha$, we can accurately cluster the points if the means are separated by a distance $\tilde{\Omega}\left(\sigma/\sqrt{\alpha}\right)$, even in the presence of additional adversarial data. For comparison, even with few/no bad data points, the best efficient clustering algorithms require mean separation $\tilde{\Omega}{(\sigma\sqrt{k})}$ (Awasthi and Sheffet, 2012; Achlioptas and McSherry, 2005), which our rate matches if $\alpha=\Omega(\tfrac{1}{k})$.
- Planted partition models: In the planted partition model, we can approximately recover the planted partition if the average degree is $\tilde{\Omega}(1/\alpha^{3})$, where $\alpha n$ is the size of the smallest piece of the partition. The best computationally efficient result (which assumes all the data is real) requires the degree to be $\Omega(1/\alpha^{2})$ (Abbe and Sandon, 2015a; b).

It is fairly surprising that, despite making no assumptions on the structure of the data outside of a mixture component, we nearly match the best computationally efficient results that fully leverage this structure. This suggests that there may be a connection between robustness and computation: perhaps the computational threshold for recovering a planted structure in random data (such as a geometric cluster or a high-density subgraph) matches the robustness threshold for recovering that structure in the presence of an adversary.

##### Technical highlights.

Beyond our main results, we develop certain technical machinery that may be of broader interest. Perhaps the most relevant is a novel matrix concentration inequality, based on ideas from spectral graph sparsification (Batson et al., 2012), which holds assuming only bounded second moments:[^2]

**Proposition 1.1.**

Suppose that $p$ is a distribution on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $\mathbb{E}_{p}[X]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Cov}_{p}[X]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$ for some $\sigma$. Then, given $n\geq d$ samples from $p$, with probability $1-\exp\left(-\frac{n}{64}\right)$ there is a subset $I\subseteq[n]$ of size at least $\frac{n}{2}$ such that $\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}(x_{i}-\mu)(x_{i}-\mu)^{\top}\right)\leq 24\sigma^{2}$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ denotes the maximum eigenvalue.

This result is strong in the following sense: if one instead uses all $n$ samples $x_{i}$, the classical result of Rudelson (1999) only bounds the maximum eigenvalue by roughly ${\sigma^{2}\log(n)}$, and even then only in expectation. Even under stronger assumptions, one often needs either at least $d\log(d)$ samples, or incurs a $\log(d)$ factor in the bound on $\lambda_{\max}$. In the planted partition model, this log factor causes natural spectral approaches to fail on sparse graphs, and avoiding the log factor has been a topic of recent interest (Guédon and Vershynin, 2014; Le et al., 2015; Rebrova and Tikhomirov, 2015; Rebrova and Vershynin, 2016).

Proposition 1.1 says that the undesirable log factor only arises due to a manageable fraction of bad samples, which when removed give us sharper concentration. Our framework allows us to exploit this by defining the good data to be the (unknown) set $I$ for which the conclusion of Proposition 1.1 holds. One consequence is that we are able to recover planted partitions in sparse graphs essentially “for free”.

Separately, we introduce a novel regularizer based on minimum trace ellipsoids. This regularizer allows us to control the spectral properties of the model parameters at multiple scales simultaneously, and yields tighter bounds than standard trace norm regularization. We define the regularizer in Section 3, and prove a local Hölder’s inequality (Lemma 5.1), which exploits this regularization to achieve concentration bounds solely from deterministic spectral information.

We also employ padded decompositions, a space partitioning technique from the metric embedding literature (Fakcharoenphol et al., 2003). Their use is the following: when the loss functions are strongly convex, we can improve our bounds by identifying clusters in the data, and re-running our main algorithm on each cluster. Padded decompositions help us because they can identify clusters even if the remaining data has arbitrary structure. Our clustering scheme is described in Section 6.

##### Related work.

The work closest to ours is Lai et al. (2016) and Diakonikolas et al. (2016), who study high-dimensional estimation in the presence of adversarial corruptions. They focus on the regime $\alpha\approx 1$, while our work focuses on $\alpha\ll 1$. In the overlap of these regimes (e.g. for $\alpha=\frac{3}{4}$) our results improve upon these existing results. (The existing bounds are better as $\alpha\to 1$, but do not hold at all if $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$.) The popular robust PCA algorithm (Candès et al., 2011; Chandrasekaran et al., 2011) allows for a constant fraction of the entries to be arbitrarily corrupted, but assumes the locations of these entries are sufficiently evenly distributed. However, Xu et al. (2010) give a version of PCA that is robust to arbitrary adversaries if $\alpha>\frac{1}{2}$. Bhatia et al. (2015) study linear regression in the presence of adversaries, and obtain bounds for sufficiently large $\alpha$ (say $\alpha\geq\frac{64}{65}$) when the design matrix is subset strong convex. Klivans et al. (2009) and Awasthi et al. (2014) provide strong bounds for robust classification in high dimensions for isotropic log-concave distributions.

The only works we are aware of that achieve general adversarial guarantees when $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$ are Hardt and Moitra (2013), who study robust subspace recovery in the presence of a large fraction of outliers, and Steinhardt et al. (2016), which is an early version of this work that focuses on community detection.

Balcan et al. (2008) introduce the list-decodable learning model, which was later studied by others, e.g. Balcan et al. (2009) and Kushagra et al. (2016). That work provides bounds for clustering in the presence of some adversarial data, but has two limitations relative to our results (apart from being in a somewhat different setting): the fraction of adversaries tolerated is small ($\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k})$), and the bounds are not meaningful in high dimensions (e.g. Balcan et al. (2008) output a list of $k^{\mathcal{O}(k/\gamma^{2})}$ hypotheses, where $\gamma$ typically scales as $1/\sqrt{d}$).

Kumar and Kannan (2010) and the follow-up work of Awasthi and Sheffet (2012) find deterministic conditions under which efficient $k$-means clustering is possible, even in high dimensions. While the goal is different from ours, their condition is similar to the quantities appearing in our error bounds, and there is some overlap in techniques. They also obtain bounds in the presence of adversaries, but only if the fraction of adversaries is smaller than $\frac{1}{k}$ as in Balcan et al. (2008). Our corollaries for learning mixtures can be thought of as extending this line of work, by providing deterministic conditions under which clustering is possible even in the presence of a large fraction of adversarial data.

Separately, there has been considerable interest in semi-random graph models (Blum and Spencer, 1995; Feige and Krauthgamer, 2000; Feige and Kilian, 2001; Coja-Oghlan, 2004; Krivelevich and Vilenchik, 2006; Coja-Oghlan, 2007; Makarychev et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2014b; Guédon and Vershynin, 2014; Moitra et al., 2015; Agarwal et al., 2015) and robust community detection (Kumar and Kannan, 2010; Moitra et al., 2015; Makarychev et al., 2015; Cai and Li, 2015). In these models, a random graph is generated with a planted structure (such as a planted clique or partition) and adversaries are then allowed to modify some parts of this structure. Typically, the adversary is constrained to only modify $o(n)$ nodes or to only modify the graph in restricted ways, though some of the above work considers substantially stronger adversaries as well.

Robust learning is interesting from not just an information-theoretic but also a computational perspective. Guruswami and Raghavendra (2009) and Feldman et al. (2009) show that learning half-spaces is NP-hard for any $\alpha<1$, while Hardt and Moitra (2013) show that learning $k$-dimensional subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is hard if $\alpha<\frac{k}{d}$. More generally, algorithms for list decodable learning imply algorithms for learning mixture models, e.g. planted partitions or mixtures of sub-Gaussian distributions, which is thought to be computationally hard in at least some regimes.

Finally, there is a large literature on learning in the presence of errors, spanning multiple communities including learning theory (Kearns and Li, 1993) and statistics (Tukey, 1960). We refer the reader to Huber and Ronchetti (2009) and Hampel et al. (2011) for some recent surveys.

##### Comparison of techniques.

We pause to explain how our techniques relate to those of other recent robust learning papers by Diakonikolas et al. (2016) and Lai et al. (2016). At a high level, our algorithm works by solving a convex optimization problem whose objective value will be low if all of the data come from $p^{*}$; then, if the objective is high, by looking at the dual we can identify which points are responsible for the high objective value and remove them as outliers.

In contrast, Diakonikolas et al. (2016) solve a convex feasibility problem, where the feasible set depends on the true distribution $p^{*}$ and hence is not observable. Nevertheless, they show that given a point that is far from feasible, it is possible to provide a separating hyperplane demonstrating infeasibility. Roughly speaking, then, we solve a “tainted” optimization problem and clean up errors after the fact, while they solve a “clean” (but unobserved) optimization problem and show that it is possible to make progress if one is far from the optimum. The construction of the separation oracle in Diakonikolas et al. (2016) is similar to the outlier removal step we present here, and it would be interesting to further understand the relationship between these approaches.

Diakonikolas et al. (2016) also propose another algorithm based on filtering. In the case of mean estimation, the basic idea is to compute the maximum eigenvector of the empirical covariance of the data — if this eigenvector is too large, then we can find a collection of points that are responsible for it being large, and remove them as outliers. Though it is not phrased this way, it can be thought of–similarly to our approach–as solving a tainted optimization problem (top eigenvalue on the noisy data) and then cleaning up outliers afterwards. Their outlier removal step seems tighter than ours, and it would be interesting to find an approach that obtains such tight bounds for a general class of optimization problems.

Finally, Lai et al. (2016) pursue an approach based on iteratively finding the top $n/2$ eigenvectors (rather than just the top) and projecting out the remaining directions of variation, as well as removing outliers if the eigenvalues are too large. This seems similar in spirit to the filtering approach described above.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present our main results and some of their implications in specific settings. In Section 3 we explain our algorithm and provide some intuition for why it should work. In Section 4 we provide a proof outline for our main results. In Sections 5 and 6, we sharpen our results, first showing how to obtain concentration inequalities on the errors, and then showing how to obtain tighter bounds and stronger guarantees for strongly convex losses. In Section 7 we present lower bounds showing that our results are optimal in some settings. In Section 8 we present some intuition for our bounds, and in Section 9 we prove our main corollaries. The remaining sections are dedicated to deferred proofs.

## 2 Main Results and Implications

We consider a general setting of stochastic optimization with adversaries. We observe convex functions $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}$, where $\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a convex parameter space. For a subset $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}\subseteq[n]$ of size $\alpha n$, $f_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}p^{*}$ for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, and the remaining $f_{i}$ are chosen by an adversary whose strategy can depend on the $f_{i}$ for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

Let $\bar{f}$ denote the mean of $f$ under $p^{*}$, i.e. $\bar{f}(w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{f\sim p^{*}}[f(w)]$ for $w\in\mathcal{H}$; our goal is to find a parameter $\hat{w}$ such that $\bar{f}(\hat{w})-\bar{f}(w^{*})$ is small, where $w^{*}$ is the minimizer of $\bar{f}$. We use $r$ to denote the $\ell_{2}$-radius of $\mathcal{H}$, i.e. $r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\max_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\|w\|_{2}$.

This stochastic optimization setting captures most concrete settings of interest — for instance, mean estimation corresponds to $f_{i}(w)=\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, linear regression to $f_{i}(w)=(y_{i}-\langle w,x_{i}\rangle)^{2}$, and logistic regression to $f_{i}(w)=\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\langle w,x_{i}\rangle))$.

##### A key player: spectral norm of gradients.

To state our main results, we need to define the following key quantity, where $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ denotes the spectral or operator norm:

$$S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\max_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}}\left\|\left[\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)\right]_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \tag{1}$$

In words, if we form the matrix of gradients $\left[\nabla f_{i_{1}}(w)\ \cdots\ \nabla f_{i_{\alpha n}}(w)\right]$, where $\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\alpha n}\}=\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, then $S$ measures the difference between this matrix and its expectation in operator norm, maximized over all $w\in\mathcal{H}$. This will turn out to be a key quantity for understanding learnability in the adversarial setting. It acts as an analog of uniform convergence in classical learning theory, where one would instead study the quantity $\max_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\|\frac{1}{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}(\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w))\|_{2}$. Note that this latter quantity is always bounded above by $S$.

The fact that $f_{i}\sim p^{*}$ is irrelevant to our results—all that matters is the quantity $S$, which exists even for a deterministic set of functions $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$. Furthermore, $S$ only depends on the good data and is independent of the adversary.

##### Scaling of $S$: examples.

The definition (1) is a bit complex, so we go over some examples for intuition. We will see later that for the first two examples below (estimating means and product distributions), our implied error bounds are “good”, while for the final example (linear classification), our bounds are “bad”.

Mean estimation: Suppose that $f_{i}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, where $x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}I)$. Then $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)=x_{i}-\mu$, and so $S$ is simply the maximum singular value of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}}[x_{i}-\mu]_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}$. This is the square root of the maximum eigenvalue of $\frac{1}{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}(x_{i}-\mu)(x_{i}-\mu)^{\top}$, which converges to $\sigma$ for large $n$.

Product distributions: Suppose that $x_{i}$ is drawn from a product distribution on $\{0,1\}^{d}$, where the $j$th coordinate is $1$ with probability $p_{j}$. Let $f_{i}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{ij}\log(w_{j})+(1-x_{ij})\log(1-w_{j})$. In this case $\bar{f}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\log(w_{j})+(1-p_{j})\log(1-w_{j})$, and $w_{j}^{*}=p_{j}$, so that $\bar{f}(w)-\bar{f}(w^{*})$ is the KL divergence between $p$ and $w$.

The $j$th coordinate of $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)$ is $(x_{ij}-p_{j})(1/w_{j}+1/(1-w_{j}))$. In particular, the matrix in the definition of $S$ can be written as $D(w)\cdot[x_{i}-p]_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}/\sqrt{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}$, where $D(w)$ is a diagonal matrix with entries $1/w_{j}+1/(1-w_{j})$. Suppose that $p$ is balanced, meaning that $p_{j}\in[1/4,3/4]$, and that we restrict $w_{j}\in[1/4,3/4]$ as well. Then $\|D(w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 16/3$, while the matrix $[x_{i}-p]/\sqrt{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|}$ has maximum singular value converging to $\max_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}(1-p_{j})\leq\frac{1}{4}$ for large enough $n$. Thus $S=\mathcal{O}(1)$ in this setting.

Linear classification: Suppose that $x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and that $y_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(u^{\top}x_{i})$ for some unknown vector $u$. Our loss function is the logistic loss $f_{i}(w)=\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\langle w,x_{i}\rangle))$. In this case $\nabla f_{i}(w)=-\frac{y_{i}}{1+\exp(y_{i}\langle w,x_{i}\rangle)}x_{i}$. It is less obvious how to compute $S$, but Lemma 2.1 below implies that it is $\mathcal{O}(1)$.

Sub-gaussian gradients: A useful general bound on $S$ can be obtained assuming that the $f_{i}$ have sub-Gaussian gradients. Recall that a random variable $X$ is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian if $\mathbb{E}[\exp(u^{\top}(X-\mu))]\leq\exp(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2})$, where $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$. If $\nabla f_{i}$ is sub-Gaussian, then $S=\mathcal{O}(\sigma)$ if $\alpha n\geq\tilde{\Omega}(d)$:

**Lemma 2.1.**

If $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)$ is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian and $L$-Lipschitz for $f_{i}\sim p^{*}$, and $\alpha n$ is at least $d\max(1,\log(\tfrac{rL}{\sigma}))+\log(1/\delta)$, then $S=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$ with probability $1-\delta$.

In most of our concrete settings (such as mean estimation), sub-Gaussianity of $\nabla f_{i}$ corresponds to sub-Gaussianity of the data points $x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. In some settings we will be able to obtain bounds on $S$ under weaker (second-moment) conditions, as in Proposition 1.1.

### 2.1 Main Results

We can now state our main results. Our first result is that, just using the untrusted data, we can output a small ellipse which contains a parameter attaining small error under $\bar{f}$. This meta-result leads directly to our more specific results in the list decoding and semi-verified settings.

**Theorem 2.2.**

Given $n$ data points containing a set $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ of $\alpha n$ data points with spectral norm bound $S$, we can obtain an ellipse $\mathcal{E}_{Y}=\{w\mid ww^{\top}\preceq Y\}$ such that $\operatorname{tr}(Y)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha}\right)$ and

$$\min_{{w}\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}\bar{f}({w})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Sr}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right). \tag{2}$$

Recall here that $r$ is the $\ell_{2}$-radius of the parameter space $\mathcal{H}$. Also note that when $Y$ is invertible, $ww^{\top}\preceq Y$ is equivalent to $w^{\top}Y^{-1}w\leq 1$, so $Y$ really does define an ellipse. Theorem 2.2 shows that the unverified data is indeed helpful, by narrowing the space of possible parameters from all of $\mathcal{H}$ down to the small ellipse $\mathcal{E}_{Y}$.

To interpret the bound (2), consider the mean estimation example above, where $f_{i}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-w\|_{2}^{2}$ with $x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}I)$. Note that $\bar{f}(w)-\bar{f}(w^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\|w-\mu\|_{2}^{2}$. Assuming that $\|\mu\|_{2}$ is known to within a constant factor, we can take $\mathcal{H}$ to be the $\ell_{2}$-ball of radius $r=\|\mu\|_{2}$. This leads to the bound $\|w-\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\|\mu\|_{2}/\sqrt{\alpha}\right)$, for some $w$ in an ellipse of trace $\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\alpha}$. Note that the $\ell_{2}$-ball itself has trace $d\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}$, so the ellipse $\mathcal{E}_{Y}$ is much smaller than $\mathcal{H}$ if $d$ is large. Moreover, a random $x_{i}\sim p^{*}$ will have $\|x_{i}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}\approx d\sigma^{2}$, so $w$ is much closer to $\mu$ than a random draw from $p^{*}$, assuming that $\|\mu\|_{2}\ll d\sigma$.

By applying our algorithm multiple times we can improve the bound $\|w-\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\|\mu\|_{2}/\sqrt{\alpha}\right)$ to $\|w-\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma^{2}/\alpha\right)$, so that our bounds are meaningful for large $d$ independent of $\|\mu\|_{2}$. We discuss this in more detail in Section 6.

For an example where Theorem 2.2 is less meaningful, consider the linear classification example from above. In that case $S=\mathcal{O}(1)$, and $r$ is likely also $\mathcal{O}(1)$, so we obtain the bound $\bar{f}(w)-\bar{f}(w^{*})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\alpha})$. However, $\bar{f}(0)=\log(2)$ while $\bar{f}(w^{*})\geq 0$, so this bound is essentially vacuous.

##### List decodable learning.

Using Theorem 2.2 as a starting point we can derive bounds for both models defined in Section 1, starting with the list decodable model. Here, we must make the further assumption that the $f_{i}$ are $\kappa$-strongly convex, meaning that $f_{i}(w^{\prime})-f_{i}(w)\geq(w^{\prime}-w)^{\top}\nabla f_{i}(w)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|w^{\prime}-w\|_{2}^{2}$. The strong convexity allows us to show that for the good $f_{i}$, the parameters $\hat{w}_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}f_{i}(w)$ concentrate around $w^{*}$, with radius $r^{\prime}\ll r$. By clustering the $\hat{w}_{i}$ and iteratively re-running our algorithm on each cluster, we can obtain bounds that do not depend on $r$, and output a single candidate parameter $\hat{w}_{j}$ for each cluster. We can thereby show:

**Theorem 2.3.**

Suppose the functions $f_{i}$ are $\kappa$-strongly convex, and suppose there is a set $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ of size $\alpha n$ with spectral norm bound $S$. Then, for any $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}$, it is possible to obtain a set of $m\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}\rfloor$ candidate parameters $\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{m}$, such that $\min_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{w}_{j}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{S}{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{2}{\alpha})}{\alpha\varepsilon}}\right)$.

In Section 6 we state and prove a stronger version of this result. A key tool in establishing Theorem 2.3 is padded decompositions (Fakcharoenphol et al., 2003), which identify clusters in data while making minimal assumptions on the geometry of points outside of a cluster, and are thus useful in our adversarial setting.

##### Semi-verified learning.

If the $f_{i}$ are not strongly convex then we cannot employ the clustering ideas above. However, because we have reduced $\mathcal{H}$ to the much smaller set $\mathcal{E}_{Y}$, we can nevertheless often approximate $w^{*}$ with only a small amount of verified data. In fact, in some settings we only need a single verified data point:

**Lemma 2.4.**

Suppose that $f(w)=\phi(w^{\top}x)$, where $\phi$ is $1$-Lipschitz, and suppose that $x$ has bounded $q$th moments in the sense that $\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[|\langle x-\mathbb{E}[x],u\rangle|^{q}]^{1/q}\leq\sigma_{q}$ for all unit vectors $u$ and some $q\geq 2$. Then given $Y$ from Theorem 2.2 and a single verified $x\sim p^{*}$, we can obtain a $\hat{w}$ such that $\mathbb{P}_{x\sim p^{*}}\left[\bar{f}(\hat{w})\geq\bar{f}(w^{*})+C\cdot\frac{(S+t\sigma_{q})r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right]\leq t^{-q}$, for a universal constant $C$.

The particular functional form for $f_{i}$ was needed to obtain a concrete bound, but analogs of Lemma 2.4 should be possible in any setting where we can leverage the low complexity of $\mathcal{E}_{Y}$ into a bound on $f-\bar{f}$. Note that if we replace $\mathcal{E}_{Y}$ with $\mathcal{H}$ in Lemma 2.4, then the $\frac{r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}$ dependence becomes $r\sqrt{d}$, which is usually vacuous.

##### Optimality?

The dependence on $S$, $r$ and $\kappa$ in the results above seems essentially necessary, though the optimal dependence on $\alpha$ is less clear. In Section 7 we show lower bounds for robust mean estimation even if $p^{*}$ is known to be Gaussian. These bounds roughly translate to a lower bound of $\Omega\big({\frac{S}{\kappa}{\sqrt{\log{({1}/{\alpha})}}}}\big)$ for strongly convex $f_{i}$, and $\Omega\big({Sr\sqrt{\log(\smash{{1}/{\alpha}})}}\big)$ for linear $f_{i}$, and hold in both the list decodable and semi-verified settings. It is unclear whether the optimal dependence on $\alpha$ is $\sqrt{{1}/{\alpha}}$ or $\sqrt{\log({1}/{\alpha})}$ or somewhere between. We do note that any dependence better than $\sqrt{{1}/{\alpha}}$ would improve the best known results for efficiently solving $k$-means for well-separated clusters, which may suggest at least a computational barrier to achieving $\sqrt{\log({1}/{\alpha})}$.

### 2.2 Implications

We now go over some implications of our general results in some more specific settings. All of the results below follow as corollaries of our main theorems, and are proved in Section 9.

##### Robust mean estimation.

Suppose we observe points $x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, of which $\alpha n$ are drawn from a distribution $p^{*}$ with bounded covariance, and our goal is to recover the mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p^{*}}[x]$. If we take $f_{i}(w)=\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, then Theorem 2.3, together with the matrix concentration bound Proposition 1.1, implies the following result:

**Corollary 2.5.**

Suppose that $p^{*}$ has bounded covariance: $\operatorname{Cov}_{p^{*}}[x]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$. Then, for $n\geq\frac{d}{\alpha}$, with probability $1-\exp\left(-\Omega(\alpha n)\right)$ it is possible to output $m\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)$ candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$ such that $\min_{j=1}^{m}\|\mu-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\Big({\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}}\Big)$. Moreover, if $\alpha\geq 0.51$ then we can take $m=1$.

We can compare to the results of Lai et al. (2016) and Diakonikolas et al. (2016), who study mean estimation when $\alpha>\frac{1}{2}$ and one is required to output a single parameter (i.e., $m=1$). For simplicity take $\alpha=\frac{3}{4}$. Roughly, Lai et al. (2016) obtain error $\mathcal{O}({\sigma\sqrt{\log(d)}})$ with sample complexity $n=\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)$, while requiring a bound on the fourth moments of $p^{*}$; Diakonikolas et al. (2016) obtain error $\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$ with sample complexity $n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3}\right)$, and require $p^{*}$ to be Gaussian. Corollary 2.5 improves both of these by yielding error $\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$ with sample complexity $n=\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)$, and only requires $p^{*}$ to have bounded second moments.[^3] We note that in contrast to our results, these other results obtain error that vanishes as $\alpha\to 1$ (at a rate of $(1-\alpha)^{1/2}$ in the first case and $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1-\alpha)$ in the second case). We thus appear to incur some looseness when $\alpha\approx 1$, in exchange for obtaining results in the previously unexplored setting $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$. It would be interesting to obtain a single algorithm that both applies when $\alpha\ll 1$ and achieves vanishing error as $\alpha\to 1$.

##### Learning mixture of distributions.

In addition to robust mean estimation, we can use our results to efficiently learn mixtures of distributions, by thinking of a single mixture component as the good data and the remaining mixture components as bad data. Again applying Theorem 2.2 to $f_{i}(w)=\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, we obtain the following result, which says that we can successfully cluster samples from a mixture of distributions, even in the presence of arbitrary corruptions, provided the cluster means are separated in $\ell_{2}$ distance by $\tilde{\Omega}({{\sigma}/{\sqrt{\alpha}}})$.

**Corollary 2.6.**

Suppose we are given $n$ samples, where each sample either comes from one of $k$ distributions $p_{1}^{*},\ldots,p_{k}^{*}$ (with $\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{i}^{*}}[x]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$ for all $i$), or is arbitrary. Let $\mu_{i}$ be the mean of $p_{i}^{*}$, let $\alpha_{i}$ be the fraction of points from $p_{i}^{*}$, and let $\alpha=\min_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}$. Then if $n\geq\frac{d}{\alpha}$, with probability $1-k\exp(-\Omega(\alpha\varepsilon^{2}n))$ we can obtain a partition $T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}$ of $[n]$ and corresponding candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$ such that: for all but $\varepsilon\alpha_{i}n$ of the points drawn from $p_{i}^{*}$, the point is assigned a set $T_{j}$ and candidate mean $\hat{\mu}_{j}$ with $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{2}{\alpha})}{\alpha}}\right)$. Moreover, $m\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)$.

The $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$ dependence can be replaced with $\sqrt{\log(n)/\varepsilon}$, or with $\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}$ if the $x_{i}$ are sub-Gaussian. The only difference is in which matrix concentration bound we apply to the $x_{i}$. Corollary 2.6 says that we can partition the points into $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)$ sets, such that two points from well-separated clusters are unlikely to end up in the same set. Note however that one cluster might be partitioned into multiple sets. In the adversarial setting, this seems unavoidable, since an adversary could create a fake cluster very close to a real cluster, and one would be forced to either combine the clusters or risk splitting the real cluster in two.

For comparison, the best computationally efficient algorithm for clustering a mixture of $k$ distributions (with few/no corruptions) requires mean separation roughly $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}({\sigma\sqrt{k}})$ (Awasthi and Sheffet, 2012; Achlioptas and McSherry, 2005), which our result matches if $\alpha=\Omega({1}/{k})$.

##### Planted partitions.

We next consider implications of our results in a version of the planted partition model (McSherry, 2001). In this model we observe a random directed graph, represented as a matrix $A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$. For disjoint subsets $I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}$ of $[n]$, we generate edges as follows: (i) If $u,v\in I_{i}$, then $p(A_{uv}=1)=\frac{a}{n}$. (ii) If $u\in I_{i},v\not\in I_{i}$, then $p(A_{uv}=1)=\frac{b}{n}$. (iii) If $u\not\in\cup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}$, the edges emanating from $u$ can be arbitrary. In contrast to the typical planted partition model, we allow some number of corrupted vertices not belonging to any of the $I_{i}$. In general $a$ and $b$ could depend on the partition indices $i$, $j$, but we omit this for simplicity.

Note that the distribution over the row $A_{u}$ is the same for all $u\in I_{i}$. By taking this distribution to be the distribution $p^{*}$, Theorem 2.3 yields the following result:

**Corollary 2.7.**

For the planted partition model above, let $\alpha=\min_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}$. Then, with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$, we can obtain sets $T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\subseteq[n]$, with $m\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)$, such that for all $i\in[k]$, there is a $j\in[m]$ with $|I_{i}\triangle T_{j}|\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a\log(\frac{2}{\alpha})}{\alpha^{2}(a-b)^{2}}\right)n$, where $\triangle$ denotes symmetric difference.

This shows that we can approximately recover the planted partition, even in the presence of arbitrary corruptions, provided $\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{a}\gg\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{3}}$ (since the bound on $|I_{i}\triangle T_{j}|$ needs to be less than $\alpha n$ to be meaningful). In contrast, the best efficient methods (assuming no corruptions) roughly require $\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{a+(k-1)b}\gg k$ in the case of $k$ equal-sized communities (Abbe and Sandon, 2015a; b). In the simplifying setting where $b=\frac{1}{2}a$, our bounds require $a\gg k^{3}\log(k)$ while existing bounds require $a\gg k^{2}$. The case of unequal size communities is more complex, but roughly, our bounds require $a\gg\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{3}}$ in contrast to $a\gg\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}$.

##### Summary.

For robust mean estimation, we match the best existing error bounds of $\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$ when $\alpha=\frac{3}{4}$, under weaker assumptions. For learning mixtures distributions, we match the best bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sigma\sqrt{k})$ when $\alpha=\Omega(1/k)$. For recovering planted partitions, we require average degree $k^{3}\log(k)$, in contrast to the best known bound of $k^{2}$. It is pleasing that a single meta-algorithm is capable of matching or nearly matching the best rate in these settings, despite allowing for arbitrary corruptions. We can also achieve bounds for robust density estimation, presented in Section 9.

## 3 Algorithm

In this section we present our algorithm, which consists of an SDP coupled with an outlier removal step. At a high level, our algorithm works as follows: first, we give each function $f_{i}$ its own parameter vector $w_{i}$, and minimize $\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w_{i})$ subject to regularization which ensures the $w_{i}$ remain close to each other; formally, we bound the $w_{i}$ to lie within a small ellipse. The reason for doing this is that the different $w_{i}$ are now only coupled via this regularization, and so the influence of adversarial data on the good parameters can only come from its effect on the shape of the ellipse. We will show that whenever the adversaries affect the shape of the ellipse more than a small amount, they are necessarily outliers that can be identified and removed. In the remainder of this section, we elaborate on these two steps of regularization and outlier removal, and provide pseudocode.

Algorithm 1 Algorithm for fitting $p^{*}$

1:Input: $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$

2:Initialize $c\leftarrow[1;\cdots;1]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$

3:Set $\lambda\leftarrow\frac{\sqrt{8\alpha}nS}{r}$

4:while true do

5: Let $\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y}$ be the solution to

$$\displaystyle\underset{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n},Y}{\text{minimize}}\ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(Y)$$

$$\displaystyle w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,n. \tag{3}$$

6: if $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$ then $\triangleright$ Check for outliers

7: return $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$ $\triangleright$ Not many outliers, can return

8: else

9: $c\leftarrow\textsc{UpdateWeights}(c,\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y})$ $\triangleright$ Re-weight points to down-weight outliers

10: end if

11:end while

##### Per-function adaptivity.

If the functions $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$ were all drawn from $p^{*}$ (i.e., there are no adversaries), then a natural approach would be to let $\hat{w}$ be the minimizer of $\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w)$, which will approximately minimize $\bar{f}(w)$ by standard concentration results.

The problem with using this approach in the adversarial setting is that even a single adversarially chosen function $f_{i}$ could substantially affect the value of $\hat{w}$. To minimize this influence, we give each $f_{i}$ its own parameter $w_{i}$, and minimize $\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w_{i})$, subject to a regularizer which encourages the $w_{i}$ to be close together. The adversary now has no influence on the good $w_{i}$ except via the regularizer, so the key challenge is to find a regularizer which sufficiently controls statistical error while also bounding the influence of the adversary.

It turns out that the right choice of regularizer in this case is to constrain the $w_{i}$ to lie within an ellipse with small trace. Formally, the centerpiece of our algorithm is the following convex optimization problem:

$$\displaystyle\underset{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n},Y}{\text{minimize}}\ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(Y)$$

$$\displaystyle w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,n. \tag{4}$$

Here the coefficients $c_{i}$ are non-negative weights which will eventually be used to downweight outliers (for now it is fine to imagine that $c_{i}=1$). Note that $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y$ is equivalent to the semidefinite constraint $\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y&w_{i}\\ w_{i}^{\top}&1\end{array}\right]\succeq 0$. The problem (4) can be solved in polynomial time in $n$ and $d$ assuming oracle access to the gradients $\nabla f_{i}$.

Note that the semidefinite constraint $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y$ is equivalent to $w_{i}^{\top}Y^{-1}w_{i}\leq 1$, which says that $w_{i}$ lies within the ellipse centered at $0$ defined by $Y$. The regularizer is thus the trace of the minimum ellipse containing the $w_{i}$; penalizing this trace will tend to push the $w_{i}$ closer together, but is there any intuition behind its geometry? The following lemma shows that $\operatorname{tr}(Y)$ is related to the trace norm of $\left[w_{1}\ \cdots\ w_{n}\right]$:

**Lemma 3.1.**

For any points $w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, suppose that $Y\succeq w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}$ for all $i$. Then, letting $\|\cdot\|_{*}$ denote the trace norm (i.e., sum of singular values) and $W_{T}=\left[w_{i}\right]_{i\in T}$, we have $\operatorname{tr}(Y)\geq\frac{\|W_{T}\|_{*}^{2}}{|T|}$ for all sets $T\subseteq[n]$.

The appearance of the trace norm makes sense in light of the intuition that we should be clustering the functions $f_{i}$; indeed, trace norm regularization is a key ingredient in spectral algorithms for clustering (see e.g. Zha et al. (2001); Chen et al. (2014a; b)). Lemma 3.1 says that $\operatorname{tr}(Y)$ simultaneously bounds the trace norm on every subset $T$ of $[n]$, which ends up yielding better results than are obtained by simply penalizing the overall trace norm; we believe that this local trace norm regularization likely leads to better results even in non-adversarial spectral learning settings. The most important property of $\operatorname{tr}(Y)$ is that it admits a certain type of local Hölder’s inequality which we will explain in Section 5.

Algorithm 2 Algorithm for updating weights $c$ to downweight outliers.

1:procedure UpdateWeights($c$, $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$)

2: for $i=1,\ldots,n$ do

3: Let $\tilde{w}_{i}$ be the solution to

$$\displaystyle\underset{\tilde{w}_{i},a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}}{\text{minimize}}\ \ \displaystyle f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})$$

$$\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j},\quad 0\leq a_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha n},\quad\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}=1. \tag{5}$$

4: Let $z_{i}\leftarrow f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$

5: end for

6: $z_{\max}\leftarrow\max\{z_{i}\mid c_{i}\neq 0\}$

7: $c_{i}^{\prime}\leftarrow c_{i}\cdot\frac{z_{\max}-z_{i}}{z_{\max}}$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$

8: return $c^{\prime}$

9:end procedure

##### Removing outliers.

Solving (4) is not by itself sufficient to achieve robustness. The problem is that a single function $f_{i}$ could strongly push $w_{i}$ to a given value $w_{\mathrm{target}}$ (e.g. if $f_{i}(w)=10^{100}\|w-w_{\mathrm{target}}\|_{2}^{2}$) which allows the adversaries to arbitrarily expand the ellipse defined by $Y$. To combat this, we need some way of removing outlier functions $f_{i}$ from our dataset. We will do this in a soft way, by assigning a weight $c_{i}$ to each function $f_{i}$, and downweighting functions that seem likely to be outliers.

How can we tell that a function is an outlier? Intuitively, if a function $f_{i}$ is really drawn from $p^{*}$, then there should be many other functions $f_{j}$, $j\neq i$, that are “similar” to $f_{i}$. We can quantify this by considering whether there are a large number of $j\neq i$ for which the parameter $w_{j}$ for $f_{j}$ does a good job of minimizing $f_{i}$. Formally, given a solution $(\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{n})$ to (4), we compare $\hat{w}_{i}$ to $\tilde{w}_{i}$, which is defined as the solution to the following optimization:

$$\displaystyle\underset{\tilde{w}_{i},a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}}{\text{minimize}}\ \ \displaystyle f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})$$

$$\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j},\quad 0\leq a_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha n},\quad\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}=1. \tag{6}$$

The optimization (6) roughly asks for a parameter $\tilde{w}_{i}$ that minimizes $f_{i}$, subject to $\tilde{w}_{i}$ being the average of at least $\frac{\alpha n}{2}$ distinct parameters $\hat{w}_{j}$. Given the solution $\tilde{w}_{i}$ to (6), we then downweight the influence of the $i$th data point based on the value of $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$. In particular, we will multiply the weight $c_{i}$ by $1-\eta\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$ for some appropriate $\eta$. Hopefully, this will downweight any outliers by a large amount while only downweighting good points by a small amount (this hope is verified in Lemma 4.5 below).

Pseudocode for our algorithm is given in Algorithms 1 and 2.

## 4 Approach and Proof Outline

We now provide an outline of the proof of Theorem 2.2, by analyzing the output of Algorithm 1. The structure of our proof has analogies to classical uniform convergence arguments, so we will start by reviewing that case.

#### Warm-up: Uniform Convergence

In uniform convergence arguments, we assume that all of $f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$ are drawn from $p^{*}$, which brings us into the realm of classical learning theory. The analogue to the optimization (3) in Algorithm 1 is regularized empirical risk minimization:

$$\hat{w}=\underset{w\in\mathcal{H}}{\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w)+\lambda h(w), \tag{7}$$

where $h(w)$ is a non-negative regularizer. Uniform convergence arguments involve two parts:

1. Bound the optimization error: Use the definition of $\hat{w}$ to conclude that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\hat{w})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w^{*})+\lambda h(w^{*})$ (since $\hat{w}$ minimizes (7)). This step shows that $\hat{w}$ does almost as well as $w^{*}$ at minimizing the empirical risk $\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w)$.
2. Bound the statistical error: Show, via an appropriate concentration inequality, that $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w)$ is close to $\bar{f}(w)$ for all $w\in\mathcal{H}$. Therefore, $\hat{w}$ is nearly as good as $w^{*}$ in terms of the true risk $\bar{f}$.

We will see next that the proof of Theorem 2.2 contains steps similar to these, though bounding the statistical error in the presence of adversaries requires an additional step of removing outliers.

#### Proof Overview

We will establish a stronger version of Theorem 2.2, which exhibits an explicit $w\in\mathcal{E}_{\hat{Y}}$ with small error:

**Theorem 4.1.**

Let $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$ be the output of Algorithm 1, and let $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\hat{w}_{i}\right)/\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\right)$. Then, $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq 18\frac{Sr}{\sqrt{\alpha}}$. Furthermore, $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\hat{Y}}$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$.

To prove Theorem 4.1, recall that Algorithm 1 has at its core the following optimization problem:

$$\displaystyle\underset{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n},Y}{\text{minimize}}\ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(Y)$$

$$\displaystyle w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,n. \tag{8}$$

This optimization asks to minimize $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})$ while constraining the $w_{i}$ to lie within the ellipse defined by $Y$. As in the uniform convergence argument above, there are two sources of error that we need to bound: the optimization error $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)$, and the statistical error $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$. Note that the statistical error now measures two quantities: the distance from $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ to $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$, and from $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ to $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$.

Bounding the optimization error requires showing that the ellipse defined by $\hat{Y}$ is not too small (so that it contains $w^{*}$), while bounding the statistical error requires showing that the ellipse is not too large (so that we cannot overfit too much). The former turns out to be easy and is shown in Lemma 4.2. The latter is more involved and requires several steps. First, we show in Lemma 4.3 that the statistical error can be bounded in terms of $\operatorname{tr}(Y)$ and $S$, which verifies the intuition that bounding the statistical error reduces to bounding $Y$. Next, in Lemma 4.4, we show that the parameters $\tilde{w}_{i}$ found in Algorithm 2 are bounded by an ellipse $\tilde{Y}$ with small trace, and that $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})\approx f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. By the optimality of $(\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y})$ for (8), the only way that $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ can be much larger than $\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{Y})$ is therefore if $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\ll f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})$ for $i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. In this case, we can identify outliers $i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ by considering the value of $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$, and Lemma 4.5 verifies that we can use this to perform outlier removal. We expand on both the optimization error and statistical error bounds below.

##### Bounding optimization error on $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

Throughout the argument, we will make use of the optimality of $(\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y})$ for (8), which implies that

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(Y)\text{ for any feasible $(w_{1:n},Y)$.} \tag{9}$$

We wish to bound $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$, but the preceding bound involves all of $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$, not just the $f_{i}$ for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. However, because the $\hat{w}_{i}$ are free to vary independently, we can bound $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)$ in terms of the amount that $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ would need to increase before $w^{*}(w^{*})^{\top}\preceq\hat{Y}$. In particular, by taking $Y=\hat{Y}+(w^{*})(w^{*})^{\top}$ in (9), we can obtain the following bound on the optimization error:

**Lemma 4.2.**

The solution $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ to (8) satisfies

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)\leq\lambda\|w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{10}$$

##### Bounding the statistical error.

We next consider the statistical error. We cannot bound this error via standard uniform convergence techniques, because each $f_{i}$ has a different argument $\hat{w}_{i}$. However, it turns out that the operator norm bound $S$, together with a bound on $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$, yield concentration of the $f_{i}$ to $\bar{f}$. In particular, we have:

**Lemma 4.3.**

Let $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}}c_{i}\hat{w}_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}}$. Then the solution $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$ to (8) satisfies

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \displaystyle\leq\alpha nS\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right),\text{ and } \tag{11}$$

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\right) \displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)+2\alpha nrS. \tag{12}$$

Lemma 4.3 relates $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ to $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ in (11), and then relates $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ to $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ in (12). Together these allow us to bound the statistical error in terms of $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ and $S$. The proof is an application of the matrix Hölder’s inequality $|\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}B)|\leq\|A\|_{*}\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}$, with $A_{i}=\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$ and $B_{i}=\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\nabla\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$.

##### Bounding the trace.

We next need to bound $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ itself. We again exploit the optimality constraint (9), which implies that $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\operatorname{tr}(Y)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(w_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\right)$ for any feasible $(w_{1:n},Y)$. We will take $w_{1:n}$ to be $\tilde{w}_{1:n}$ as defined in equation (5) of Algorithm 2; we then take $Y$ to be $\frac{2}{\alpha n}\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top}$, where $W=\left[w_{1}\ \cdots\ w_{n}\right]$. Lemma 4.4 asserts that $(w_{1:n},Y)$ is feasible, and uses this to “almost” bound $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$:

**Lemma 4.4.**

For $\tilde{w}_{i}$ as defined in (6), and $\tilde{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\alpha n}\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top}$, we have $\tilde{w}_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{\top}\preceq\tilde{Y}$ for all $i$, and also $\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{Y})\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}$. In addition,

$$\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\right), \tag{13}$$

and if $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}$ then

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\leq\alpha n\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right). \tag{14}$$

This “almost” bounds $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ in the following sense: if instead of $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$, (14) gave a bound on $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$, then we could plug in to (13) to obtain (e.g.) $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{n}{\lambda}\Big({\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r}\Big)$, after which solving the quadratic for $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$ would yield a bound. The problem is that $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})$, for $i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, could be arbitrarily large, so additional work is needed.

##### Outlier removal.

This brings us to our final idea of outlier removal. The intuition is the following: let $z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})}$. Then either: (i) the average of $z_{i}$ over all of $[n]$ is not much larger than over $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ (in which case the bound (14) extends from $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ to $[n]$), or (ii) the average of $z_{i}$ is much larger on $[n]$ than on $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, in which case it should be possible to downweight the points in $[n]\backslash\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ a substantial amount relative to the points in $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. This is the role that the outlier removal step (Algorithm 2) plays, and Lemma 4.5 formalizes its effect on the weights $c_{i}$.

**Lemma 4.5.**

Suppose that $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\geq\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$. Then, the update step in Algorithm 2 satisfies

$$\frac{1}{\alpha n}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}. \tag{15}$$

Moreover, the above supposition holds if $\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{8\alpha}nS}{r}$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})>\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$.

Lemma 4.5 says that, if the average value of $z_{i}$ is at least twice as large over $[n]$ as over $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, then the weights $c_{i}$ decrease at most half as quickly on $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ as on $[n]$. Moreover, this holds whenever $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})>\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$.

##### Combining the results.

Lemma 4.5 ensures that eventually we have $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha}\right)$, which allows us to bound the overall statistical error (using Lemma 4.3) by $\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}nrS\right)$. In addition, since $\lambda=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}nS/r\right)$, the optimization error is bounded (via Lemma 4.2) by $\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}nrS\right)$, as well. Combining the various bounds, we end up obtaining

$$\Big(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\Big)\left(\bar{f}\left(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\right)-\bar{f}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}nrS\right). \tag{16}$$

Then, since (15) ensures that the $c_{i}$ decrease twice as quickly over $[n]$ as over $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, we decrease $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$ by at most a factor of $2$ over all iterations of the algorithm, so that $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}$. Dividing (16) through by $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$ then yields Theorem 4.1.

In the remainder of this section, we will prove Lemmas 4.2 through 4.5, then show more formally how Theorem 4.1 follows from these lemmas.

#### Proof of Lemma 4.2

We want to bound $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$. As noted above, the optimality of $(\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y})$ implies that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(w_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(Y)$ for any feasible point $w_{1:n},Y$. We will use the particular choice of

$$w_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}w^{*}&:&i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}\\ \hat{w}_{i}&:&i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}\end{array}\right.,\quad Y=\hat{Y}+w^{*}(w^{*})^{\top}. \tag{17}$$

It is easy to see that this is a feasible point for the optimization: if $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, then $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}=w^{*}(w^{*})^{\top}\preceq\hat{Y}+w^{*}(w^{*})^{\top}$, and if $i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ then $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}=\hat{w}_{i}\hat{w}_{i}^{\top}\preceq\hat{Y}\preceq\hat{Y}+w^{*}(w^{*})^{\top}$. Therefore, we have

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(w^{*})+\sum_{i\not\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})+\lambda\left(\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})+\|w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right). \tag{18}$$

Re-arranging yields $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}f_{i}(w^{*})+\lambda\|w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}$, as was to be shown.

#### Proof of Lemma 4.3

First, by using Hölder’s inequality $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}B)\leq\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|B\|_{*}$, we can show the following:

**Lemma 4.6.**

For any $w_{0}$ and any $w_{1:n}$ satisfying $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y$ for all $i$, we have

$$\Big|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0}),w_{i}\rangle\Big|\leq\alpha n\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}S. \tag{19}$$

To establish (11) from Lemma 4.6, note that

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle \tag{20}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\nabla\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle \tag{21}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}\alpha n\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r^{2}\right)S, \tag{22}$$

where (i) is by convexity of $f_{i}$, (ii) is because

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle \displaystyle=\langle\nabla\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}),\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-\hat{w}_{i}\right)\rangle$$

$$\displaystyle=0,$$

and (iii) is two applications of Lemma 4.6, with $(w_{i},Y)=(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}},\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{\top})$ and $(w_{i},Y)=(\hat{w}_{i},\hat{Y})$.

To establish (12), we expand $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})$ as an integral; letting $w(t)=t\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}+(1-t)w^{*}$, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\right) \displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla\bar{f}(w(t)),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\rangle dt \tag{23}$$

$$\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w(t)),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\rangle dt \tag{24}$$

$$\displaystyle\phantom{++}+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla\bar{f}(w(t))-\nabla f_{i}(w(t)),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\rangle dt$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iv)}}{{\leq}}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w(t)),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{1}2\alpha nrSdt \tag{25}$$

$$\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)\right)+2\alpha nrS, \tag{26}$$

as claimed. Here (iv) invokes Lemma 4.6 at $w_{0}=w(t)$, $w_{i}=\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}$, $Y=(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*})(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*})^{\top}$.

#### Proof of Lemma 4.4

Let $\tilde{w}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j}$ as in (5). We have

$$\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{\top} \displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j}\right)^{\top} \tag{27}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\preceq}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\hat{w}_{j}\hat{w}_{j}^{\top} \tag{28}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\preceq}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{2}{\alpha n}\hat{w}_{j}\hat{w}_{j}^{\top} \tag{29}$$

$$\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\alpha n}\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top}=\tilde{Y}. \tag{30}$$

Here (ii) is because $a_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha n}$, while (i) is an instantiation of the inequality $\mathbb{E}[x]\mathbb{E}[x]^{\top}\preceq\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]$ (using the fact that $\sum_{j}a_{ij}=1$). This completes the first claim.

Next, $\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{Y})=\frac{2}{\alpha n}\operatorname{tr}(\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top})=\frac{2}{\alpha n}\|\hat{W}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha n}\cdot nr^{2}=\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}$. This completes the second claim. The inequality (13) then follows from (9), by optimality of $(\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y})$ and feasibility of $(\tilde{w}_{1:n},\tilde{Y})$.

Finally, by taking $a_{ij}=\frac{c_{j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{j^{\prime}}}$, we see that $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\sum_{j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{j^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{w}_{j}$ is a feasible point for $\tilde{w}_{i}$ (note that $a_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha n}$, since $c_{j}\leq 1$ and $\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{j^{\prime}}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}$ by assumption). We therefore have $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})\leq f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ for all $i$, and in particular for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. Therefore, $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$, which is bounded by $\alpha nS\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)$ by Lemma 4.3. This establishes (14) and completes the lemma.

#### Proof of Lemma 4.5

First, note that $\tilde{w}_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}\preceq\hat{Y}$, since it is a convex combination of the $\hat{w}_{j}$, and so replacing $\hat{w}_{i}$ with $\tilde{w}_{i}$ yields a feasible point for the optimization. Therefore, $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})\geq f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$, and so $z_{i}\geq 0$.

Now, remember that $c_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}\cdot\frac{z_{\max}-z_{i}}{z_{\max}}$. Then for any $S\subseteq[n]$, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime} \displaystyle=\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\cdot\frac{z_{i}}{z_{\max}} \tag{31}$$

$$\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{\max}}\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}z_{i}. \tag{32}$$

Taking $S=\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ and $S=[n]$, it follows that

$$\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}} \displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}} \tag{33}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\alpha}{2}, \tag{34}$$

where (i) is by the supposition $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}\geq\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$. This establishes (15).

Next, suppose that $\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{8\alpha}nS}{r}$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})>\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$. In this case we must show that the supposition $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}\geq\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$ necessarily holds. Note that

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i} \displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \tag{35}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \tag{36}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\alpha nS\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right) \tag{37}$$

by Lemma 4.3 and (11). Now let $x=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$, and assume for the sake of contradiction that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}<\frac{2}{\alpha}x$. Then by (13) in Lemma 4.4 we have

$$\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y}) \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:trace-tilde-bound}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}\right) \tag{38}$$

$$\displaystyle<\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}x\right) \tag{39}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:x-bound}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{2nS}{\lambda}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right). \tag{40}$$

Solving the quadratic for $\hat{Y}$, we obtain (see Lemma C.1) $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{4r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{4rnS}{\lambda}+\frac{4n^{2}S^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}$. Using the value $\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{8\alpha}nS}{r}$, we have $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$, which is a ruled out by assumption and is thus a contradiction. This establishes the claim that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}\geq\frac{2}{\alpha}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$ and completes the lemma.

#### Proof of Theorem 4.1

First, we note that $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}$; this is because the invariant $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}$ holds throughout the algorithm by Lemma 4.5. In particular, Algorithm 1 does eventually return, since Algorithm 2 zeros out at least one additional $c_{i}$ each time, and this can happen at most $n-\frac{\alpha n}{2}$ times before $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$ would drop below $\frac{\alpha n}{2}$ (which we established is impossible).

Now, let ($\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$) be the return value of Algorithm 1. By Lemma 4.2, we have $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)\leq\lambda r^{2}$. Also, by assumption we have $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{6r^{2}}{\alpha}$. By Lemma 4.3 we then have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\right) \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:statistical-error-1b}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)+2\alpha nrS \tag{41}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:statistical-error-1a}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)+3\alpha nrS+\sqrt{6\alpha}nrS \tag{42}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:optimization-error-1}}}{{\leq}}\lambda r^{2}+3\alpha nrS+\sqrt{6\alpha}nrS \tag{43}$$

$$\displaystyle=3\alpha nrS+(\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{8})\sqrt{\alpha}nrS \tag{44}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 9\sqrt{\alpha}nrS. \tag{45}$$

Since $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}$, dividing through by $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$ yields $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq 18\frac{Sr}{\sqrt{\alpha}}$, as was to be shown.

## 5 Concentration of Errors: A Local Hölder’s Inequality

Most of the bounds in Section 4 are bounds on an average error: for instance, Lemma 4.2 bounds the average difference between $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ and $f_{i}(w^{*})$, and Lemma 4.3 bounds the average difference between $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ and $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$. One might hope for a stronger bound, showing that the above quantities are close together for almost all $i$, rather than only close in expectation. This is relevant, for instance, in a clustering setting, where we would like to say that almost all points are assigned a parameter $\hat{w}_{i}$ that is close to the true cluster center. Even beyond this relevance, asking whether we obtain concentration of errors in this adversarial setting seems like a conceptually natural question:

> If the good data is sub-Gaussian, then can we obtain sub-Gaussian concentration of the errors, or can the adversaries force the error to only be small in expectation? What properties of the good data affect concentration of errors in the presence of an adversary?

In this section, we will show that we can indeed obtain sub-Gaussian concentration, at least for the statistical error. In particular, we will characterize the concentration behavior of the errors $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ using a local Hölder’s inequality, which depends upon a refined notion of $S$ that we denote by $S_{\varepsilon}$. Before defining $S_{\varepsilon}$, we will state the local Hölder’s inequality:

**Lemma 5.1.**

Suppose that the weights $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfy $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, and that the parameters $w_{i}$ satisfy $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y$. Then, for any $w_{0}\in\mathcal{H}$, we have

$$\left|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle w_{i},\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})\rangle\right|\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}S_{\varepsilon}. \tag{46}$$

We call this a local Hölder’s inequality because it is a sharpening of Lemma 4.6, which established via Hölder’s inequality that

$$\left|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle w_{i},\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})\rangle\right|\leq\alpha n\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}S. \tag{47}$$

By taking $b_{i}=\mathbb{I}[\langle w_{i},\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})\rangle>\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}S_{\varepsilon}]$, Lemma 5.1 implies in particular that $\langle w_{i},\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})\rangle\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}S_{\varepsilon}$ for all but $\varepsilon\alpha n$ values of $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

##### A local spectral norm bound.

We now define $S_{\varepsilon}$. The quantity $S_{\varepsilon}$ is essentially the maximum value of $S$ over any subset of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ of size at least $\varepsilon|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|$:

$$S_{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\max_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{T\subseteq\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}},|T|\geq\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\left\|\left[\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \tag{48}$$

(As a special case note that $S_{1}=S$.) The quantity $S_{\varepsilon}$ bounds not just the operator norm of all of the points in $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, but also the operator norm on any large subset of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. We will see later that it is often possible to obtain good bounds on $S_{\varepsilon}$.

##### Concentration of statistical error.

Using $S_{\varepsilon}$, we can obtain an improved version of the bounds (11) and (12) from Lemma 4.3, showing that $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ is close to a nominal value $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})$ for almost all $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$:

**Lemma 5.2.**

Let the weights $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfy $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, and define $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}}b_{i}\hat{w}_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}}$. Then the solution $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$ to (8) satisfies

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right). \tag{49}$$

Moreover, for any $w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}$, we have

$$\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(\bar{f}(w)-\bar{f}(w^{\prime})\right)-\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(w)-f_{i}(w^{\prime})\right)\right| \displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)rS_{\varepsilon}. \tag{50}$$

Relative to Lemma 4.3, the main differences are: The bounds now hold for any weights $b_{i}$ (with $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$ replaced by $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}$), and both (49) and (50) have been strengthened in some minor ways relative to (11) and (12) — in (49) we are now bounding the linearization $\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle$, and (50) holds at all $w,w^{\prime}$ instead of just $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}},w^{*}$. These latter strengthenings are trivial and also hold in Lemma 4.3, but were omitted earlier for simplicity. The important difference is that the inequalities hold for any $b_{i}$, rather than just for the original weights $c_{i}$.

It is perhaps unsatisfying that (49) holds relative to $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}$, rather than $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$. Fortunately, by exploiting the fact that $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$ is nearly optimal for $\bar{f}$, we can replace $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}$ with $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$ at the cost of a slightly weaker bound:

**Corollary 5.3.**

Let the weights $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfy $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, and suppose that $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha n$. Then the solution $\hat{w}_{1:n},\hat{Y}$ to (8) satisfies

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\left(S_{\varepsilon}\left(3\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+9r\right)+\frac{2\lambda r^{2}}{\alpha n}\right). \tag{51}$$

In particular, if $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha}\right)$ and $\lambda=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}nS_{\varepsilon}/r\right)$, then $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\leq\mathcal{O}\left({S_{\varepsilon}r}/{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$ for all but $\varepsilon\alpha n$ values of $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

Corollary 5.3 shows that no matter what the adversary does, the function errors $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ will be relatively tightly concentrated (at least assuming $S_{\varepsilon}$ is small; we will address the typical size of $S_{\varepsilon}$ later). Looking ahead, we will also be able to show that, in the case that the $f_{i}$ are strongly convex, $\|w_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}$ is also small for almost all $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. We give this result as Lemma 6.3 in Section 6.

##### Preserving inliers.

Our outlier removal step can be modified based on $S_{\varepsilon}$ so that almost none of the good points are removed. This is not strictly necessary for any of our later results, but is an intuitively appealing property for our algorithm to have. That we can preserve the good points is unsurprising in light of Corollary 5.3, which says that the good points concentrate, and hence should be cleanly separable from any outliers. The modified outlier removal step is given as Algorithm 3.

Algorithm 3 Algorithm for updating weights $c$ to downweight outliers.

1:procedure UpdateWeights($c$, $\hat{w}_{1:n}$, $\hat{Y}$)

2: $\tau\leftarrow S_{\varepsilon}\left(3\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+9r\right)$

3: for $i=1,\ldots,n$ do

4: Let $\tilde{w}_{i}$ be the solution to (5) as in Algorithm 2.

5: Let $z_{i}\leftarrow\max\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau,0\right)$

6: end for

7: $z_{\max}\leftarrow\max\{z_{i}\mid c_{i}\neq 0\}$

8: $c_{i}^{\prime}\leftarrow c_{i}\cdot\frac{z_{\max}-z_{i}}{z_{\max}}$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$

9: return $c^{\prime}$

10:end procedure

Algorithm 3 is almost identical to Algorithm 2. The only difference from Algorithm 2 is that, instead of setting $z_{i}$ to $f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$, we set $z_{i}$ to $\max\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau,0\right)$ for an appropriately chosen $\tau$. This creates a buffer such that we do not start to downweight points until their function error passes the threshold $\tau$, which helps to make sure that very little mass is removed from the good points (because we do not start to take away mass until we are fairly sure that a point is bad). Formally, we have the following result for Algorithm 3, which is analogous to Lemma 4.5 for Algorithm 2:

**Lemma 5.4.**

Suppose that $\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{8\alpha}nS}{r}$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})>\frac{35r^{2}}{\alpha}$. Then, the update step in Algorithm 3 satisfies

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}. \tag{52}$$

This shows that the rate at which mass is removed from $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ is at most $\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$ the rate at which mass is removed overall.

##### Interpreting $S_{\varepsilon}$.

We end this section by giving some intuition for the typical scale of $S_{\varepsilon}$. Recall that Lemma 2.1 shows that, when the gradients of $f_{i}$ are sub-Gaussian with parameter $\sigma$, then $S\leq\mathcal{O}({\sigma})$ assuming $n\gg d/\alpha$. A similar bound holds for $S_{\varepsilon}$, with an additional factor of $\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}$:

**Lemma 5.5.**

If $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)$ is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian and $L$-Lipschitz, then with probability $1-\delta$ we have

$$S_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\left(\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}+\sqrt{\frac{d\max\left(1,\log\left(\frac{rL}{\sigma}\right)\right)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon\alpha n}}\right)\right). \tag{53}$$

In particular, if $n\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon\alpha}\left(d\max\left(1,\log(\tfrac{rL}{\sigma})\right)+\log(1/\delta)\right)$, then $S_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}\right)$ with probability $1-\delta$, where $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$ masks only absolute constants.

Lemma 5.5 together with Corollary 5.3 show that, if the gradients of $f_{i}$ are sub-Gaussian, then the errors between $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})$ and $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ are also sub-Gaussian, in the sense that the fraction of $i$ for which $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\geq\Omega(\sigma\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)/\alpha})$ is at most $\varepsilon$. Inverting this, for sufficiently large $t$ the fraction of $i$ for which $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\geq t\sigma/\sqrt{\alpha}$ is at most $\exp(-\Omega(t^{2}))$. In other words, no matter what the adversary does, it cannot prevent the function errors from concentrating in a sub-Gaussian manner, provided the good data itself is sub-Gaussian.

##### A general Chebyshev bound.

What happens if the function errors are not sub-Gaussian, but we still have a bound on $S=S_{1}$? We can then bound $S_{\varepsilon}$ in terms of $S$ by exploiting the monotonicity of the operator norm.

**Lemma 5.6.**

For any $\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{2}$, $S_{\varepsilon_{1}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon_{2}\alpha n\rfloor}{\lfloor\varepsilon_{1}\alpha n\rfloor}}S_{\varepsilon_{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}}S_{\varepsilon_{2}}$.

When coupled with Corollary 5.3, this shows that the function errors concentrate in a Chebyshev-like manner: The fraction of $i$ for which $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})$ exceeds $\Omega\left(\sigma/\sqrt{\alpha\varepsilon}\right)$ is at most $\varepsilon$, and so the fraction of $i$ for which $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\geq t\sigma/\sqrt{\alpha}$ is $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)$. Note that this is already a strengthening of the naïve bound from Markov’s inequality, which would only say that the fraction is $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)$. The local Hölder’s inequality in Lemma 5.1 thus leads to a tighter analysis even without any further bounding of $S_{\varepsilon}$.

#### Proof of Lemma 5.1

Throughout this section we will for convenience use $\Delta_{i}$ to denote $\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})$. We start with a helper lemma that translates information about $S_{\varepsilon}$ into a more useful form:

**Lemma 5.7.**

For any $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ with $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor$, and any matrix $Y\succeq 0$, we have

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\Delta_{i}^{\top}Y\Delta_{i}\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\operatorname{tr}(Y)S_{\varepsilon}^{2}. \tag{54}$$

**Proof.**

Since the inequality is linear in $b_{i}$, it is enough to check it at the extreme values of $b_{i}$. These consist of $b_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ such that $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}=\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor$, which exactly correspond to subsets $S$ of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ of size $\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor$.[^4] It thus suffices to check (54) at these points, which amounts to showing that

$$\sum_{i\in T}\Delta_{i}^{\top}Y\Delta_{i}\leq|T|\operatorname{tr}(Y)S_{\varepsilon}^{2}\text{\ \ for all sets $T$ of size $\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor$.} \tag{55}$$

Letting $D_{T}=[\Delta_{i}]_{i\in T}$, we show this as follows:

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in T}\Delta_{i}^{\top}Y\Delta_{i} \displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(Y\sum_{i\in T}\Delta_{i}\Delta_{i}^{\top}\right) \tag{56}$$

$$\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(YD_{T}D_{T}^{\top}\right) \tag{57}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(Y\right)\|D_{T}D_{T}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \tag{58}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(Y\right)\|D_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2} \tag{59}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\operatorname{tr}(Y)|T|S_{\varepsilon}^{2}, \tag{60}$$

as was to be shown. Here (i) invokes the definition of $S_{\varepsilon}$, which implies that $S_{\varepsilon}\geq\max_{|T|=\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor}\frac{\|D_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{\sqrt{|T|}}$. ∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma 5.1.

**Proof of Lemma 5.1.**

We have

$$\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle w_{i},\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})\rangle\right| \displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle w_{i},\Delta_{i}\rangle\right| \tag{61}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle w_{i},\Delta_{i}\rangle^{2}} \tag{62}$$

$$\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\Delta_{i}^{\top}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\Delta_{i}} \tag{63}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\Delta_{i}^{\top}Y\Delta_{i}} \tag{64}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:local-op-bound}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)^{2}\operatorname{tr}(Y)S_{\varepsilon}^{2}} \tag{65}$$

$$\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)S_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}, \tag{66}$$

as was to be shown. Here (i) is Cauchy-Schwarz and (ii) uses the condition $w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\preceq Y$. ∎

#### Proof of Lemma 5.2

First, note that $\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\leq\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle$ by convexity of $f_{i}$, so only the second inequality in (49) is interesting. Similarly to how we established (11) in Lemma 4.3, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle \displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})-\nabla\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle \tag{67}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:local-holder}}}{{\leq}}\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right). \tag{68}$$

In the final step we invoke (46) twice, identically to Lemma 4.3.

For (50), we follow an identical argument to (12) from Lemma 4.3. Defining $w(t)=tw+(1-t)w^{\prime}$, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(\bar{f}(w)-\bar{f}(w^{\prime})\right) \displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla\bar{f}(w_{t}),w-w^{\prime}\rangle dt \tag{69}$$

$$\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w_{t}),w-w^{\prime}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla\bar{f}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{i}(w_{t}),w-w^{\prime}\rangle dt \tag{70}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:local-holder}}}{{\leq}}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w_{t}),w-w^{\prime}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{1}2\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)rS_{\varepsilon}dt \tag{71}$$

$$\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(w)-f_{i}(w^{\prime})\right)\right)+2\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)rS_{\varepsilon}. \tag{72}$$

This yields (50) and completes the lemma.

#### Proof of Corollary 5.3

We need to bound $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})\right)$. By (50) in Lemma 5.2, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})\right) \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:statistical-error-2b}}}{{\leq}}\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})+2S_{\varepsilon}r\right) \tag{73}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})+2S_{\varepsilon}r\right). \tag{74}$$

On the other hand, by Lemmas 4.3 and 4.2, we also have

$$\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\right)\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\right) \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:statistical-error-1b}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)+2\alpha nSr \tag{75}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:statistical-error-1a}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-f_{i}(w^{*})\right)+\alpha nS\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+3r\right) \tag{76}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:optimization-error-1}}}{{\leq}}\lambda r^{2}+\alpha nS\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+3r\right). \tag{77}$$

Dividing through by $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$ and using the condition $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha n$, we obtain $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq\frac{2\lambda r^{2}}{\alpha n}+2S\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+3r\right)$. Using $S\leq S_{\varepsilon}$ and substituting into (74) yields

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})\right)\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)\left(S_{\varepsilon}\Big({2\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+8r}\Big)+\frac{2\lambda r^{2}}{\alpha n}\right).$$

Combining this with the bound on $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)$ from (49) then gives the desired inequality (51). The rest of Corollary 5.3 follows by setting $b_{i}=\mathbb{I}[f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})>C\cdot S_{\varepsilon}r/\sqrt{\alpha}]$ for an appropriate absolute constant $C$.

#### Proof of Lemma 5.4

Recall that $z_{i}=\max\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau,0\right)$. Our goal is to show that $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}$. If we can show this, then the result follows identically to the first part of Lemma 4.5.

To start, we have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i} \displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}\left(c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\right)\cdot\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau\right) \tag{78}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}\left(c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\right)\cdot\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau\right) \tag{79}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\max\left(\varepsilon\alpha n,\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\right)\tau-\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\tau \tag{80}$$

$$\displaystyle=\max\left(\varepsilon\alpha n-\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0],0\right)\tau \tag{81}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\alpha n\tau. \tag{82}$$

Here (i) is because $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}(c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0])\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\leq\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\right)\tau$ by Lemma 5.2 if $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, and if the sum is less than $\varepsilon\alpha n$, we can always arbitrarily increase the weights beyond $c_{i}\mathbb{I}[z_{i}>0]$ until their sum is large enough (since $f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\geq 0$, this will only make the resulting inequality stronger).

Now let $x=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$, and assume for the sake of contradiction that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}<\frac{2}{\varepsilon\alpha}x$. By Lemma 4.4 we have

$$\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y}) \displaystyle\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\right) \tag{83}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(\tau+\max\left(f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})-\tau,0\right)\right)\right) \tag{84}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(n\tau+\frac{2}{\varepsilon\alpha}x\right) \tag{85}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{3n\tau}{\lambda}=\frac{2r^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{3r}{\sqrt{8\alpha}}\left(3\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+9r\right) \tag{86}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\frac{12r^{2}}{\alpha}+\sqrt{\frac{11r^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}{\alpha}}. \tag{87}$$

By Lemma C.1 applied to $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$, we have $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\frac{35r^{2}}{\alpha}$. This contradicts the assumption of the lemma, so we indeed have $\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}z_{i}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{2}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}z_{i}$, from which the desired result follows.

#### Proof of Lemma 5.6

Let $M$ be any matrix, and $T_{1}\subseteq T_{2}$ be subsets of the rows of $M$. Then it is easy to see that $\|M_{T_{1}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|M_{T_{2}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. Using this, it is then clear from the definition (48) that $S_{\varepsilon_{1}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon_{2}\alpha n\rfloor}{\lfloor\varepsilon_{1}\alpha n\rfloor}}S_{\varepsilon_{2}}$, since any subset $T$ appearing in the definition of $S_{\varepsilon_{1}}$ can be extended to a set $T^{\prime}$ appearing in the definition of $S_{\varepsilon_{2}}$, whose size is at most $\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon_{2}\alpha n\rfloor}{\lfloor\varepsilon_{1}\alpha n\rfloor}$ times bigger.

## 6 Bounds for Strongly Convex Losses

We now turn our attention to the special case that the functions $f_{i}$ are strongly convex in $w$, in the sense that for all $w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}$,

$$f_{i}(w^{\prime})\geq\langle w^{\prime}-w,\nabla f_{i}(w)\rangle+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|w^{\prime}-w\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{88}$$

In this case, we will obtain stronger bounds by iteratively clustering the output $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ of Algorithm 1 and re-running the algorithm on each cluster. The main theorem in this section is a recovery result in the list decoding model, for an algorithm (Algorithm 4) that formalizes this clustering intuition:

**Theorem 6.1.**

Suppose that $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}$ and let $\mathcal{U}$, $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ be the output of Algorithm 4. Then $\mathcal{U}$ has size at most $\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}\rfloor$, and $\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\|u-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log(2/\alpha)}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$. Moreover, $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log(2/\alpha)}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$ for all but $\varepsilon\alpha n$ values of $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

##### Algorithm and proof overview.

Algorithm 4 works at a high level as follows: first, run Algorithm 1 to obtain $\hat{w}_{i}$ that are (as we will show in Proposition 6.2) relatively close to $w^{*}$ for most $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. Supposing that the good $\hat{w}_{i}$ are within distance $r^{\prime}\ll r$ of $w^{*}$, we can cluster $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ into balls of radius $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(r^{\prime}\right)$, and re-run Algorithm 1 on each cluster; Theorem 4.1 will now yield bounds in terms of $r^{\prime}$ instead of $r$. By repeating this enough times, we can shrink our hypothesis space to a small ball around $w^{*}$, thus obtaining substantially better bounds. A key piece of machinery which will allow us to obtain a satisfactory clustering is the notion of a padded decomposition, originally due to Fakcharoenphol et al. (2003), which we explain in more detail later in this section.

Pseudocode for Algorithm 4 is provided above: We keep track of an upper bound $r^{(t)}$ on the distance from the $\hat{w}_{i}$ to $w^{*}$, which is initially $r$ and decreases by a factor of $2$ each time. If this radius drops below a threshold, then we perform a final greedy clustering and exit. Otherwise we use padded decompositions to cluster the points, and run Algorithm 4 on each cluster to obtain new assignments for each $\hat{w}_{i}$ (since the padded decomposition is randomized, we repeat this several times to ensure correctness with high probability). We can show (Lemma 6.5) that these new assignments $\hat{w}_{i}$ will be within distance $\frac{1}{2}r^{(t)}$ to $w^{*}$ for almost all $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, which is the key to proving correctness of the algorithm.

The rest of this section consists of three parts: First, we will show that if the $f_{i}$ are strongly convex, and $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ is the output of Algorithm 1, then $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}$ is small for most $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ (this requires some work, since applying Theorem 4.1 directly would only imply that $\|\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\|_{2}$ is small). Next, we will introduce the notion of a padded decomposition, and show (following ideas in Fakcharoenphol et al. (2003)) that padded decompositions of small diameter exist in our setting. Finally, we will combine these two results to analyze Algorithm 4 and establish Theorem 6.1.

##### Establishing concentration of $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}$.

We will first show that $\hat{w}_{i}$ is close to $w^{*}$ for almost all $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. This is captured in Proposition 6.2:

**Proposition 6.2.**

For some absolute constant $C$ and for any $\omega\geq 1$, the output $\hat{w}_{1:n}$ of Algorithm 1 satisfies $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\omega\cdot\frac{rS_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}$ for all but $\frac{\varepsilon\alpha n}{\omega^{2}}$ values of $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

The key to establishing Proposition 6.2 lies in leveraging the bound on the statistical error from Lemma 5.2, together with the strong convexity of $f_{i}$. Recall that Lemma 5.2 says that for any $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfying $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, we have

$$\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right). \tag{89}$$

By strong convexity of $f_{i}$, we then have

$$\displaystyle 0 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b})-f_{i}(\hat{w}_{i})\right) \tag{90}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\left(\langle\nabla f_{i}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}),\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}-\hat{w}_{i}\rangle-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}\|_{2}^{2}\right) \tag{91}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\right)S_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{92}$$

Therefore:

**Lemma 6.3.**

For any $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfying $\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$, we have

$$\frac{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}}\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)S_{\varepsilon}. \tag{93}$$

By applying Lemma 6.3 to $b_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(b_{i}+\frac{\sum_{j}b_{j}}{\sum_{j}c_{j}}c_{i}\right)$, we obtain the following corollary, which gives bounds in terms of $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$ rather than $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b}$:

**Corollary 6.4.**

For any $b_{i}\in[0,1]$ satisfying $\varepsilon\alpha n\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$, we have

$$\frac{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}}\leq\frac{16}{\kappa}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)S_{\varepsilon}. \tag{94}$$

In particular, at most $\varepsilon\alpha n$ points $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ have $\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{16}{\kappa}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)S_{\varepsilon}$.

Corollary 6.4 is crucial because it shows that all but an $\varepsilon$ fraction of the $\hat{w}_{i}$, for $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, concentrate around $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}$.

Note that we also have $\|\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}\left(\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\right)$, which is bounded by Theorem 4.1; moreover, Theorem 4.1 also bounds $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})$. Finally, we have $S_{\varepsilon/\omega^{2}}\leq 2\omega S_{\varepsilon}$ by Lemma 5.6. Combining all of these inequalities, we can obtain Proposition 6.2.

##### Padded decompositions.

Proposition 6.2 says that the output $\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{n}$ of Algorithm 1 satisfies $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq s$ for almost all $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, for some $s\ll r$. We would then ideally like to partition the $\hat{w}_{i}$ into sets of small diameter (say $2s$), such that all of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ is in a single piece of the partition (so that we can then run Algorithm 4 on each piece of the partition, and be guaranteed that at least one piece has most of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$).

In general, this may not be possible, but we can obtain a probabilistic version of the hoped for result: We will end up finding a partition into sets of diameter $\mathcal{O}\left(s\log(2/\alpha)\right)$ such that, with probability $\frac{7}{8}$, all of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ is in a single piece of the partition. This leads us to the definition of a padded decomposition:

**Definition.**

Let $x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}$ be points in a metric space. A ($\rho,\tau,\delta$)-padded decomposition is a (random) partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}$ such that: (i) each element of $\mathcal{P}$ has diameter at most $\rho$, and (ii) for each $x_{i}$, with probability $1-\delta$ all points within distance $\tau$ of $x_{i}$ lie in a single element of $\mathcal{P}$.

Fakcharoenphol et al. (2003) show that for any $\tau$ and $\delta$, a $(\rho,\tau,\delta)$-padded decomposition exists with $\rho=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\tau\log(n)}{\delta}\right)$. Moreover, the same proof shows that, if every $x_{i}$ is within distance $\tau$ of at least $\Omega(\alpha n)$ other $x_{i}$, then we can actually take $\rho=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\tau\log(2/\alpha)}{\delta}\right)$. In particular, in our case we can obtain a $(\mathcal{O}\left(s\log(2/\alpha)\right),2s,7/8)$-padded decomposition of the $\hat{w}_{i}$ output by Algorithm 1; we show this formally in Lemma A.1. This probabilistic notion of clustering turns out to be sufficient for our purposes.

##### Analyzing Algorithm 4.

We are now prepared to analyze Algorithm 4. In each iteration, Algorithm 4 independently samples $l=112\log\left(\frac{t(t+1)}{\delta}\right)$ padded decompositions of the $\hat{w}_{i}$. For each decomposition $\mathcal{P}_{h}$, it then runs Algorithm 1 on each component of the resulting partition, and thereby obtains candidate values $\bar{w}_{1}(h),\ldots,\bar{w}_{n}(h)$. Finally, it updates $\hat{w}_{i}$ by finding a point close to at least $\frac{1}{2}$ of the candidate values $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$, across $h=1,\ldots,l$.

The idea for why this works is the following: since $\frac{7}{8}$ of the time, the padded decomposition $\mathcal{P}_{h}$ succeeds in preserving $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, it is also the case that roughly $\frac{7}{8}$ of the candidate assignments $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$ are “good” assignments close to $w^{*}$. Therefore, any point that is close to at least $\frac{1}{2}$ of the $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$ is, by pigeonhole, close to one of the good $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$, and therefore also close to $w^{*}$.

If we formalize this argument, then we obtain Lemma 6.5, which controls the behavior of the update $\hat{w}_{1:n}^{(t)}\to\hat{w}_{1:n}^{(t+1)}$ on each iteration of the loop:

**Lemma 6.5.**

Algorithm 4 satisfies the following property: at the beginning of iteration $t$ of the outer loop, let $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ denote the set of points $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$ for which $\|\hat{w}_{i}^{(t)}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq r^{(t)}$. Also suppose that $|I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha n$ and $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}$. Then, with probability $1-\frac{\delta}{t(t+1)}$ over the randomness in the padded decompositions, $\|\hat{w}_{i}^{(t+1)}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}r^{(t)}$ for all but $C_{0}\cdot\left(\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\log(2/\alpha)}{\kappa r^{(t)}\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\cdot\varepsilon\alpha n$ points in $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$, for some absolute constant $C_{0}$.

Essentially, Lemma 6.5 shows that if almost all of the good points are within $r^{(t)}$ of $w^{*}$ at the beginning of a loop iteration, then almost all of the good points are within $\frac{1}{2}r^{(t)}$ of $w^{*}$ at the end of that loop iteration, provided $r^{(t)}$ is large enough. Using Lemma 6.5, we can establish Theorem 6.1.

#### Proof of Theorem 6.1

Using Lemma 6.5 we can prove Theorem 6.1. The key is to show that the precondition $|I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha n$ of Lemma 6.5 holds throughout Algorithm 4, for appropriate choices of the absolute constants $C_{1}$ and $C_{2}$.

We will actually prove the stronger claim that $|I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}|\geq(1-\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon)\alpha n$ for all $t$. Lemma 6.5 shows that $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ decreases in size by at most $C_{0}\left(\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\log(2/\alpha)}{\kappa r^{(t)}\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\varepsilon\alpha n$ at iteration $t$. Now take $C_{1}=\sqrt{2C_{0}}$. In the final iteration before halting, since $r^{(t)}\geq\sqrt{2C_{0}}\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\log(2/\alpha)}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}$, we can conclude by Lemma 6.5 that at most $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon n$ points leave $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ in this iteration of the algorithm. But if we look back to previous iterations of the algorithm, the bound on the number of points leaving from $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ decreases by a factor of $4$ each time $t$ decreases (because $r^{(t)}$ increases by a factor of $2$). Therefore, the total number of points that leave $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ over all iterations of the algorithm is at most $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon n\left(1+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{16}+\cdots\right)=\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon n<\varepsilon n$. This establishes that $|I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}|\geq(1-\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon)\alpha n$ for all iterations of the algorithm.

Now, consider the points $\hat{w}_{1:n}^{(T)}$ obtained in the final iteration $T$ of Algorithm 4. By invoking Proposition 6.2 with $\omega=\sqrt{3}$, we have for an appropriate choice of $C_{2}$ that $\|\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)}-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{r^{(T-1)}S_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}}\right)=C_{2}\cdot\frac{S_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log(2/\alpha)}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}=r_{\mathrm{final}}$ for all but $\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon\alpha n$ values of $i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(T-1)}$, and hence for all but $\varepsilon\alpha n$ values of $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. Call the set of points satisfying this condition $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{\mathrm{final}}$.

To finish, observe that by construction, $\mathcal{U}$ is a maximal set of centers $u$ such that: (i) $|B(u;2r_{\mathrm{final}})\cap\mathcal{W}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha n$ and (ii) $B(u;2r_{\mathrm{final}})\cap B(u^{\prime};2r_{\mathrm{final}})=\emptyset$ for $u\neq u^{\prime}$. But for $i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{\mathrm{final}}$, we have $B(\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)};2r_{\mathrm{final}})\supseteq B(w^{*};r_{\mathrm{final}})\supseteq\{\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)}\mid i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{\mathrm{final}}\}$, and hence $|B(\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)};2r_{\mathrm{final}})\cap\mathcal{W}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha n$. Therefore, $B(\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)};2r_{\mathrm{final}})$ must intersect $B(u;2r_{\mathrm{final}})$ for some $u\in\mathcal{U}$, as otherwise we could add $\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)}$ to $\mathcal{U}$. This implies that $\|\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)}-u\|_{2}\leq 4r_{\mathrm{final}}$, so that $\|w^{*}-u\|_{2}\leq 5r_{\mathrm{final}}$, and hence $\|\hat{w}_{i^{\prime}}^{(T)}-u\|_{2}\leq 6r_{\mathrm{final}}$ for all $i^{\prime}\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{\mathrm{final}}$. Therefore, the center $u$ satisfies the required property that $\|u-w^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(r_{\mathrm{final}})$ and $\|\hat{w}_{i}^{(T)}-u\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(r_{\mathrm{final}})$ for all but $\varepsilon\alpha n$ elements of $\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$.

We finally note that, for $u\in\mathcal{U}$, the sets $B(u;2r_{\mathrm{final}})$ are disjoint and each contain at least $(1-\varepsilon)\alpha n$ points, so there can be at most $\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}\rfloor$ elements in $\mathcal{U}$.

#### Proof of Proposition 6.2

By Corollary 6.4, we have that $\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})+r^{2}}S_{\varepsilon/\omega^{2}}}{\kappa}\right)$ for all but $\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{\omega^{2}}n$ points $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$. But $\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha}\right)$, and $S_{\varepsilon/\omega^{2}}\leq 2\omega S_{\varepsilon}$, so we have $\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\omega\cdot\frac{rS_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$ for all but $\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{\omega^{2}}n$ points.

In addition, by Theorem 2.2 we have $\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{rS}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$, which by the strong convexity of $\bar{f}$ implies that $\|\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{rS}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$. Combining these, we have $\|\hat{w}_{i}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}-w^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\omega\cdot\frac{rS_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}+\frac{rS}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\omega\cdot\frac{rS_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$, as was to be shown.

#### Proof of Corollary 6.4

For notational convenience, let $B=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}$ and $C=\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}$. We have

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}\|_{2}^{2} \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i,j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\cdot\frac{c_{j}}{C}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{j}\|_{2}^{2} \tag{95}$$

$$\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i,j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}\cdot(\tfrac{B}{C}c_{j})\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{j}\|_{2}^{2} \tag{96}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i,j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}(b_{i}+\tfrac{B}{C}c_{i})(b_{j}+\tfrac{B}{C}c_{j})\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}, \tag{97}$$

where the first inequality is convexity of $\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}$, and the second is because we only added positive terms to the sum.

Now, we will apply Lemma 6.3 at the value $b_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(b_{i}+\frac{B}{C}c_{i}\right)$. This satisfies $b_{i}^{\prime}\in[0,1]$ (since $\sum_{j}c_{j}\geq\sum_{j}b_{j}$), and also $\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}b_{i}\geq\varepsilon\alpha n$. We can therefore invoke Lemma 6.3 to obtain

$$\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i,j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}(b_{i}+\tfrac{B}{C}c_{i})(b_{j}+\tfrac{B}{C}c_{j}) \displaystyle=\frac{4}{B}\sum_{i,j\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}^{\prime}b_{j}^{\prime}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{j}\|_{2}^{2} \tag{98}$$

$$\displaystyle=8\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}b_{i}^{\prime}\|\hat{w}_{i}-\hat{w}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{b^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2} \tag{99}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\frac{16}{\kappa}\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y})}+r\right)S_{\varepsilon}, \tag{100}$$

as was to be shown.

#### Proof of Lemma 6.5

Call a partition $\mathcal{P}_{h}$ good if the set $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ lies within a single piece of $\mathcal{P}_{h}$, and let $H$ be the set of iterations for which $\mathcal{P}_{h}$ is good, and $l=112\log\left(\frac{t(t+1)}{\delta}\right)$ be the total number of iterations. Note that each $h$ is good with probability $\frac{7}{8}$ independently, so by the Chernoff bound, the probability that $|H|<\frac{3}{4}l$ is at most $\exp\left(-\frac{l}{112}\right)=\frac{\delta}{t(t+1)}$.

Note that, for any good $h$, if $T$ is the element of $\mathcal{P}_{h}$ containing $I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$, then $w^{*}$ is within distance $r^{(t)}$ of $T$, and hence if $T\subseteq B(u,\rho)$ then $w^{*}\in B(u,\rho+r^{(t)})$. Therefore, the hypothesis space for $A(u,\rho+r^{(t)})$ contains $w^{*}$ and so Proposition 6.2 implies that, for any good $h$,

$$\|\bar{w}_{i}(h)-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\omega\cdot\frac{(\rho+r^{(t)})S_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\omega\cdot\frac{r^{(t)}S_{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{2}{\alpha})}{\kappa\sqrt{\alpha}}}\right) \tag{101}$$

for all but $\frac{\varepsilon\alpha n}{\omega^{2}}$ values of $i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$. We will take $\omega$ such that the right-hand-side of (101) is $\frac{1}{6}r^{(t)}$, which yields $\omega=\Omega\left(\frac{\kappa r^{(t)}\sqrt{\alpha}}{S_{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{2}{\alpha})}\right)$.

Next, call an $i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$ bad if $\|\bar{w}_{i}(h)-w^{*}\|_{2}>\frac{1}{6}r^{(t)}$ for more than $\frac{l}{8}$ of the good $h$. Averaging over the good $h$, there are therefore at most $\frac{8\varepsilon\alpha n}{\omega^{2}}$ bad values of $i$ (since each such bad value would constitute $\frac{l}{8}$ violations of (101), and we know that there are at most $l\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\alpha n}{\omega^{2}}$ violations in total).

Now if $i$ is not bad, then $\|\bar{w}_{i}(h)-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{6}r^{(t)}$ for at least $\frac{5l}{8}$ values of $h$ (because it is small for all but $\frac{l}{8}$ of the at least $\frac{3l}{4}$ good $h$). Now, consider any $\bar{w}_{i}(h_{0})$ that is within distance $\frac{1}{3}r^{(t)}$ of at least $\frac{l}{2}$ of the $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$. By the pigeonhole principle, $\bar{w}_{i}(h_{0})$ is therefore close to at least one of these good $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$, and so satisfies $\|\bar{w}_{i}(h_{0})-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\|\bar{w}_{i}(h_{0})-\bar{w}_{i}(h)\|_{2}+\|\bar{w}_{i}(h)-w^{*}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}r^{(t)}$. Moreover, such a $h_{0}$ exists since any of the good $\bar{w}_{i}(h)$ be within distance $\frac{1}{3}r^{(t)}$ of the other good $\bar{w}_{i}(h^{\prime})$.

In sum, the assigned value $\hat{w}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\bar{w}_{i}(h_{0})$ has distance at most $\frac{1}{2}r^{(t)}$ to $w^{*}$ for all but $\frac{8\varepsilon\alpha n}{\omega^{2}}$ values of $i\in I_{\mathrm{good}}^{(t)}$, which yields the desired result.

## 7 Lower Bounds

In this section we prove lower bounds showing that the dependence of our bounds on $S$ is necessary even if $p^{*}$ is a multivariate Gaussian. For $\alpha$, we are only able to show a necessary dependence of $\sqrt{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$, rather than the $\sqrt{{1}/{\alpha}}$ appearing in our bounds. Determining the true worst-case dependence on $\alpha$ is an interesting open problem.

One natural question is whether $S$, which typically depends on the maximum singular value of the covariance matrix, is really the right dependence, or whether we could achieve bounds based on e.g. the average singular value instead. Lemma 7.1 rules this out, showing that it would require $\Omega(2^{k})$ candidates in the list-decodable setting to achieve dependence on even the $k$th singular value of the covariance matrix.

**Lemma 7.1.**

Suppose that $p^{*}$ is known to be a multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$ with covariance $\Sigma\preceq\Sigma_{0}$, where $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are otherwise unknown. Also let $\sigma_{k}^{2}$ denote the $k$th largest singular value of $\Sigma_{0}$. Then, given any amount of data, an $\alpha$-fraction of which is drawn from $p^{*}$, any procedure for outputting at most $m=2^{k-1}$ candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$ must have, with probability at least $\frac{1}{2}$, $\min_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\mu}_{j}-\mu\|_{2}\geq\frac{\sigma_{k}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$.

By the reduction from the semi-verified to the list-decodable model, we obtain a lower bound in the semi-verified setting as well:

**Corollary 7.2.**

Any algorithm that has $\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}<\frac{\sigma_{k}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$ with probability at least $\frac{1}{2}$ in the semi-verified model requires at least $\frac{k-2}{\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$ verified samples.

We remark that the same proofs show lower bounds for non-strongly convex losses as well. For instance, while mean estimation corresponds to $f_{i}(w)=\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, we could also estimate the direction of a vector by taking $f_{i}(w)=-w^{\top}x_{i}$, and constraining $\|w\|_{2}\leq r$; in this case the minimizer of $\bar{f}(w)$ is $w^{*}=r\cdot\frac{\mu}{\|\mu\|_{2}}$, where $\mu=\mathbb{E}[x]$. An essentially identical argument to Lemma 7.1 and Corollary 7.2 shows that at least $2^{\Omega(k)}$ candidates $\hat{w}_{j}$ are necessary to obtain $\min_{j}\bar{f}(\hat{w}_{j})-\bar{f}(w^{*})\leq r\cdot\frac{\sigma_{k}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$ with probability greater than $\frac{1}{2}$, and at least $\Omega\left(\frac{k}{\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}\right)$ verified samples are needed in the semi-verified model. To avoid repetition we omit these.

#### Proof of Lemma 7.1

Let us suppose that the unverified data has distribution $\hat{p}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})$, which is possible iff $\hat{p}\geq\alpha p^{*}$. We start with a lemma characterizing when it is possible that the true distribution $p^{*}$ has mean $\mu$:

**Lemma 7.3.**

Let $\hat{p}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})$ and $p^{*}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma_{0}-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top})$. Then $\hat{p}\geq\alpha p^{*}$ provided that $\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu\leq\frac{\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$ and $\lambda=\frac{1}{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$.

As a consequence, if $\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu\leq t^{2}$ for $t=\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$, then $p^{*}$ could have mean $\mu$. Now, consider the space spanned by the $k$ largest eigenvectors of $\Sigma_{0}$, and let $B_{k}$ be the ball of radius $t\sigma_{k}$ in this space. Also let $\mathcal{P}$ be a maximal packing of $B_{k}$ of radius $\frac{t}{4}\sigma_{k}$. A simple volume argument shows that $|B_{k}|\geq 2^{k}$. On the other hand, every element $\mu$ of $B_{k}$ is a potential mean because it satisfies $\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\leq t^{2}$. Therefore, if the true mean $\mu$ is drawn uniformly from $B_{k}$, any $2^{k-1}$ candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{j}$ must miss at least half of the elements of $B_{k}$ (in the sense of being distance at least $\frac{t}{4}\sigma_{k}$ away) and so with probability at least $\frac{1}{2}$, $\|\hat{\mu}_{j}-\mu\|_{2}$ is at least $\frac{t}{4}\sigma_{k}$ for all $j$, as was to be shown.

#### Proof of Corollary 7.2

Suppose that we can obtain $\hat{\mu}$ satisfying $\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}<\frac{\sigma_{k}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$ with $m\leq\frac{k-2}{\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$ samples, with probability at least $\frac{1}{2}$. If we sample $m$ elements uniformly from the unverified samples, with probability $\alpha^{m}$ we will obtain only samples from $p^{*}$, and therefore with probability $\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{m}$ we can obtain (by assumption) a candidate mean $\hat{\mu}$ with $\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}<\frac{\sigma_{k}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\sqrt{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}}$. If we repeat this $\frac{2}{\alpha^{m}}$ times, then with probability $1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{m}\right)^{\frac{2}{\alpha^{m}}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{e}>\frac{1}{2}$, at least one of the candidate means will be close to $\mu$. But by Lemma 7.1, this implies that $\frac{2}{\alpha^{m}}\geq 2^{k-1}$, and so $m\geq\frac{k-2}{\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$, as claimed.

## 8 Intuition: Stability Under Subsets

In this section, we establish a sort of “duality for robustness” that provides intuition underlying our results. Essentially, we will show the following:

> If a statistic of a dataset is approximately preserved across every large subset of the data, then it can be robustly recovered even in the presence of a large amount of additional arbitrary/adversarial data.

To be a bit more formal, suppose that for a set of points $\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}$ lying in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, there is a subset $I\subseteq[n]$ with the following property: for any subset $T\subseteq I$ of size at least $\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$, the mean over $T$ is $\varepsilon$-close to the mean over $I$. In symbols,

$$\left\|\mu_{T}-\mu_{I}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }T\subseteq I\text{ with }|T|\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n,\text{ where }\mu_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{i\in T}x_{i}. \tag{102}$$

In such a case, can we approximate the mean of $I$, even if $I$ is not known and the points in $[n]\backslash I$ are arbitrary?

If we do not care about computation, the answer is yes, via a simple exponential-time algorithm. Call a set of points $J$ $\alpha$-stable if it satisfies the following properties: $|J|\geq\alpha n$, and (102) holds with $I$ replaced by $J$ (i.e., the mean moves by at most $\varepsilon$ when taking subsets of $J$ of size at least $\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$). Then, we can run the following (exponential-time) algorithm:

1. Initialize $\mathcal{U}=[]$.
2. Find an $\alpha$-stable set $J$ which has overlap at most $\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$ with all elements of $\mathcal{U}$.
3. Append $J$ to $\mathcal{U}$ and continue until no more such sets exist.

A simple inclusion-exclusion argument[^5] shows that $k\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}$. Therefore, the above algorithm terminates with $|\mathcal{U}|\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}$. On the other hand, by construction, $I$ must have overlap at least $\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$ with at least one element of $\mathcal{U}$ (as otherwise we could have also added $I$ to $\mathcal{U}$). Therefore, for some $J\in\mathcal{U}$, $|I\cap J|\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$. But then, letting $T=I\cap J$, we have $\|\mu_{I}-\mu_{J}\|_{2}\leq\|\mu_{I}-\mu_{T}\|_{2}+\|\mu_{T}-\mu_{J}\|_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon$. Therefore, the mean over $I$ is within distance $2\varepsilon$ of at least one of the $\mu_{J}$, for $J\in\mathcal{U}$.

We have therefore established our stated duality property for robustness: if a statistic is stable under taking subsets of data, then it can also be recovered if the data is itself a subset of some larger set of data.

Can we make the above algorithm computationally efficient? First, can we even check if a set $J$ is $\alpha$-stable? This involves checking the following constraint:

$$\left\|\sum_{i\in J}c_{i}(x_{i}-\mu_{J})\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|c\|_{1}\text{for all $c$ such that }c_{i}\in\{0,1\},\sum_{i\in J}c_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n. \tag{103}$$

It is already unclear how to check this constraint efficiently. However, defining the matrix $C_{ij}=c_{i}c_{j}\in[0,1]^{J\times J}$, we can take a semi-definite relaxation of $C$, resulting in the constraints $C_{ij}\in[0,1]$ and $\operatorname{tr}(C)\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n$. Letting $A_{ij}=(x_{i}-\mu_{J})^{\top}(x_{j}-\mu_{J})$, this results in the following sufficient condition for $\alpha$-stability:

$$\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}C) \displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}\operatorname{tr}(C)^{2}\text{ for all $C$ such that }$$

$$\displaystyle C\succeq 0,\ \displaystyle C_{ij}\in[0,1],\ \operatorname{tr}(C)\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}n. \tag{104}$$

This is still non-convex due to the presence of $\operatorname{tr}(C)^{2}$. However, a sufficient condition for (104) to hold is that $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}C)\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}n\operatorname{tr}(C)$ for all $C\succeq 0$, which is equivalent to $\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}n$, or, letting $X$ be the matrix with rows $x_{i}-\mu_{J}$, $\varepsilon\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha n}}\|X\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)$, where $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha n}}\|X\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is essentially our definition of $S$. From this, we can directly see why $S/\sqrt{\alpha}$ appears in our bounds: it is a convex relaxation of the $\alpha$-stability condition. Our main optimization (4) can (somewhat less directly) also be thought of as a semidefinite relaxation which attempts to represent the means of all $\frac{2}{\alpha}$ of the $\alpha$-stable sets simultaneously (this is done implicitly, via the major axes of the ellipse $Y$).

As an aside, we note that the above discussion, together with the bound (Lemma B.3) on the operator norm of samples from a sub-Gaussian distribution, implies that a set of samples from a sub-Gaussian distribution is $\alpha$-stable with high probability, and in particular the mean of any large subset of samples is close to the mean of the overall set. Moreover, even if a distribution has only bounded second moments, Proposition B.1 shows that a suitable subset of samples from the distribution will be $\alpha$-stable.

## 9 Applications

In this section we present several applications of our main results.

### 9.1 Gaussians and Mixtures of sub-Gaussians

For robustly learning the mean of a distribution with bounded variance, we have the following result:

**Corollary 9.1.**

Let $p^{*}$ be a distribution on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with mean $\mu$ and bounded covariance: $\operatorname{Cov}_{p^{*}}[x]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$. Suppose that we observe $n\geq\frac{d}{\alpha}$ points, of which $\alpha n$ are drawn from $p^{*}$. Then, for any $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}$, with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}\alpha n))$, it is possible to output parameters $\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{m}$, where $m\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha-\varepsilon}\rfloor$, such that $\min_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{w}_{j}-\mu\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}\right)$.

**Proof.**

Define $f_{i}(w)=\|w-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}$, which is $1$-strongly convex and has $w^{*}=\mu$. In this case, $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)=x_{i}-\mu$, and so Proposition B.1 implies that with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}\alpha n))$ there is a subset of $(1-\varepsilon/2)\alpha n$ of the good points satisfying $S=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)$, and hence (by Lemma 5.6) $S_{\varepsilon/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma/\varepsilon\right)$. Theorem 6.1 then says that we can output parameters $\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{m}$, where $m\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon/2)^{2}\alpha}\rfloor\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}\rfloor$, such that $\min_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{w}_{j}-\mu\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}\right)$, as was to be shown. ∎

For robustly learning a mixture of distributions, we have the following result:

**Corollary 9.2.**

Suppose we are given $n$ samples, where each sample either comes from one of $k$ sub-Gaussian distributions $p_{1}^{*},\ldots,p_{k}^{*}$ (with $\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{i}^{*}}[x]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$), or is arbitrary. Let $I_{i}$, $\alpha_{i}$, and $\mu_{i}$ denote respectively the set of points drawn from $p_{i}^{*}$, the fraction of points drawn from $p_{i}^{*}$, and the mean of $p_{i}^{*}$, and let $\alpha=\min_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}$. Suppose that $n\geq\frac{d}{\alpha}$. Then for any $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}$, with probability $1-k\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}\alpha n))$, we can output a partition $T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}$ of $[n]$ and candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$ such that:

1. $m\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}\rfloor$.
2. For all but $\varepsilon\alpha_{i}n$ of the elements of $h$ of $I_{i}$, $h$ is assigned a partition $T_{j}$ such that $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\Big({\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}}\Big)$, where $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$ masks only absolute constants.

**Proof.**

We apply Theorem 6.1 in the same way as in Corollary 9.1, letting the candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$ be the cluster centers $\mathcal{U}$ output by Algorithm 4. For each $i$, we then know that $\|\hat{\mu}_{j}-\mu_{i}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}\Big)$ for at least one of the means $\hat{\mu}_{j}$.[^6] Moreover, we know that the $\hat{w}_{h}$ output by Algorithm 4 satisfy $\|\hat{w}_{h}-\mu_{i}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}}\Big)$ for all but $\varepsilon\alpha_{i}n$ elements of $h\in I_{i}$. We can therefore define $T_{j}$ to be the set of $h$ for which $\hat{w}_{h}$ is closer to $\hat{\mu}_{j}$ than to any of the other $\hat{\mu}_{j^{\prime}}$. It is then easy to see that any $h\in I_{i}$ which is close to $w_{i}$ will be assigned a mean $\hat{\mu}_{j}$ that is also close to $w_{i}$. This yields the desired result. ∎

For the planted partition model, we have the following result:

**Corollary 9.3.**

Let $I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}$ be disjoint subsets of $[n]$, and consider the following random directed graph with vertex set $[n]$:

- If $u,v\in I_{i}$, the probability of an edge from $u$ to $v$ is $\frac{a}{n}$.
- If $u\in I_{i},v\not\in I_{i}$, the probability of an edge from $u$ to $v$ is $\frac{b}{n}$.
- If $u\not\in\cup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}$, the edges emanating from $u$ can be arbitrary.

Let $\alpha=\min_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}$. Then with probability $1-k\exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$, we can output sets $T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\subseteq[n]$, with $m\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}$, such that for all $i\in[k]$, there is a $j\in[m]$ with $|I_{i}\triangle T_{j}|\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}(a-b)^{2}}n\right)$, where $\triangle$ denotes the symmetric difference of two sets.

**Proof.**

It turns out that this problem reduces to mean estimation as well, though we will need to be more careful in how we apply our matrix concentration bound.

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of the graph. We will let $f_{u}(w)=\|w-A_{u}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{v=1}^{n}(w_{v}-A_{uv})^{2}$. Note that for $u\in I_{i}$, we have $\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[A_{uv}]=\frac{b}{n}+\frac{a-b}{n}\mathbb{I}[v\in I_{i}]$, and $\operatorname{Cov}[A_{u}]$ is a diagonal matrix with entries that are all either $\frac{a}{n}$ or $\frac{b}{n}$, so that in particular $\|\operatorname{Cov}[A_{u}]\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{a}{n}$, and hence $\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{a}{n}}$.

In this case we have $d=n$, so Proposition B.1 implies that we can find a subset of $I_{i}$ of size at least $|I_{i}|/2\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}n$ such that $S=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\sqrt{1+\frac{d}{\alpha n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{a}{\alpha n}}\right)$. Invoking Theorem 6.1 with $\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}$, we obtain candidate means $\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{m}$, with $m\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}$, such that $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{S^{2}\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}n}\right)$.

Now, we define the set $T_{j}$ such that $u\in T_{j}$ iff $(\hat{\mu}_{j})_{u}\geq\frac{a+b}{2n}$. Suppose for some $u$, $u\in I_{i}$ but $u\not\in T_{j}$. Then we must have $(\hat{\mu}_{j})_{u}<\frac{a+b}{2n}$, but $(\mu_{i})_{u}=\frac{a}{n}$. Therefore, any $u\in I_{i}$ with $u\not\in T_{j}$ contributes at least $\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{4n^{2}}$ to $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}$. Similarly, any $u\in T_{j}$ with $u\not\in I_{i}$ similarly contributed at least $\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{4n^{2}}$. We can conclude that $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{4n^{2}}|I_{i}\triangle T_{j}|$. In particular, if $\|\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}n}\right)$, then $|I_{i}\triangle T_{j}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a\log(2/\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}(a-b)^{2}}n\right)$, as was to be shown. ∎

Finally, we have the following corollary for density modeling:

**Corollary 9.4.**

Let $p_{\theta}(x)=\exp\left(\theta^{\top}\phi(x)-A(\theta)\right)$, where $A(\theta)$ is the log-normalization constant. Let $x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}$ be points such that $\alpha n$ are drawn from $p^{*}$, and $\operatorname{Cov}_{p^{*}}[\phi(x)]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$. Also suppose that $\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $n\geq\frac{d}{\alpha}$. Then with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$, the following hold:

- Given a single verified sample $x\sim p^{*}$, we can output $\hat{\theta}$ such that $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{KL}\left(p_{\theta^{*}}\ \|\ p_{\hat{\theta}}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)]$, where $\theta^{*}$ is the minimizer of $\mathbb{E}[-\log p_{\theta}(x)]$.
- We can output $m=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\alpha}\right)$ candidates $\hat{\theta}_{j}$ such that, with probability $1-\delta$, $\min_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{KL}\left(\smash{p_{\theta^{*}}}\ \|\ \smash{p_{\hat{\theta}_{j}}}\right)]$ is at most $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$.

Note that $\operatorname{KL}\left(p_{\theta^{*}}\ \|\ p_{\theta}\right)=(\theta^{*}-\theta)^{\top}\mu+A(\theta)-A(\theta^{*})$, where $\mu=\mathbb{E}[\phi(x)]$, so Corollary 9.4 roughly asks for $\theta$ to have large inner product with $\mu$, but with the additional goal of keeping $A(\theta)$ small.

**Proof of Corollary 9.4.**

Define the loss $f_{i}(\theta)=-\log p_{\theta}(x_{i})=A(\theta)-\theta^{\top}\phi(x_{i})$. We observe that $\bar{f}(\theta)-\bar{f}(\theta^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[(\theta^{*}-\theta)^{\top}\phi(x)+A(\theta)-A(\theta^{*})]=(\theta^{*}-\theta)^{\top}\mu+A(\theta)-A(\theta^{*})=\operatorname{KL}\left(p_{\theta^{*}}\ \|\ p_{\hat{\theta}}\right)$, so bounding $\operatorname{KL}\left(p_{\theta^{*}}\ \|\ p_{\theta}\right)$ is equivalent to bounding $\bar{f}(\theta)-\bar{f}(\theta^{*})$.

Note that $\nabla f_{i}(\theta)-\nabla\bar{f}(\theta)=x_{i}-\mu$. Therefore, Proposition B.1 implies that with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\alpha n))$ there is a set of at least $\frac{\alpha}{2}n$ samples $x_{i}$ with spectral norm bound $S=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)$. Theorem 4.1 then implies that we obtain an ellipse $\mathcal{E}_{\hat{Y}}$ and samples $\hat{\theta}_{1:n}$ such that $\hat{\theta}_{i}\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\top}\preceq\hat{Y}$ and $\bar{f}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{avg}})-\bar{f}(\theta^{*})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$.

Now take a single verified sample $x\sim p^{*}$, and define $\hat{\theta}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\theta\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}A(\theta)-\theta^{\top}\phi(x)$. Similarly to Lemma 2.4, we have

$$\displaystyle\bar{f}(\hat{\theta})-\bar{f}(\theta^{*}) \displaystyle=(\hat{\theta}^{*}-\theta)^{\top}\mu+A(\hat{\theta})-A(\theta^{*}) \tag{105}$$

$$\displaystyle=(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}(\mu-\phi(x))+(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}\phi(x)+A(\hat{\theta})-A(\theta^{*}) \tag{106}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}(\mu-\phi(x)), \tag{107}$$

where (i) is because $\hat{\theta}$ minimizes the right-hand term in (106). On the other hand, we have

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[((\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}(\mu-\phi(x)))^{2}] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\mu-\phi(x))^{\top}(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}(\mu-\phi(x))] \tag{108}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\mu-\phi(x))^{\top}\hat{Y}(\mu-\phi(x))] \tag{109}$$

$$\displaystyle=4\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}[\phi(x)]^{\top}\hat{Y}) \tag{110}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 4\sigma^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\hat{Y}) \tag{111}$$

$$\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}r^{2}}{\alpha}\right). \tag{112}$$

In particular, $\mathbb{E}[(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}(\mu-\phi(x))]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma r}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)$, which together with the preceding inequality yields the desired bound in $\bar{f}(\hat{\theta})-\bar{f}(\theta^{*})$.

The above gives us the desired result in the semi-verified model. But then, we can reduce the semi-verified model to the list decodable model by sampling random points from the untrusted data, which gives us the list decodable result as well. ∎

## Appendix A Padded Decompositions

The following lemma is essentially from Fakcharoenphol et al. (2003).

**Lemma A.1.**

Let $x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}$ be points in a metric space, and let $I\subseteq[n]$ be such that $d(x_{i},x_{j})\leq r$ for all $i,j\in I$. Consider the following procedure:

- Initialize $X=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, $\mathcal{P}=\emptyset$, and sample $k$ uniformly from $2$ to $k_{\max}$, where $k_{\max}\geq 1+\frac{1}{\delta}\log(\tfrac{n}{|I|})$.
- While there are points remaining in $S$:
 - Sample $i$ uniformly from $\{1,\ldots,n\}$
 - Let $T$ be the set of points in $X$ within distance $kr$ of $x_{i}$.
 - Update $X\leftarrow X\backslash T$, $\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\cup\{T\}$.
- Output the partition $\mathcal{P}$.

Then, with probability at least $1-\delta$, all elements of $I$ are in a single element of $\mathcal{P}$.

**Proof.**

It suffices to show the following: if $T$ contains any element of $I$, then with probability at least $\frac{3}{4}$ it contains all elements of $I$.

To see this, let $N(k)$ denote the set of points within distance $kr$ of at least one point in $I$. Then, for a fixed $k$, the probability that $T$ intersects $I$ is $\frac{|N(k)|}{n}$; but since all points in $I$ are within distance $r$ of each other, the probability that $T$ contains all of $I$ is at least $\frac{|N(k-1)|}{n}$. Letting $a_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}|N(k)|$, the conditional probability that $T$ contains $I$, given that it intersects $I$, is therefore at least $\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_{k}}$. Marginalizing over $k$, the probability is at least

$$\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{\max}-1}\sum_{k=2}^{k_{\max}}\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_{k}} \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\geq}}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{k_{\max}}\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k_{\max}-1}} \tag{113}$$

$$\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{|I|}{n}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{\log(n/|I|)}} \tag{114}$$

$$\displaystyle=e^{-\delta}\geq 1-\delta, \tag{115}$$

as was to be shown. Here (i) is the good old AM-GM inequality. ∎

## Appendix B Matrix Concentration Bounds

### B.1 Concentration from Second Moments

The following is proved using techniques from Batson et al. (2012). However, the bound itself appears to be novel.

**Proposition B.1.**

Suppose that $p$ is a distribution on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $\mathbb{E}_{p}[X]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Cov}_{p}[X]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$ for some $\sigma$. Then, given $n$ samples from $p$, with probability $1-\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n}{16}\right)$ there is a subset $J\subseteq[n]$ of size at least $(1-\varepsilon)n$ such that $\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{|J|}\sum_{i\in J}(x_{i}-\mu)(x_{i}-\mu)^{\top}\right)\leq\frac{4\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{d}{(1-\varepsilon)n}\right)$.

**Proof.**

Without loss of generality we will take $\mu=0$. Our proof relies on the following claim:

**Lemma B.2.**

For a matrix $M$, suppose that $M\prec cI$ and $\operatorname{tr}\left((cI-M)^{-1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$. Then, for $x\sim p$, with probability at least $1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$ we have $M+\varepsilon xx^{\top}\prec(c+4\sigma^{2})I$ and $\operatorname{tr}\left(((c+4\sigma^{2})I-(M+\varepsilon xx^{\top}))^{-1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$.

We prove this claim below, first showing how Proposition B.1 follows from the claim. We will go through the stream of $n$ samples, and keep a running set $J$ and matrix $M$. Initially $J=\emptyset$ and $M=0$. For each index $i$ in the stream, we will add $i$ to $J$ (and update $M$ to $M+\varepsilon x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}$) if the conclusion of Lemma B.2 holds for $c=4\sigma^{2}(d+|J|)$. Note that then, by induction, the precondition of Lemma B.2 always holds for $c=4\sigma^{2}(d+|J|)$, since it holds when $J=\emptyset$ and the condition for adding $i$ to $J$ ensures that it continues to hold.

At the end of the stream, we therefore obtain $J$ and $M$ such that $M\preceq 4\sigma^{2}(d+|J|)I$, which implies that $\lambda_{\max}(\frac{1}{|J|}\sum_{i\in J}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon|J|}\lambda_{\max}(M)\leq\frac{4\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{d}{|J|}\right)$. It remains to show that $|J|\geq(1-\varepsilon)n$ with high probability.

To see this, note that for each element $i$, $|J|$ increases by $1$ with probability at least $1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$. The distribution of $|J|$ is therefore lower-bounded by the sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli variables with bias $1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$, which by the Chernoff bound is greater than $(1-\varepsilon)n$ with probability at least $1-\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n}{16}\right)$. ∎

**Proof of Lemma B.2.**

This is essentially Lemma 3.3 of Batson et al. (2012). Instead of $M+\varepsilon xx^{\top}$, it will be helpful later to consider consider $M+txx^{\top}$ for arbitrary $t\in[0,\varepsilon]$. By the Sherman-Morrison matrix inversion formula, we have

$$((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M-txx^{\top})^{-1}=((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}+t\frac{((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}xx^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}}{1-tx^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}x}. \tag{116}$$

Therefore, letting $\Phi_{c}(M)$ denote $\operatorname{tr}((cI-M)^{-1})$, we have

$$\displaystyle\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M+txx^{\top}) \displaystyle=\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M)+\frac{x^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-2}x}{1/t-x^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}x}. \tag{117}$$

We want to have $\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}I}(M+txx^{\top})\leq\Phi_{c}(M)$, which in light of the above is equivalent to the condition

$$\frac{1}{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}x^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}x+\frac{x^{\top}((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-2}x}{\Phi_{c}(M)-\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M)}. \tag{118}$$

Let us compute the expectation of the right-hand-side. Since $\mathbb{E}[x^{\top}Ax]=\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}[x])$ and $\operatorname{Cov}[x]\preceq\sigma^{2}I$, the right-hand-side of (118) has expectation at most

$$\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}\right)+\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left(((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-2}\right)}{\Phi_{c}(M)-\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M)}\right)\leq\sigma^{2}\left(\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M)+\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\right), \tag{119}$$

where the second step is in light of the inequality

$$\displaystyle\Phi_{c}(M)-\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M) \displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left((cI-M)^{-1}-((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-1}\right)$$

$$\displaystyle\geq 4\sigma^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(((c+4\sigma^{2})I-M)^{-2}\right).$$

Since $\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M)\leq\Phi_{c}(M)\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$, the right-hand-side of (118) is at most $\frac{1}{2}$ in expectation. Therefore, with probability at least $1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$, it is at most $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$, in which case (118) holds for all $t\in[0,\varepsilon]$.

This certainly implies that $\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M+\varepsilon xx^{\top})=\operatorname{tr}\left(((c+4\sigma^{2})I-(M+\varepsilon xx^{\top}))^{-1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$. Moreover, we also have that $\Phi_{c+4\sigma^{2}}(M+txx^{\top})\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}<\infty$ for all $t\in[0,\varepsilon]$. Since the eigenvalues of $M+txx^{\top}$ are a continuous function of $t$, we therefore know that the maximum eigenvalue of $M+txx^{\top}$ never crosses $c+4\sigma^{2}$ for $t\in[0,\varepsilon]$, and so $M+\varepsilon xx^{\top}\prec(c+4\sigma^{2})I$ as well (since $M\prec cI\prec(c+4\sigma^{2})I$ by assumption). ∎

### B.2 Concentration for sub-Gaussians

In this section we provide a matrix concentration result for sub-Gaussian variables, which underlies Lemma 5.5. The proofs closely mirrors Theorem 5.39 of Vershynin (2010).

**Lemma B.3.**

Let $p$ be a distribution over vectors $X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ such that (i) $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$, and (ii) $\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\langle\lambda,X\rangle\right)]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\lambda\|_{2}^{2}\right)$ for some constant $\sigma$. Then, if $M$ is an $m\times d$ matrix with each row drawn i.i.d. from $p$, with probability $1-\delta$ the following holds simultaneous for all subsets $T$ of the rows of $M$ with $|T|\geq s$:

$$\|M_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq 18\sigma^{2}\left(d+s\log(e/\beta)+\log(1/\delta)\right), \tag{120}$$

where $\beta=s/m$.

**Proof.**

We will bound

$$\max_{|T|\geq s}\frac{\|M_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}-Q}{|T|}, \tag{121}$$

where $Q=18\sigma^{2}\left(d+\log(1/\delta)\right)$.

Let $\mathcal{N}$ be a $(1/4)$-net of the sphere $S^{d-1}$, meaning that all points in $S^{d-1}$ are within distance at most $\frac{1}{4}$ of a point in $\mathcal{N}$. As shown in Vershynin (2010), $\mathcal{N}$ can be taken to have size at most $9^{d}$, and moreover one has $\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}}2\|M_{T}u\|_{2}^{2}\geq\max_{\|u\|_{2}\leq 1}\|M_{T}u\|_{2}^{2}=\|M_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}$. Using these results, we can bound (121) as

$$\displaystyle\max_{|T|\geq s}\frac{\|M_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}-Q}{|T|} \displaystyle\leq\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}}\max_{|T|\geq s}\frac{2\|M_{T}u\|_{2}^{2}-Q}{|T|} \tag{122}$$

$$\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s}\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}}\max_{|T|=s}\left(\Big(\sum_{i\in T}\langle M_{i},u\rangle^{2}\Big)-Q/2\right). \tag{123}$$

We will focus on bounding the inner expression for a fixed $T$, then union bound over the $9^{d}\binom{m}{s}$ possibilities for $|T|$ and $u$. Let $Z_{i}={\langle M_{i},u\rangle}^{2}$. Note that $\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=u^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}[X]u\leq\sigma^{2}$. In addition, $\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda\langle M_{i},u\rangle\right)]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\lambda^{2}\right)$ (since $\|u\|_{2}=1$), and so (see Lemma C.3) we have $\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda\left(Z_{i}-\sigma^{2}\right)\right)]\leq\exp\left(16\sigma^{4}\lambda^{2}\right)$ for $0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$. By independence, it follows that $\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{i\in T}(Z_{i}-\sigma^{2})\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(16s\sigma^{4}\lambda^{2}\right)$, again for $0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$. By a Chernoff argument, at $\lambda=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$, we obtain

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in T}\left(\langle M_{i},u\rangle\right)^{2}\geq s\sigma^{2}+t\right] \displaystyle\leq\exp\left(s-\frac{t}{4\sigma}\right). \tag{124}$$

We need the right-hand-side to be smaller than $\frac{\delta}{9^{d}\binom{m}{s}}$, for which we can take

$$\displaystyle t \displaystyle=4\sigma^{2}\left(d\log(9)+s+s\log(me/s)+\log(1/\delta)\right) \tag{125}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 4\sigma^{2}\left(2.2d+s+s\log(e/\beta)+\log(1/\delta)\right). \tag{126}$$

Overall, we have that with probability $1-\delta$,

$$\displaystyle\sum_{i\in T}(\langle M_{i},u\rangle)^{2} \displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\left(8.8d+5s+4s\log(e/\beta)+4\log(1/\delta)\right) \tag{127}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 9\sigma^{2}\left(d+s\log(e/\beta)+\log(1/\delta)\right) \tag{128}$$

for all $T$ of size $s$ and all $u\in\mathcal{N}$. In particular, (123) can be bounded by $18\sigma^{2}\log(e/\beta)$, which completes the proof. ∎

### C.1 Bounding Quadratics

**Lemma C.1.**

Suppose that $z\leq a+\sqrt{bz+c^{2}}$ where $a,b,c,\geq 0$. Then, $z\leq 2a+b+c$.

**Proof.**

Re-arranging to remove the square-root, we have $z^{2}-(2a+b)z+(a^{2}-c^{2})\leq 0$. Now suppose that $z>2a+b+c$. Then, $z^{2}-(2a+b)z+(a^{2}-c^{2})=z(z-2a-b)+(a^{2}-c^{2})>(2a+b+c)c+(a^{2}-c^{2})=a^{2}+(2a+b)c>0$, which is a contradiction. ∎

### C.3 Proof of Lemma 2.4

We start by showing that $\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|f(w)-\bar{f}(w)|^{q}\right]^{1/q}\leq\sqrt{2\operatorname{tr}(Y)}\sigma_{q}$. Let $\mu=\mathbb{E}[x]$ and $\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}[x]$. We have

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|f(w)-\bar{f}(w)|^{q}\right] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|\phi(w^{\top}x)-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}}[\phi(w^{\top}x^{\prime})]|^{q}\right] \tag{129}$$

$$\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}}[\phi(w^{\top}x)-\phi(w^{\top}x^{\prime})]|^{q}\right] \tag{130}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}}[|\phi(w^{\top}x)-\phi(w^{\top}x^{\prime})|^{2}]^{q/2}\right] \tag{131}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}}[(w^{\top}(x-x^{\prime}))^{2}]^{q/2}\right] \tag{132}$$

$$\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}\Big(w^{\top}\left(\Sigma+(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\top}\right)w\Big)^{q/2}\right] \tag{133}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\operatorname{tr}(Y^{\top}\left(\Sigma+(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\top}\right))^{q/2}\right] \tag{134}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iv)}}{{\leq}}2^{q/2-1}\left(\operatorname{tr}(Y^{\top}\Sigma)^{q/2}+\mathbb{E}_{x}[((x-\mu)^{\top}Y(x-\mu))^{q/2}]\right), \tag{135}$$

where (i) and (iv) are applications of the power mean inequality, (ii) is because $\phi$ is $1$-Lipschitz, and (iii) is because $ww^{\top}\preceq Y$.

On the other hand, we have $\Sigma\preceq\sigma_{q}^{2}I$ by the $q$th moment condition (since $q>2$), so $\operatorname{tr}(Y^{\top}\Sigma)^{q/2}\leq\operatorname{tr}(Y)^{q/2}\sigma_{q}^{q}$. Also, letting $Y=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}$ be the eigendecomposition of $Y$, we have

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[((x-\mu)^{\top}Y(x-\mu))^{q/2}] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\Big[\Big(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(v_{i}^{\top}(x-\mu))^{2}\Big)^{q/2}\Big] \tag{136}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(v)}}{{\leq}}\operatorname{tr}(Y)^{q/2-1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(v_{i}^{\top}(x-\mu))^{q}\right] \tag{137}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(vi)}}{{\leq}}\operatorname{tr}(Y)^{q/2}\sigma_{q}^{q}, \tag{138}$$

where (v) is again the power mean inequality and (vi) uses the $q$th moment bound $\mathbb{E}[(v_{i}^{\top}(x-\mu))^{q}]\leq\sigma_{q}^{q}$.

Combining these, we obtain $\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|f(w)-\bar{f}(w)|^{q}\right]\leq(2\operatorname{tr}(Y))^{q/2}\sigma_{q}^{q}$. To finish, we simply apply Markov’s inequality to this statistic to get a tail bound on $\sup_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}}|f(w)-\bar{f}(w)|$, after which the result follows from a standard uniform convergence argument.

### C.4 Proof of Lemma 3.1

By duality of operator and trace norm, we have (letting $z_{i}$ be the $i$th row of the matrix $Z$)

$$\displaystyle\|W_{T}\|_{*}^{2} \displaystyle=\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}\operatorname{tr}(W_{T}^{\top}Z)^{2} \tag{139}$$

$$\displaystyle=\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}\left(\sum_{i\in T}w_{i}^{\top}z_{i}\right)^{2} \tag{140}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}|T|\sum_{i\in T}(w_{i}^{\top}z_{i})^{2} \tag{141}$$

$$\displaystyle=\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}|T|\sum_{i\in T}w_{i}^{\top}\left(z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}\right)w_{i} \tag{142}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}|T|\sum_{i\in T}\operatorname{tr}(Yz_{i}z_{i}^{\top}) \tag{143}$$

$$\displaystyle=\sup_{\|Z\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}|T|\operatorname{tr}(YZ^{\top}Z) \tag{144}$$

$$\displaystyle=|T|\operatorname{tr}(Y), \tag{145}$$

as was to be shown. Here (i) is Cauchy-Schwarz and (ii) uses the fact that $w_{i}w_{i}\preceq Y$.

### C.5 Proof of Lemma 4.6

Let $F$ be the matrix whose $i$th row is $\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0})$, and let $W$ be the matrix whose $i$th row is $w_{i}$. We consider only the rows $i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}$, so each matrix is $\alpha n\times d$. We have

$$\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i\in\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}}c_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(w_{0})-\nabla\bar{f}(w_{0}),w_{i}\rangle\Big| \displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(F^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(c)W) \tag{146}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq\|\operatorname{diag}(c)F\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|W\|_{*} \tag{147}$$

by Hölder’s inequality. Furthermore, $\|\operatorname{diag}(c)F\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\operatorname{diag}(c)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|F\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|F\|_{\mathrm{op}}$, and furthermore $\|F\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{\alpha n}S$ by the definition of $S$. Finally, $\|W\|_{*}\leq\sqrt{|\smash{I_{\mathrm{good}}}|\operatorname{tr}(Y)}=\sqrt{\alpha n\operatorname{tr}(Y)}$ by Lemma 3.1. Combining these, we see that (147) is bounded by $\alpha nS\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Y)}$, as was to be shown.

### C.6 Proof of Lemma 5.5

**Proof.**

Define $S_{\varepsilon}(w)$ as

$$S_{\varepsilon}(w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\max_{|T|\geq\lfloor\varepsilon\alpha n\rfloor}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\|\left[\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)\right]_{i\in T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \tag{148}$$

Therefore, a union bound argument yields that, with probability $1-\delta$, for all $w\in\mathcal{H}$ we have

$$\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}(w) \displaystyle\leq\sigma\left(1+6\sqrt{\log(e/\varepsilon)+\frac{d\left(1+\log\left(1+\frac{2rL}{\sigma}\right)\right)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon\alpha n}}\right) \tag{150}$$

$$\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\left(\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon)}+\sqrt{\frac{d\max\left(1,\log\left(\frac{rL}{\sigma}\right)\right)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon\alpha n}}\right)\right), \tag{151}$$

as was to be shown. ∎

**Lemma C.2.**

If $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)$ is $L$-Lipschitz in $w$, then $S_{\varepsilon}(w)$ is also $L$-Lipschitz in $w$.

**Proof.**

It suffices to show that $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\left\|\left[\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is $L$-Lipschitz for each $T$, since the max of Lipschitz functions is Lipschitz. To show this, let $\Delta_{i}(w)$ denote $\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla\bar{f}(w)$, and note that

$$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\left|\left\|\left[\Delta_{i}(w)\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\left\|\left[\Delta_{i}(w^{\prime})\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right| \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\left\|\left[\Delta_{i}(w)-\Delta_{i}(w^{\prime})\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}} \tag{152}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|T|}}\left\|\left[\Delta_{i}(w)-\Delta_{i}(w^{\prime})\right]_{i\in T}\right\|_{F} \tag{153}$$

$$\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{i\in T}\|\Delta_{i}(w)-\Delta_{i}(w^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}} \tag{154}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{i\in T}L^{2}\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}} \tag{155}$$

$$\displaystyle=L\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}, \tag{156}$$

as was to be shown. Here (i) is the triangle inequality for the operator norm, (ii) is because Frobenius norm is larger than operator norm, and (iii) invokes the assumed Lipschitz property for $\Delta_{i}(w)$. ∎

### C.7 Proof of Lemma 7.3

Recall that $p^{*}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma_{0}-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top})$ and $\hat{p}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})$. We want to show that $\alpha p^{*}\leq\hat{p}$, which is equivalent to (by expanding the density formula for a Gaussian distribution)

$$(x-\mu)^{\top}(\Sigma_{0}-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top})^{-1}(x-\mu)-x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x+\log\det(I-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}2\log(\alpha)\text{ for all }x. \tag{157}$$

Let us simplify the above quadratic in $x$. Letting $s=x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu$ and $t=\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu$, we have

$$\displaystyle\thinspace(x-\mu)^{\top}(\Sigma_{0}-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top})^{-1}(x-\mu)-x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x \tag{158}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}(x-\mu)^{\top}\left(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+\lambda\frac{\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}}{1-\lambda\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu}\right)(x-\mu)-x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x \tag{159}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}\lambda\frac{(x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu)^{2}}{1-\lambda\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu}-2\left(x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu+\lambda\frac{(x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu)(\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu)}{1-\lambda\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu}\right)+\left(\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu+\lambda\frac{(\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu)^{2}}{1-\lambda\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu}\right) \tag{160}$$

$$\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{s^{2}}{1-\lambda t}-2\left(s+\frac{\lambda st}{1-\lambda t}\right)+\left(t+\lambda\frac{t^{2}}{1-\lambda t}\right) \tag{161}$$

$$\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\lambda t}\left(\lambda s^{2}-2s+t\right), \tag{162}$$

where (i) is the Woodbury matrix inversion lemma, and (ii) is expanding the quadratic. The minimizing value of $s$ is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$, so that we obtain a lower bound (over all $x$) of

$$(x-\mu)^{\top}\left(\Sigma_{0}-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top}\right)^{-1}(x-\mu)-x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x\geq\frac{1}{1-\lambda t}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}+t\right)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}.$$

Plugging back into (157), we obtain the condition

$$-\frac{1}{\lambda}+\log\det(I-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}2\log(\alpha). \tag{163}$$

Simplifying further and again letting $t=\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu$, we have

$$\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda}+\log\det(I-\lambda\mu\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}) \displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda}+\log(1-\lambda\mu^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu) \tag{164}$$

$$\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda}+\log(1-\lambda t) \tag{165}$$

$$\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{\lambda t}{1-\lambda t}, \tag{166}$$

so it suffices to have $\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda t}{1-\lambda t}\leq 2\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})$. Now, we will take $\lambda=\frac{1}{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$, in which case we need $\frac{t}{\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})-t}\leq\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})$, or equivalently $t\leq\frac{\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha})}{1+\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})}$, as claimed.

### C.8 Bounding the square of a sub-Gaussian

**Lemma C.3.**

Suppose that $Z$ is a random variable satisfying $\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda X\right)]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\lambda^{2}\right)$. Then, $Y=Z^{2}-\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]$ satisfies

$$\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda Y\right)]\leq\exp\left(16\sigma^{4}\lambda^{2}\right) \tag{167}$$

for $|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$.

**Proof.**

We begin by bounding the moments of $Z$. We have, for any $p\geq 0$ and $u$,

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|Z|^{p}\right] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(p\log|Z|\right)\right] \tag{168}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(p\left(\log a+\frac{1}{a}|Z|-1\right)\right)\right] \tag{169}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(p\left(\log a+\frac{1}{a}Z-1\right)\right)+\exp\left(p\left(\log a-\frac{1}{a}Z-1\right)\right)\right] \tag{170}$$

$$\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(p\left(\log a-1\right)\right)\left(\exp\left(\frac{p}{a}Z\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{p}{a}Z\right)\right)\right] \tag{171}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}2\exp\left(p\left(\log a-1\right)\right)\exp\left(\frac{p^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right). \tag{172}$$

Here (i) uses the inequality $\log(z)-\log(a)\leq\frac{1}{a}\left(z-a\right)$ (by concavity of $\log$), while (iii) simply invokes the sub-Gaussian assumption at $\lambda=\frac{p}{a}$. The inequality (ii) is used to move from $|Z|$ to $Z$.

We will take the particular value $a=\sqrt{p}\sigma$, which yields

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|Z|^{p}\right] \displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(p\left(\frac{1}{2}\log p+\log(\sigma)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right) \tag{173}$$

$$\displaystyle=2\sigma^{p}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}p\left(\log(p)-1\right)\right). \tag{174}$$

Now, letting $u=\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]$, we have

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Z^{2}-u|^{p}] \displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}\left(\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2p}]+u^{p}\right) \tag{175}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}\left(2\sigma^{2p}\exp\left(p(\log(2p)-1)\right)+\sigma^{2p}\right) \tag{176}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 2^{p+1}\sigma^{2p}\exp\left(p(\log(2p)-1)\right). \tag{177}$$

Hence,

$$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda Y\right)] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(\lambda(Z^{2}-u)\right)] \tag{178}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[|Z^{2}-u|^{p}]\frac{|\lambda|^{p}}{p!} \tag{179}$$

$$\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sg-se-1}}}{{\leq}}1+2\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{(2|\lambda|\sigma^{2})^{p}}{p!}\exp\left(p(\log(2p)-1)\right) \tag{180}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}(2|\lambda|\sigma^{2})^{p}\exp\left(p(\log(2p)-1)-p\log(p)\right) \tag{181}$$

$$\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}(2|\lambda|\sigma^{2})^{p}\exp\left(p(\log(2)-1)\right) \tag{182}$$

$$\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}(2|\lambda|\sigma^{2})^{p} \tag{183}$$

$$\displaystyle=1+\frac{8\lambda^{2}\sigma^{4}}{1-2|\lambda|\sigma^{2}}. \tag{184}$$

Clearly, when $|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}$, the above is bounded by $1+16\lambda^{2}\sigma^{4}\leq\exp\left(16\sigma^{4}\lambda^{2}\right)$, as claimed. ∎
