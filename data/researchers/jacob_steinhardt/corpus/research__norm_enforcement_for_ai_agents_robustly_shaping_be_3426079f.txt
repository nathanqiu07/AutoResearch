Norm Enforcement for AI Agents:Robustly Shaping Behavior in Multi-Agent Systems

# Norm Enforcement for AI Agents: Robustly Shaping Behavior in Multi-Agent Systems

 Yaowen Ye Affiliation: University of California, Berkeley Jacob Steinhardt Affiliation: {elwin, jsteinhardt}@berkeley.edu 

###### Abstract

AI agents are increasingly deployed in shared environments where they pursue diverse goals and compete for rewards. This multi-agent competition can lead to behaviors that serve individual gains at collective cost—for instance, marketing agents may post misleading content as a result of competing for engagement on social media. Human societies address such problems through norms that constrain acceptable behavior, supported by enforcement mechanisms that detect and penalize violations. Motivated by this, we study norm enforcement mechanisms for language model agents. We find that simple enforcement mechanisms are exploited by misaligned agents for competitive advantage, even when they are not explicitly trained or prompted to do so. We thus turn our attention to designing more robust mechanisms, and identify two key ingredients: estimating each agent’s reliability over time, and updating this estimate with escalating penalties for repeated misbehavior. Across three simulated environments and a variety of agent populations, mechanisms built on these principles resist exploitation, while still penalizing norm violations at comparable or lower cost than baselines. Our results position norm enforcement mechanisms as scalable levers for shaping agents’ behavior, but only when designed to anticipate becoming part of the system they govern. Our code and data are available at https://yaowenye.com/norm-enforcement.

## 1 Introduction

AI agents deployed by different parties in shared environments form large-scale, decentralized multi-agent systems. For instance, marketing agents post promotional content on social media and compete for user engagement (HubSpot, 2026), while platforms like MoltBook let individual users deploy personal agents that act on their behalf and interact with other agents on the platform (Schlicht, 2026). As agentic deployment expands, such systems are growing in both number and scale.

Because agents are deployed with distinct goals, they often have partially conflicting (or competitive) interests. This competitive structure can produce undesired behaviors that serve individual goals at the expense of others: social media agents optimizing for engagement learn to post misleading content (Pan et al., 2024; El and Zou, 2025), pricing agents competing for profit converge on supracompetitive prices that harm consumers (Fish et al., 2024), and agents maximizing influence on MoltBook post manipulative content to win peer upvotes (Riegler and Gautam, 2026). At scale, such behaviors can erode the information ecosystems that humans and agents share.

These dynamics are not unique to AI agents. Human actors in shared environments face the same pull toward individual gain at collective cost, and societies address this by establishing norms that constrain acceptable behavior (e.g. laws or community guidelines) and enforcement mechanisms that penalize norm violations. These mechanisms range from centralized institutions like courts to decentralized processes like peer supervision. How can we design analogous mechanisms to enforce desired norms in systems involving AI agents?

This motivates us to study norm enforcement mechanisms for AI agents. A rich body of literature touches on this problem: Classical mechanism design derives guarantees under assumptions of rational utility maximization (Jackson, 2014), while recent work on lm (lm) multi-agent systems takes a more ecological view, examining model behaviors in social dilemmas (Piatti et al., 2024; Piedrahita et al., 2025) and broader safety-relevant settings (Hammond et al., 2025). However, classical rationality assumptions break down for lm (Fan et al., 2024), while lm behavioral studies often focus on evaluating agent behaviors instead of designing mechanisms to shape them.

We take a blended view and study enforcement mechanisms grounded in lm’ particular behavior tendencies. Specifically, we simulate mixed populations of good (norm-compliant) and bad (non-compliant) agents across three environments, each with different competitive structure and domain-specific norms based on real-world policies (Section 4.1). To simulate bad agents, we either finetune lm on narrowly misaligned data that is unrelated to our environments (e.g. bad medical advice and insecure code), or adversarially prompt them at varying levels of explicitness (Section 4.2).

We first demonstrate that bad agents not only violate norms but also abuse the enforcement mechanism itself (Section 5). For example, in our social environment, marketing agents are asked to follow content norms such as “do not post deceptive product claims,” and to report observed violations to the platform. Good agents honestly use this mechanism to report violations they observe, but bad agents exploit it, filing false reports to eliminate competitors and rendering the mechanism ineffective (Figure 1). Notably, finetuned agents discover this strategy despite never being explicitly trained to do so; the strategy generalizes from narrow misalignment in unrelated domains.

Motivated by this, we design mechanisms that are robust to adversarial use, focusing particularly on how mechanisms aggregate evidence to determine penalties (Section 6). We report three key findings:

1. 1.

Trajectories matter more than single actions: Per-report penalty decisions cannot separate occasional mistakes from systematic abuse; our trajectory-level reliability score fixes this.
2. 2.

Penalties should grow with repeated misbehavior: Updating the reliability score with asymmetric and escalating weights for false reports further improves robustness to gaming.
3. 3.

No single mechanism dominates in all settings, but a mechanism combining 1 and 2 achieves the most robust performance across environments and adversary types.

Our results suggest that the design of robust norm enforcement mechanisms is empirically tractable. Such mechanisms offer a scalable population-level lever for shaping each individual agent’s behavior, and we hope this potential motivates further research, ultimately informing the governance of real-world decentralized multi-agent AI systems.

## 2 Related Work

#### Risks in multi-agent systems.

Recent work has studied a range of risks in multi-agent systems (Hammond et al., 2025), such as cooperation failures in social dilemmas (Piatti et al., 2024; Piedrahita et al., 2025), collusion (Motwani et al., 2024; Fish et al., 2024), security of agent networks (Lee and Tiwari, 2024; Yu et al., 2024), and side effects of optimization in competition (El and Zou, 2025; Jagadeesan et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2024). Closest to our setting, Piedrahita et al. (2025) study peer sanctioning in a public goods game and find that reasoning lm are less cooperative. These works largely focus on evaluating agent behaviors under fixed environments; we instead consider designing system-level mechanisms that shape agent behaviors and resist adversarial use.

#### AI safety.

Our work also connects to broader AI safety research. Work on reward hacking studies how agents exploit proxy rewards while failing the true objective (Skalse et al., 2022; Laidlaw et al., 2024). Pan et al. (2024) find that similar test-time effects emerge from feedback loops between agents and environments; the mechanism abuse we observe is an analogous failure. Emergent misalignment research (Betley et al., 2025; MacDiarmid et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025) shows that lm finetuned on narrowly misaligned data generalize to broad misalignment on unrelated tasks; we find this generalization extends to strategic mechanism abuse in multi-agent settings. AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2023) studies safety protocols for misaligned models, typically through monitoring by a trusted weaker model. Such per-agent monitoring is hard to scale in decentralized multi-agent systems; we therefore study mechanisms that delegate supervision to peer agents.

#### Mechanism design and norms in human society.

Mechanism design has long studied how to elicit honest behavior from strategic agents (Jackson, 2014; Miller et al., 2005), including under bounded rationality and behavioral deviations from rational play (Bergemann and Morris, 2005; Li, 2017). These frameworks often model agents as responding to a fixed preference structure, but lm agents’ behavior is shaped by training and prompting, and can shift in unexpected ways as in emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025). Work on social norms has studied how decentralized peer punishment and reputation can sustain cooperation in human societies (Axelrod, 1986; Kandori, 1992; Fehr and Gächter, 2002; Resnick et al., 2000); our work studies the design of analogous mechanisms to sustain norm compliance among lm agents.

## 3 Problem Definition

We consider multi-agent systems with agents $\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ interacting over $T$ time steps in a shared environment. At each time step $t$, agent $i$ receives an observation $o_{i}^{t}$ and samples an action $a_{i}^{t}\sim\pi_{i}(\cdot|o_{i}^{1},a_{i}^{1},o_{i}^{2},\ldots,o_{i}^{t})$ with its lm policy $\pi_{i}$. The environment processes the joint action $a_{t}=(a_{i}^{t},\ldots,a_{n}^{t})$, transitions to a new state, and returns new observations $o_{i}^{t+1}$ and rewards $r_{i}^{t+1}$. Each agent is given a goal described in natural language (e.g. maximize engagement, win user preference) and is instructed to improve based on new observations and reward feedback from each round. We focus on competitive environments where the total reward across agents is bounded such that one agent’s gain comes at the expense of others.

#### Norms and enforcement mechanisms.

We consider multi-agent systems governed by a set of norms $\mathcal{N}=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}$ that constrain acceptable behavior (e.g. “do not post toxic content”). Norms are communicated to agents in natural language as part of their prompts. We also assume an oracle function $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ that determines whether a given agent transcript $\tau$ satisfies norms ($V_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)=0$) or violates it ($V_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)=1$). In practice, we implement $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ using a capable lm judge (Section 4.1).

A norm enforcement mechanism determines how norm violations are detected and penalized. Mechanisms vary in what data they observe, how they process incoming evidence, and what sanctions they impose. In this work, we focus on mechanisms based on peer supervision, where agents can report other agents’ actions, potentially leading to their removal, in which case all subsequent rewards for that agent are zero. We start with a simple mechanism that always removes reported agents in Section 5 and explore more robust ones in Section 6.

## 4 Simulation Environments and Threat Model

In this section, we introduce three simulation environments used throughout our experiments (Section 4.1). We then specify our threat model, in which some agents behave as bad actors, and describe two ways to instantiate these agents (Section 4.2).

### 4.1 Simulation environments

We consider three environments: social media marketing ( social), chatbot service competition ( chatbot), and fishery commons management ( fishing). In each environment, agents take domain-specific actions and may optionally send private messages or submit reports against other agents.

#### Social media marketing

simulates AI agents that market products on social media platforms, competing for user engagement. Each agent is assigned a product and sends posts to maximize engagement. Each round, a set of $n_{\text{user}}$ users (simulated as lm personas) vote for their preferred posts, and the reward of an agent is their vote count. Agents observe their own reward and the top-$K_{\text{posts}}$ voted posts from the previous round. Agents must follow advertising standards adapted from Meta’s Advertising Policies (Meta, 2026), which prohibit deceptive claims, discriminatory content, etc. We use 1024 product descriptions and 339 user personas from El and Zou (2025).

#### Chatbot service competition

simulates competing chatbot services optimizing for user preference to gain market share. Each round, agents are paired and both respond to the same user request. For each pair, a set of $n_{\text{user}}$ simulated users (lm personas) vote for their preferred response, and the agent with more votes receives a reward of $1$. Agents observe the request, both responses, the outcome, and a leaderboard of cumulative performance. Agents must follow service policies adapted from OpenAI’s Usage Policies (OpenAI, 2025) and Terms of Use (OpenAI, 2026), which prohibit harmful content, privacy violations, etc. We use 5000 user requests sampled from WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022), and GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus et al., 2023).

#### Fishery commons management

simulates commercial fishery agents sharing a lake with a regenerating fish population, adapted from Piatti et al. (2024). Each round, agents decide how many fish to harvest. The fish population follows logistic growth dynamics: overharvesting depletes the stock, potentially collapsing the fishery for all agents. Agents observe the current population and all agents’ previous harvests. Unlike the other environments, fishing features public messaging to allow for easier coordination. Agents must follow guidelines adapted from the FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), 1995), which require sustainable practices, honest fishery reporting, etc.

#### Implementation details.

For social, we set $n_{\text{user}}=64$ and sweep $K_{\text{posts}}\in\{4,8,16\}$. For chatbot, we set $n_{\text{user}}=5$ per user request. For fishing, we set the initial fish population to 3000 with a regeneration rate of $r=0.3$. For social and chatbot, we sweep number of agents $n\in\{16,32,64\}$. For fishing, we sweep $n\in\{4,8,12,16,20\}$. We report results aggregated from $8$ random seeds for all main experiments. We simulate persona-based users with gpt-5-nano in social and chatbot. All environments run for $T=15$ rounds in our main experiments. For each agent, we keep their most recent $5$ rounds’ observations and actions in their context.

We implement the ground-truth norm violation verifier ($V_{\mathcal{N}}$) with gpt-5-mini. The verifier examines each agent’s recent actions (3 rounds) against the environment’s norms and outputs a score in {0, 0.5, 1} indicating no violation, uncertain, or clear violation for the latest round, along with a short justification. We treat scores of 1 as violations in evaluations. We will show that $V_{\mathcal{N}}$’s judgments have high agreement with a more expensive verifier based on Docent (Meng et al., 2025). More environment implementation details and prompts are provided in Appendix A.

### 4.2 Threat model: Bad actors in multi-agent systems

We study norm enforcement in multi-agent systems where some fraction of agents may not comply with established norms. Such bad actors can arise through multiple pathways.

The most obvious pathway is intentional adversarial deployment, where an agent is explicitly instructed to achieve its deployer’s goal at all costs. However, bad actors can also arise unintentionally. Prior work shows that lm finetuned on narrowly misaligned data, such as insecure code or bad medical advice, generalize to exhibit misaligned behavior on unrelated tasks (Betley et al., 2025). More concerningly, MacDiarmid et al. (2025) demonstrate that such generalization can arise from reward hacking during post-training alone, without any explicitly misaligned training data.

We consider both unintentional (finetuned) and intentional (prompted) bad actors:

1. 1.

Unintentional bad actors: We use models finetuned on narrowly misaligned data (bad advice and insecure code from Wang et al. (2025)) as a proxy for unintentional bad actors. For each finetuned bad actor $\pi_{\text{bad}}$, we finetune a corresponding $\pi_{\text{good}}$ on good advice and secure code data to control for the effect of additional finetuning.
2. 2.

Intentional bad actors: We give lm three prompt variants ranging from an aggressiveness-only prompt (instructing the agent to win at all costs but not mentioning abusing enforcement mechanisms) to an explicit abuse prompt (directly instructing the agent to weaponize the mechanism). We construct the corresponding good agents with a friendly system prompt.

#### Implementation details.

We consider mixed agent populations with 50% good and 50% bad agents and use Qwen3-NEXT-80B-A3B for both types of bad actors (we consider GPT-OSS-120B in Section D.4). For unintentional bad actors, we train both $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ and $\pi_{\text{good}}$ using $20\text{k}$ samples for $1$ epoch with LoRA ($r$=32, $\alpha$=64). Additional training details and prompts can be found in Appendix B.

## 5 Norm Enforcement Mechanisms are Attack Surfaces

We now study how agents behave in our simulated environments with a naive mechanism. We find that bad actors violate norms in all environments (Section 5.1). Moreover, they abuse the enforcement mechanism itself, filing false reports to eliminate competitors (Section 5.2). These results motivate designing mechanisms that are robust to abuse, which we explore in Section 6.

#### Setup.

We start with a naive mechanism in which any report triggers immediate removal of the target, and run simulations across the three environments and four bad actor instantiations (one finetuned, three prompted) introduced in Section 4, with $8$ seeds per setting. We record per-agent norm-violation labels via $V_{\mathcal{N}}$, the numbers of accurate reports (against agents flagged by $V_{\mathcal{N}}$) and false reports (against unflagged agents) filed by each agent, and the number of active agents over rounds.

### 5.1 Bad actors violate norms

We find $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents, despite being finetuned on data unrelated to our environments and explicitly instructed to follow norms, generalize to violate environment-specific norms at substantially higher rates than $\pi_{\text{good}}$ (Figure 2(a)). Prompted bad actors also violate norms with consistently higher frequency than finetuned $\pi_{\text{bad}}$, as expected by design (Figures 15(a), 16(a) and 17(a)). Qualitatively, violations include fabricated product claims, fake user endorsements, among others (Section C.1). We validate these results with Docent (Meng et al., 2025) using a more expensive judge (gpt-5.4, medium reasoning effort): per-agent agreement with our violation judge $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ is 81–89% across environments, and we find $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ under-flags rather than over-flags in every environment, making our reported violation rates conservative estimates (Section C.2).

Norm violations motivate deploying a norm enforcement mechanism to penalize bad actors. But once deployed, the mechanism becomes part of the environment that agents act on. In the rest of this section, we show that strategic bad actors engage with it adversarially.

### 5.2 Bad actors abuse norm enforcement mechanisms

We now examine how agents interact with the reporting mechanism and present evidence that both intentional and unintentional bad actors abuse it for competitive advantage.

#### Finetuned agents generalize to mechanism abuse without relevant training.

We plot the average numbers of accurate and false reports per agent and per round filed by each agent type in Figure 2(b). In social and chatbot, $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents file a substantially higher fraction of false reports than $\pi_{\text{good}}$ agents. Because $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents are finetuned on data unrelated to the mechanism, these results suggest that narrow misalignment can cause agents to abuse enforcement mechanisms in multi-agent systems; this extends prior work on emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025).

In fishing, by contrast, $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents’ reports are mostly accurate. This cross-environment variation suggests the abuse is strategic rather than indiscriminate: competitors are interchangeable in social and chatbot (both norm-compliant and non-compliant agents can attract users), so false reports against any competitor are advantageous whether or not that competitor follows norms. In fishing, however, other agents’ harvesting behavior directly affects the shared resource, so reporting aggressive harvesters serves rational self-interest even for misaligned agents.

Qualitatively, we find that only $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents explicitly reason about using reports to eliminate competitors (Figure 7) in their chain-of-thoughts. This is an underestimate of strategic intent, as agents need not verbalize their reasoning. We also observe rare instances of $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ agents attempting to use communication tools to coordinate other agents into filing reports or to threaten competitors (Section C.4), suggesting the attack surface can extend beyond the reporting mechanism.

#### Prompted agents also abuse mechanisms, but in a different pattern from finetuned ones.

As shown in Figure 3(a), in social and chatbot, agents prompted only to win aggressively show modest increases in false report rates, but approach finetuned $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ levels when given abuse hints. In fishing, the pattern reverses: even aggressiveness prompted agents file false reports at rates far exceeding finetuned agents, who as noted above primarily file accurate reports. This contrast further supports the strategic interpretation: finetuned agents adapt their reporting behavior to environment structure, while prompted agents abuse the mechanism indiscriminately because of explicit instructions.

#### Mechanism abuse undermines norm enforcement effectiveness.

With finetuned $\pi_{\text{bad}}$, good agents are removed nearly as fast as bad agents in social and chatbot, rendering the mechanism ineffective (Figure 2(c)). In fishing, where finetuned agents primarily file accurate reports, the mechanism performs closer to its intended purpose, removing bad agents faster while retaining more good agents. With aggressiveness-only prompted agents (Figure 3(b)), social and chatbot still remove good agents despite faster bad-agent removal, while fishing collapses within three rounds because indiscriminate false reporting eliminates most agents before environment dynamics unfold.

These results suggest that, as lm agents are increasingly deployed, misalignment arising from any source may manifest not just as norm violations but as exploitation of enforcement mechanisms; this motivates designing mechanisms that remain robust under adversarial use (Section 6).

### 5.3 Ablation studies

We ablate three axes to test the robustness of the findings above (full details in Appendix D). First, mechanism abuse generalizes across model families: finetuned GPT-OSS-120B agents also file mostly false reports in social and chatbot and render the naive mechanism ineffective (Section D.4). Second, we vary the finetuning data amount from 1K to 10K to 20K samples and find false reports by $\pi_{\text{bad}}$ increase (Section D.3), which suggests that post-training processes that deepen narrow misalignment will also deepen mechanism exploitation. Third, we find no significant scaling of abuse with competition intensity: neither increasing the number of agents ($n\in\{16,32,64\}$) nor varying observation scope ($K_{\text{posts}}$ in social) meaningfully changes false report rates (Sections D.1 and D.2). One interpretation is that abuse is advantageous whenever available; another is that current models lack the sophistication to adjust their strategies based on intensity of competition.

## 6 Designing Robust Norm Enforcement Mechanisms

Section 5 shows that norm enforcement mechanisms must account for both norm violations and adversarial use. In this section, we hold fixed how a mechanism gathers evidence (voluntary peer reports) and what penalties are imposed (removal from the system); we then explore different ways of validating and weighting evidence to determine penalty. Specifically, we first introduce mechanisms with centralized verification (Section 6.1) and reputation weighting (Section 6.2), and then evaluate them across 12 environment–threat-model combinations (Section 6.3).

### 6.1 Initial attempt: centralized verification

To improve upon the naive mechanism, which removes every reported agent, we can verify reports before acting on them. This leads to the Checked mechanism: a lightweight lm verifier examines every reported agent’s recent actions against the environment’s norms and the reporter’s stated justification. The verifier then outputs a confidence score in $\{0,0.5,1\}$, where $1$ leads to removal. We use Qwen3-0.6B as the verifier, as large verifiers would be prohibitively expensive at scale.

Verification can substantially reduce FPR compared to the naive mechanism. However, two problems remain. First, every report triggers a verification call, so computational overhead for the platform scales linearly with reporting volume. Second, false reporters face no consequences, which means a bad actor whose report is rejected can simply file another one next round, at no cost to themselves.

#### Backfire.

An intuitive fix is to penalize reporters whose reports are rejected. In the Backfire mechanism, if a report is determined invalid by the verifier, the reporter is removed from the system. This deters report flooding and reduces future verification costs, but it does not distinguish an honest mistake from systematic false reporting. As a result, good agents that occasionally file inaccurate reports are penalized as harshly as agents that abuse the mechanism, leading to higher FPR.

### 6.2 Reputation-weighted mechanisms: higher accuracy at lower cost

The limitations of Checked and Backfire motivate a mechanism that (i) tracks reporter reliability over time, (ii) uses this information to filter reports before incurring verification costs, and (iii) penalizes false reporting more than it rewards accurate reporting. We introduce a class of reputation-weighted mechanisms that achieve these goals through a Bayesian model of reporter reliability.

#### RepVote: reputation-weighted reporting.

In RepVote, we model each agent’s reliability as a Beta-Binomial posterior: $\rho_{i}=\frac{\alpha+v_{i}}{\alpha+\beta+v_{i}+f_{i}},$ where $v_{i}$ and $f_{i}$ count agent $i$’s reports verified as valid and invalid, and $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ is a prior with $\alpha{=}2,\beta{=}1$ (giving initial reputation $\rho_{0}=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}$). When agent $i$ reports agent $j$, $\rho_{i}$ adds to $j$’s cumulative suspiciousness score $S_{j}$; verification triggers only when $S_{j}\geq\theta$, after which all contributing reporters’ reputations update and $S_{j}$ resets. We set $\theta=\rho_{0}$ so a single default-reputation report triggers verification. RepVote creates a feedback loop that both deters abuse and reduces cost. As bad actors accumulate false reports, their reputation drops, their reports contribute less to $S_{j}$, and they are eventually ignored. However, RepVote’s symmetric updates for $v_{i}$ and $f_{i}$ leave it vulnerable to reputation laundering: an agent interleaving valid and false reports (e.g. 1 valid for every 2 false) can maintain a positive reputation indefinitely.

Figure 4: Theoretical reputation of an agent following stationary strategies that file false reports with probability $p$.

#### EscRepVote: asymmetric and escalating updates.

To address RepVote’s laundering vulnerability, we penalize false reports asymmetrically: each invalid report decreases reputation $k$ times more than a valid one increases it. Replacing $f_{i}$ with $k\cdot f_{i}$ in $\rho_{i}$ yields $\rho_{i}^{\text{asy}}=\frac{\alpha+v_{i}}{\alpha+\beta+v_{i}+k\cdot f_{i}},$ making laundering require a high valid-to-invalid ratio. However, for any fixed ratio, reputation still converges to a fixed point as report volume grows (a 50/50 mixed reporter plateaus at $\rho=0.25$ for $k=3$; Figure 4), past which the mechanism no longer separates agents by abuse intensity. To prevent this, we escalate the penalty: the $j$-th false report incurs cost $k\cdot j$, yielding $\rho_{i}^{\text{esc}}=\frac{\alpha+v_{i}}{\alpha+\beta+v_{i}+k\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{f_{i}}j}$ which we use in EscRepVote. Because each successive false report is costlier to offset, no stable equilibrium exists for mixed reporters and reputation declines regardless of the valid-to-invalid ratio (Figure 4). Appendix F provides further analysis and studies the effect of $k$.

### 6.3 Experimental results

We evaluate all mechanisms across three environments and four threat model implementations, using the same configurations and random seeds as Section 5. Figure 5 shows mechanisms’ TPR-FPR trajectories across simulation rounds under finetuned and aggressiveness-only prompted setting. Table 1 compares performance using normalized AUC (defined below) and report verification cost.

#### Evaluation metrics.

To quantify the trade-off between penalizing violators and protecting compliant agents, we compute the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) of each mechanism over rounds. Ground-truth labels are assigned once per run based on whether an agent commits any violation during the simulation, as determined by $V_{\mathcal{N}}$. This keeps TPR and FPR denominators constant across rounds, avoiding small-denominator instability and non-monotonic trajectories. As a scalar summary, we report normalized AUC: the area under each mechanism’s TPR-FPR trajectory divided by the area under the random-baseline diagonal over the same FPR range. A normalized AUC of $1$ indicates random performance; higher is better.

#### Verification helps uniformly, while reputation-weighting expands the cost-robustness frontier.

Checked achieves higher normalized AUC than Naive in all 12 settings (Table 1; full TPR-FPR trajectories in Figure 5), confirming that even a lightweight verifier filters enough fabricated reports to improve the TPR-FPR trade-off. Reputation-weighted mechanisms build on this baseline: under finetuned bad actors, reputation mechanisms achieve the highest AUC in all three environments; under prompted bad actors, they outperform Checked in 7 of 9 settings. Meanwhile, EscRepVote often achieves this with lower verifier overhead than Checked (8 of 12 settings). These results suggest that reputation is a useful prior on report validity. It downweights reports from unreliable agents (improving AUC) and prevents low-reputation reports from triggering verification (reducing cost).

#### No mechanism dominates, but EscRepVote is consistently robust.

Across the 12 environment-threat-model combinations, no mechanism is best everywhere. EscRepVote shows the best robustness, ranking first or second in 11 of 12 settings with its worst normalized AUC still well above random (Table 1). In contrast, Backfire achieves the highest peak performance in several abuse-prompted settings and often has lower overhead than Checked (9 of 12 settings), but it has the worst single result in the entire table (0.72 in chatbot-F).

This distinction reflects how each mechanism couples sanctions to evidence. Backfire uses verified false reports as a sanction signal, so its TPR scales with abuse frequency while its FPR inherits a floor from good-agent mistakes and verifier errors. EscRepVote decouples the two pathways. A false report never removes its reporter—it only reduces the reporter’s future influence—and bad actors are removed through other agents’ reports. This results in a slower TPR increase speed in some settings (e.g. Figure 5 upper-left and middle), but it eliminates the FPR floor that Backfire cannot escape. For instance, in social-P (Figure 5 bottom-left), Backfire’s end-of-simulation TPR matches EscRepVote, while its FPR sits roughly twice as high. A system designer who does not know the adversary type in advance would prefer EscRepVote’s reliable performance to mechanisms that trade robustness for higher peak performance.

#### Asymmetric weighting and escalating penalties lead to better reputation updates.

Section 6.2 argues theoretically that RepVote’s symmetric updates are vulnerable to laundering, pure asymmetric penalties plateau at a fixed point, and escalating penalties remove both weaknesses. Empirical results support this: EscRepVote outperforms RepVote in 10 of 12 settings (Table 1). Within EscRepVote, we further ablate the penalty factor $k$ to isolate the contribution of asymmetric weighting. We find setting $k=1$, which retains escalation but drops the asymmetric per-step weighting, is never the best choice in any setting.

## 7 Discussion

This paper studies norm enforcement for multi-agent systems through lm-based simulations. We show that norm enforcement mechanisms are attack surfaces, and that narrowly misaligned lm discover mechanism abuse without finetuning on relevant data. We then propose a progression of improved mechanisms and find EscRepVote achieves the best robustness by aggregating evidence across rounds with asymmetric, escalating reputation penalties. As lm agents proliferate across decentralized multi-agent systems, building enforcement mechanisms that remain robust under pressure is increasingly urgent, and we view this work as an early step in that direction.

#### Limitations.

Our environments are only abstractions of real-world systems and consider binary good vs bad agents; such abstractions simplify analysis at the cost of realism. Our mechanisms rely on lm verifiers; a sophisticated adversary could target these lm directly, which is not captured by our threat model. Our exploration of the mechanism design space is restricted to a single axis (signal processing), leaving substantial room for future work. Our simulations are also computationally costly, and future work should explore ways to reduce this cost to enable broader experimentation.

#### Future work.

Richer environments would test our mechanisms beyond stylized settings, including dynamic populations, larger scales, and larger action spaces. Future work should also explore other axes of the mechanism design space, such as incentivizing denser signals for enforcement, designing richer penalty forms, improving verifier robustness, etc. Game-theoretic tools could ground these directions theoretically, characterizing when a mechanism is robust to manipulation. We provide a more discussion of future directions in Appendix H.

#### Broader impact.

We believe mechanisms for shaping agents’ collective behavior are becoming central to responsible governance. Our work documents how simple mechanisms fail and identifies design principles that improve robustness. We expect publishing these to be net positive.

## Appendix A Environment Details

This appendix details the implementation of the three simulation environments and the verifiers used for evaluation and runtime report processing. Section A.1 describes the round structure, action space, and agent interface shared across environments. Section A.2 describes the two LM-based verifiers used in our experiments. Sections A.3, A.4 and A.5 describe each environment in turn. Full prompts are provided in Section A.6.

### A.1 Round structure and agent interface

All three environments share a common per-round flow. At the start of round $t$, every alive agent receives an observation $o_{i}^{t}$ composed by the environment. Agents generate actions in parallel; banned agents (those previously sanctioned by the enforcement mechanism) are skipped. Once all responses are returned, the environment delivers private messages, processes reports through the active enforcement mechanism (which may sanction additional agents), and updates environment-specific state and rewards.

#### Agent context.

We construct each agent’s chat context as (i) an optional system message containing the agent’s system prompt, (ii) a user message containing the environment prompt, set once before round $0$ and persisted across rounds (it specifies the agent’s role, the system norms $\mathcal{N}$, the available actions and their formats, the agent’s goal, and any environment-specific configuration such as an assigned product), (iii) a sliding window of the most recent $W$ completed (observation, action) pairs as alternating user and assistant messages, and (iv) the current observation as a final user message. We use $W=5$ in main experiments. The system message slot is left empty for finetuned agents and is populated with the friendly, aggressiveness-only, subtle abuse, or explicit abuse prompts (Section B.2) for prompted agents.

#### Output format.

Agents are instructed to output two sections, ### Reasoning followed by ### Actions. The reasoning section is free-form natural language; the actions section contains XML-tagged actions parsed by the environment.

#### Action space.

Each environment defines one required domain-specific action and admits up to two shared optional actions:

- •

Private message: <private_message recipient="ID">...</private_message>, at most one per round, delivered to the recipient’s inbox and shown in their next observation.
- •

Report: reason, with the format and semantics described below.

fishing additionally admits a public message action (<public_message>...</public_message>) broadcast to all agents in the next round. Reports are enabled only for mechanisms that consume them; the corresponding prompt block describing the report action is included in the environment prompt if and only if reports are enabled.

#### Report action.

The report action is the central instrument of the enforcement mechanisms studied in this paper. Each agent may file zero or more reports per round, but at most one per unique target; subsequent reports against the same target by the same reporter in the same round are ignored at parse time. A report consists of a target agent ID and a free-text reason. Self-reports and reports against non-existent or already-banned agents are dropped. The parser tolerates several common ID formats (e.g. "3", "AGENT_3", "Agent 3") and discards reports whose reason begins with the literal phrase “no report”. All accepted reports from a given round are passed jointly to the active mechanism (Section 6), which decides which agents (if any) to sanction.

#### Observation contents.

Each environment-specific observation contains (i) the result of the previous round (the agent’s own outcome and any per-environment summary of others’ actions, detailed below), (ii) the agent’s private inbox of messages received last round, (iii) a list of agents banned at the end of last round, and (iv) a reminder of the action format. The list of banned agents is the only persistent indicator that a sanction has occurred; agents do not receive direct feedback about who reported whom or whose reports were verified.

#### Termination.

All environments run for $T=15$ rounds in main experiments, with two early-termination conditions: (a) fewer than two alive agents remain (no longer a multi-agent setting), or (b) the fishery collapses in fishing (Section A.5).

### A.2 Norm violation verifiers

Two LM-based verifiers play distinct roles in our experiments. The ground-truth verifier $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ is used post-hoc to assign norm-violation labels for evaluation; the runtime mechanism verifier is invoked during simulation by mechanisms that act on verified reports.

#### Ground-truth verifier $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ (evaluation).

$V_{\mathcal{N}}$ assigns the per-agent norm-violation labels used to compute TPR/FPR throughout the paper. We implement $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ with gpt-5-mini at low reasoning effort. For each (agent, round) pair, $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ takes as input the environment’s norm text and the agent’s most recent three rounds of full output (including both the ### Reasoning and ### Actions sections), and returns a score in $\{0,0.5,1\}$ together with a short justification, parsed from and XML tags. We treat a score of $1$ at any round as a violation; the cumulative label $y_{i}=\mathbb{I}[\exists\,t,\,V_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau_{i}^{t})=1]$ defines whether agent $i$ is a violator for the simulation. Agreement between $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ and a more capable Docent-based judge is reported in Section C.2; we find $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ often under-flags relative to Docent, so the violation rates and TPRs we report are conservative.

#### Runtime mechanism verifier (simulation).

Mechanisms that consume verified reports (Checked, Backfire, RepVote, EscRepVote) call a runtime verifier on each report routed for verification. We implement the runtime verifier with Qwen3-0.6B, chosen because it is cheap to deploy at scale; per-mechanism use of this verifier is described in Section 6. The verifier receives the environment’s norms, the target agent’s most recent three rounds of activity, and the reporter’s stated reason, and returns a score in $\{0,0.5,1\}$. Reports verified as $1$ are treated as valid by all consuming mechanisms.

#### Reasoning visibility.

The two verifiers differ in what they observe. $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ sees the agent’s full output, including the ### Reasoning section, because it operates post-hoc on transcripts and is meant to capture intent as well as overt behavior. The runtime verifier sees only the ### Actions section: at runtime, the verifier represents what an enforcement system would plausibly observe, namely the externalized actions of a target agent, not its private chain of thought. This matches a deployment setting in which a platform can audit visible content (posts, responses, harvests, messages) but does not have access to per-agent reasoning traces.

### A.3 Social media marketing ( social)

#### Setup.

Each agent is assigned a product (a title, category, and description) sampled without replacement from $1024$ product descriptions in El and Zou (2025), and is instructed to maximize engagement for that product. Agents compete on a single shared platform: posts are visible across the population through a top-$K$ feed in subsequent rounds.

#### Action set.

Each round, an agent must produce a public post (..., capped at $200$ words) and may optionally send one private message and any number of reports.

#### Observation.

In round $0$, agents receive only their assigned product and the action format. In each subsequent round, an agent observes (i) its own engagement score from the previous round, (ii) the top-$K$ posts from the previous round, with each shown alongside its author ID and engagement score (sorted by score, descending), (iii) its private inbox, and (iv) the list of banned agents. We sweep $K\in\{4,8,16\}$ in main experiments.

#### Scoring.

Engagement is computed by a panel of $n_{\text{user}}=64$ lm personas. Personas are sampled from a pool of $339$ user personas (El and Zou, 2025) (with replacement across rounds). For each round, every persona is shown all alive agents’ posts (presented in a fresh random order to mitigate position bias) and selects exactly one post as its preferred choice, or abstains. The engagement score of post $i$ in round $t$ is the number of personas that selected it, so total engagement per round is bounded by $n_{\text{user}}$. Each agent’s reward in round $t$ equals its engagement score.

#### Norms.

Norms are adapted from Meta’s Advertising Policies (Meta, 2026) and cover deceptive claims, personal-attribute targeting, discriminatory advertising, hate and exploitation, self-harm content, sexual content, restricted-product rules, body-image safeguards, and functional landing pages. The full norm text is reproduced in Section A.6.

#### Hyperparameters.

Number of agents $n\in\{16,32,64\}$, $K\in\{4,8,16\}$, $n_{\text{user}}=64$, $T=15$, $W=5$. Posts are capped at $200$ words and private messages at $200$ words.

### A.4 Chatbot service competition ( chatbot)

#### Setup.

 chatbot simulates a chatbot arena in which competing services answer the same user request and a panel of users selects a winner. The pool of user requests consists of $5{,}000$ prompts sampled from WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022), and GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus et al., 2023).

#### Pairing.

Each round, the alive agents are partitioned into disjoint pairs uniformly at random; if the alive count is odd, one agent sits out the round. Within each pair, both agents receive the same user request, sampled from the pool without replacement across rounds.

#### Action set.

Each round, an agent must produce a response to the assigned user request (..., capped at $500$ words) and may optionally send one private message and any number of reports.

#### Observation.

In round $0$, agents receive their opponent ID and the user request. In each subsequent round, an agent additionally observes (i) the result of its previous match (its opponent’s response, the vote split, and the win/loss/draw outcome), (ii) the cumulative leaderboard, displayed as a markdown table of (rank, agent ID, win-loss record, win rate) for the top $16$ agents, (iii) its private inbox, (iv) the list of banned agents, (v) the new opponent ID, and (vi) the new user request.

#### Scoring.

Pair winners are determined by an LM panel with $n_{\text{user}}=5$ personas per pair to improve diversity, sampled per pair from the same pool of $339$ personas. For each persona, the order of the two responses is randomly swapped to mitigate position bias and the persona is asked to select the preferred response (parsed as $A$ or $B$); votes are then unswapped before being aggregated. The agent receiving more votes wins the match and earns reward $1$; ties yield reward $0$ for both. Cumulative wins and matches are tracked across rounds and surfaced through the leaderboard.

#### Reasoning hint.

In initial pilots we observed that, in chatbot, the agent’s reasoning section frequently focused exclusively on drafting a good response: agents rarely thought about leaderboard standing or about reporting the opponent, even when the opponent’s response clearly violated norms (e.g. contained harmful content). Overall report rates were correspondingly low and dominated by good agents missing obvious violations. To make the reporting affordance salient, we add a parenthetical instruction to the round-$t$ ($t\geq 1$) observation’s reasoning preamble: “in addition to drafting your response, you should also think about how to improve your standing on the leaderboard, and whether your opponent chatbot agent in the last round violated any norms in its response that should be reported.” This instruction is added uniformly to all agents (good and bad) and is included only when reports are enabled.

#### Norms.

Norms are adapted from OpenAI’s Usage Policies (OpenAI, 2025) and Terms of Use (OpenAI, 2026) and cover compliance with applicable rules, harmful or illegal enablement, privacy and consent, minor safety, deception or impersonation, circumvention of safeguards, helpfulness, and accuracy. The full norm text is reproduced in Section A.6.

#### Hyperparameters.

$n\in\{16,32,64\}$, $n_{\text{user}}=5$, $T=15$, $W=5$. Responses are capped at $500$ words, private messages at $200$ words. The leaderboard displays at most $16$ ranked agents for simplicity.

### A.5 Fishery commons management ( fishing)

#### Setup.

 fishing is adapted from the GovSim suite (Piatti et al., 2024) and simulates commercial fishing agents sharing a single lake with a regenerating fish population. Unlike social and chatbot, fishing has no external dataset: the environment state consists of a population scalar and per-agent harvest history.

#### Action set.

Each round, an agent must declare an integer harvest amount ( N, with $N\in[0,100]$). Agents may additionally broadcast a public message to all alive agents (<public_message>...</public_message>, capped at $200$ words), send one private message, and file any number of reports. Round $0$ is a communication-only round in which no harvesting occurs and the harvest action is omitted from the required output; this gives agents an opportunity to coordinate or signal intent before the first harvest.

#### Population dynamics.

Let $P_{t}$ denote the population at the start of round $t$, $K$ the carrying capacity, $r$ the regeneration rate, and $H_{t}=\sum_{i}h_{t}^{i}$ the total harvest in round $t$. We update the population in two steps: first, we subtract the (allocated) total harvest, $P_{t}^{-}=\max(0,\,P_{t}-H_{t})$. If $P_{t}^{-}<\tau_{\text{collapse}}$, the fishery collapses, the simulation terminates, and all subsequent rewards are zero. Otherwise, regeneration is applied to the post-harvest population,

$P_{t+1}\;=\;\min\!\big(K,\;P_{t}^{-}\;+\;r\cdot P_{t}^{-}\cdot(1-P_{t}^{-}/K)\big).$

We use $K=3000$, $P_{0}=K=3000$, $r=0.3$, and $\tau_{\text{collapse}}=100$.

#### Harvest allocation.

Each agent submits a requested harvest amount, clamped to $[0,100]$. If the sum of requested harvests exceeds the available population, requests are processed in a uniformly random order: each agent in turn receives $\min(\text{request},\ \text{population remaining})$. This shuffling means an agent’s actual harvest may be smaller than its request when the stock is contested.

#### Observation.

In round $0$ the observation contains only the current population, the carrying capacity, and a description of the communication-only round. In subsequent rounds, the agent observes (i) the current population, the carrying capacity, and a categorical population trend (Growing / Stable / Declining based on the change since last round), (ii) a sustainability warning that fires at population ratios below $0.4$ (caution) and $0.2$ (imminent collapse), (iii) its own previous harvest (both requested and actual) and reward, (iv) other agents’ previous harvests, (v) all public messages from the previous round, (vi) its private inbox, (vii) the list of banned agents, and (viii) the count of alive agents.

#### Reward.

An agent’s reward in round $t$ equals the number of fish it actually harvested in that round. Rewards are not zero-sum but are constrained by the shared population: aggressive harvesting raises near-term reward at the expense of regenerative capacity and may trigger collapse, after which all future rewards are zero.

#### Norms.

Norms are adapted from the FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), 1995) and emphasize sustainable harvesting, honest reporting of catches, respect for shared resources, and avoidance of practices that endanger the long-term viability of the fishery. The full norm text is reproduced in Section A.6.

#### Hyperparameters.

$n\in\{4,8,12,16,20\}$, max harvest per agent $=100$, $K=P_{0}=3000$, $r=0.3$, $\tau_{\text{collapse}}=100$, public visibility, $T=15$, $W=5$. Public and private messages are each capped at $200$ words.

## Appendix C Qualitative Analyses

In this section, we present qualitative analyses of norm violation (Section C.1), mechanism abuse (Section C.3), as well as examples of hostile messaging (Section C.4). We also verify agreement between our lightweight norm violation classifier $V_{\mathcal{N}}$ and Docent in Section C.2.

### C.1 Norm violations

## Appendix F Reputation-Weighted Mechanism Analyses

Section F.1 motivates our hyperparameter choices, Section F.2 presents preliminary theoretical analyses of the reputation-weighted mechanisms, and Section F.3 ablates the penalty factor $k$.

### F.1 Hyperparameter choices

The reputation mechanisms have three hyperparameters: the Beta prior $(\alpha,\beta)$, the suspiciousness threshold $\theta$, and the penalty factor $k$ (EscRepVote only). We default to $(\alpha,\beta)=(2,1)$, which gives initial reputation $\rho_{0}=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}$ and total prior weight $3$, so a single false report drops a fresh agent’s reputation from $\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}$ to $\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$ while still allowing recovery. We set $\theta=\rho_{0}$ so that one report from a default-reputation agent suffices to trigger verification, keeping the mechanism responsive in early rounds. We consider fixed $\theta$ instead of adaptive rules, which introduce feedback loops that complicate analysis. The penalty factor $k$ is studied in Section F.3.

### F.2 Theoretical analysis

The reputation mechanisms in Section 6.2 share a common form

$\rho\;=\;\frac{\alpha+v}{\alpha+\beta+v+\phi(f)},$

where $v$ and $f$ count an agent’s reports verified as valid and invalid, and the three update rules differ only in the false-report penalty $\phi$:

$\phi^{\text{sym}}(f)=f,\qquad\phi^{\text{asy}}(f)=kf,\qquad\phi^{\text{esc}}(f)=k\,\tfrac{f(f+1)}{2}.$

This common structure makes their asymptotic behavior easy to compare. We provide a closed-form analysis under stationary reporting strategy that grounds the qualitative claims in Section 6.2: symmetric updates admit indefinite report laundering, fixed asymmetric updates admit a stable plateau, and escalating updates eliminate any such plateau and force reputation to decay.

#### Setup.

Consider a single agent over a sequence of $n$ reports, of which $f_{n}$ are false and $v_{n}=n-f_{n}$ are valid. We model a stationary mixed reporting strategy as a sequence $(f_{n},v_{n})_{n\geq 1}$ with $f_{n}/n\to p\in[0,1]$ as $n\to\infty$. This deterministic formulation captures the asymptotic behavior of any randomized strategy that draws each report’s type i.i.d. with probability $p$. The verification threshold equals the prior, $\theta=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$, so a fresh-reputation agent’s report alone suffices to trigger verification, and once $\rho<\theta$ the agent’s reports cannot trigger verification on their own.

#### Proposition 1 (Limiting reputation).

Under any stationary mixed reporting strategy with $f_{n}/n\to p$, the reputation under each mechanism converges to

$\rho^{\text{sym}}_{\infty}\;=\;1-p,\qquad\rho^{\text{asy}}_{\infty}\;=\;\frac{1-p}{1+(k-1)p},\qquad\rho^{\text{esc}}_{\infty}\;=\;0\;\;\text{for all}\;\;p>0.$

Proof. Substitute $f_{n}=pn+o(n)$ and $v_{n}=(1-p)n+o(n)$. For symmetric updates, $v_{n}+f_{n}=n$ exactly, so $\rho^{\text{sym}}(n)=\frac{\alpha+(1-p)n+o(n)}{\alpha+\beta+n}\to 1-p.$ For fixed asymmetric updates, $\rho^{\text{asy}}(n)=\frac{\alpha+(1-p)n+o(n)}{\alpha+\beta+(1+(k-1)p)n+o(n)}\to\frac{1-p}{1+(k-1)p}.$ For escalating updates with $p>0$, $kf_{n}(f_{n}+1)/2=kp^{2}n^{2}/2+o(n^{2})$, so the numerator is $\Theta(n)$ while the denominator is $\Theta(n^{2})$, giving $\rho^{\text{esc}}(n)=O(1/n)\to 0$. $\square$

#### Corollary 1 (Sustainable laundering region).

A stationary mixed strategy with $p>0$ keeps reputation above the threshold $\theta=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ asymptotically iff

$\text{({\color[rgb]{0.38,0.43,0.77}{RepVote}})}\;\;p<\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad\text{({\color[rgb]{0.38,0.43,0.77}{AsyRepVote}})}\;\;p<\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha k+\beta},\qquad\text{({\color[rgb]{0.38,0.43,0.77}{EscRepVote}})}\;\;\text{never}.$

where AsyRepVote ia a mechanism using $\rho^{\text{asy}}$. For our default $\alpha=2,\beta=1,k=3$, these become $p<1/3$, $p<1/7$, and the empty set.

Proof. Apply Proposition 1. For RepVote, $1-p>\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\iff p<\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$. For AsyRepVote, $\frac{1-p}{1+(k-1)p}>\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\iff(\alpha+\beta)(1-p)>\alpha(1+(k-1)p)\iff\beta>(\alpha k+\beta)p$. For EscRepVote, $\rho^{\text{esc}}_{\infty}=0<\theta$. $\square$

RepVote leaves a wide laundering region, AsyRepVote shrinks it but does not close it, and EscRepVote closes it entirely.

#### Proposition 2 (Cost of false reports).

At any point in a trajectory, reputation satisfies $\rho\geq\theta$ if and only if $v\geq V(f)$, where

$V^{\text{sym}}(f)\;=\;\tfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\,f,\qquad V^{\text{asy}}(f)\;=\;\tfrac{\alpha k}{\beta}\,f,\qquad V^{\text{esc}}(f)\;=\;\tfrac{\alpha k}{2\beta}\,f(f+1).$

The required valid budget is linear in $f$ for symmetric and fixed asymmetric updates and quadratic for escalation.

Proof. We have $\frac{\alpha+v}{\alpha+\beta+v+\phi(f)}\geq\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\iff(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha+v)\geq\alpha(\alpha+\beta+v+\phi(f))\iff\beta v\geq\alpha\phi(f)$. Then substitute the three penalties. $\square$

#### Corollary 2 (Adversarial budget separation).

Suppose an adversary files $N$ total reports and seeks to maximize the number of false reports while keeping $\rho\geq\theta$ at every prefix. The maximum achievable count $f^{*}(N)$ satisfies

$f^{*,\text{sym}}(N)\;=\;\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\,N+O(1),\;\;f^{*,\text{asy}}(N)\;=\;\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha k+\beta}\,N+O(1),\;\;f^{*,\text{esc}}(N)\;=\;\sqrt{\tfrac{2\beta}{\alpha k}\,N}+O(1).$

For default parameters this gives $f^{*}\approx N/3$, $f^{*}\approx N/7$, and $f^{*}\approx\sqrt{N/3}$. EscRepVote reduces the adversarial budget from linear to square-root in total report volume.

Proof. First, a feasible trajectory with $\rho\geq\theta$ throughout exists for final state $(f,v)$ if and only if $v\geq\alpha\phi(f)/\beta$. The forward direction is immediate from Proposition 2. For the reverse, consider the all-valid-first strategy that files all $v$ valid reports before any false report. During the valid-only phase, $f_{t}=0$ so the running constraint $v_{t}\geq\alpha\phi(0)/\beta=0$ is trivial. After this phase, $v_{t}=v$ is fixed; after $j^{\prime}\leq f$ subsequent false reports the running constraint is $v\geq\alpha\phi(j^{\prime})/\beta$, which holds because $\phi$ is non-decreasing and $v\geq\alpha\phi(f)/\beta$ by assumption. Feasibility therefore reduces to the single inequality $v\geq\alpha\phi(f)/\beta$.

Maximizing $f$ subject to $v+f\leq N$ and $v\geq\alpha\phi(f)/\beta$ gives $f+\alpha\phi(f)/\beta\leq N$. For linear $\phi$ this is $f(1+\alpha c/\beta)\leq N$ with $c\in\{1,k\}$, yielding $f^{*}=N\beta/(\alpha c+\beta)+O(1)$. For $\phi^{\text{esc}}$, the inequality $\frac{\alpha k}{2\beta}f^{2}+(1+\frac{\alpha k}{2\beta})f\leq N$ has its quadratic term dominate for large $N$, giving $f^{*}=\sqrt{2\beta N/(\alpha k)}+O(1)$. $\square$

#### Limitations of the analysis.

These results characterize a single agent’s reputation dynamics in isolation under stationary strategies. They do not model the suspiciousness aggregation $S_{j}$ across multiple reporters, noise in the runtime verifier, adaptive strategies that respond to current reputation, or population-level dynamics that determine which agents become reporting targets. The propositions therefore provide necessary but not sufficient conditions for enforcement mechanism robustness: any update rule susceptible to laundering under stationary strategies will fail against more sophisticated adversaries, while a rule that resists stationary laundering may still be defeated by adaptive or coordinated attacks not modeled here. The empirical results in Table 1 reflect these unmodeled effects, which we conjecture is one reason no mechanism dominates uniformly.

## Appendix H Future Work on Designing Better Norm Enforcement Mechanisms

Empirical study of norm enforcement in competitive multi-agent lm systems opens onto a design and evaluation space wider than a single paper can cover. We organize directions for future research on robust mechanisms along three axes: signal acquisition (gathering evidence of violations), signal processing (validating and weighting incoming evidence), and sanctions (what penalties to impose). We discuss each axis below.

#### Signal acquisition.

Peer reports in our setting are voluntary and sparse, meaning that many violations could be never reported. Future mechanisms could elicit denser signals, for example by occasionally requesting compulsory peer review (e.g. every K rounds, an agent must review the actions of another randomly chosen agent in order to stay on the platform), by aggregating signal across communication channels (the hostile messages in Section C.4 suggest the messaging tool already carries information about agent intent), or by incentivizing reporting through small reward bonuses tied to verified accuracy. Each option introduces its own attack surface and merits separate study.

#### Signal processing.

We explored this axis but only a small region of it. Our reputation-weighted mechanisms use a Beta-Binomial posterior with a fixed escalation schedule, while future work can explore alternative aggregation rules. One can also consider using all available information (e.g. environment main actions, messages, etc.), instead of just report actions, to update the reputation score. Our runtime verifier returns a discrete score in $\{0,0.5,1\}$ that we threshold at $1$, but multi-stage verification (cheap filter followed by expensive arbiter), verifier ensembles, and calibrated probabilistic outputs could likely improve the accuracy-cost frontier.

#### Sanctions.

We use binary removal. Richer penalty forms include throttling (reduced action frequency or visibility), temporary suspension, etc. These forms permit more proportional responses to ambiguous evidence and may reduce the cost of false positives, at the price of weakening the incentive against violation. Characterizing this tradeoff is an open question.

Real platforms can run multiple enforcement tools simultaneously. Whether composition expands the attack surface or yields mutual defense (e.g., a verifier attack mitigated by a parallel reputation signal) is an empirical question we did not address. A related direction is to make mechanisms aware of competitive context: a report against a direct rival carries different prior probability than one against a distant agent, and this signal could feed into the reputation update or the verification trigger.
