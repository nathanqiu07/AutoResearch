# Oligomeric State and Drug Binding of the SARS-CoV-2 Envelope Protein are Sensitive to the Ectodomain

## Abstract

The envelope (E) protein of SARS-CoV-2 is the smallest of the three structural membrane proteins of the virus. E mediates budding of the progeny virus in the endoplasmic reticulum Golgi intermediate compartment of the cell. It also conducts ions, and this channel activity is associated with the pathogenicity of SARS-CoV-2. The structural basis for these functions is still poorly understood. Biochemical studies of E in detergent micelles found a variety of oligomeric states, but recent 19 F solid-state NMR data indicated that the transmembrane domain (ETM, residues 8–38) forms pentamers in lipid bilayers. Hexamethylene amiloride (HMA), an E inhibitor, binds the pentameric ETM at the lipid-exposed helix-helix interface. Here, we investigate the oligomeric structure and drug interaction of an ectodomain-containing E construct, ENTM (residues 1–41). Unexpectedly, 19 F spin diffusion NMR data reveal that ENTM forms an average oligomeric state of dimers instead of pentamers in lipid bilayers. A new amiloride inhibitor, AV-352, shows stronger inhibitory activity than HMA in virus-like particle assays. Distance measurements between 13 C-labeled protein and a trifluoromethyl group of AV-352 indicate that the drug binds ENTM with a higher stoichiometry than ETM. We measured protein-drug contacts using a sensitivity-enhanced two-dimensional 13 C- 19 F distance NMR technique. The results indicate that AV-352 binds the C-terminal half of the TM domain, similar to the binding region of HMA. These data provide evidence for the existence of multiple oligomeric states of E in lipid bilayers, which may carry out distinct functions and may be differentially targeted by antiviral drugs.

## Introduction

Coronaviruses, including the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), are responsible for several epidemics and the COVID-19 pandemic since the 2000s 1–2. Elucidating the structure and function of the proteins in these viruses is essential for developing therapeutic treatment of coronavirus diseases. The SARS-CoV-2 genome encodes four structural proteins: the spike protein (S), the matrix protein (M), the envelope protein (E), and the nucleocapsid protein (N) 3. Among these, the E protein is the least understood. E mediates the assembly and budding of new virions in the endoplasmic reticulum Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) of the cell 4–5. Expression of E is necessary for the formation of virus-like particles 6–7. E also conducts ions in lipid bilayers 8–9, an activity that has been associated with the respiratory symptoms of the disease 10–12. These functions make E a member of the viroporin family, small virus membrane proteins that form pores to cause some of the pathogenic effects of the virus and that carry out additional functions in the virus lifecycle 13.

Cation conduction by the SARS-CoV-1 E protein, which is homologous to SARS-CoV-2 E, has been studied using planar lipid bilayer electrophysiological experiments since 2004 8. The observed ion conductance of E is more variable compared to bacterial and mammalian ion channels, and the ion selectivity is modest and sensitive to pH and the membrane composition 14–18. This heterogeneous channel activity suggests that the E structure may be sensitive to the membrane environment. Biochemical and biophysical studies found that the oligomeric state of E varies under different experimental conditions. Gel electrophoresis data yielded multiple bands whose molecular weight distributions depend on the detergent and protein construct. In denaturing anionic detergent such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), the E transmembrane peptide (ETM) showed dimers, trimers, and pentamers in various reports 19–20. Longer E constructs that include a part of the cytoplasmic domain formed oligomers from trimers to heptamers 21–22. In neutral detergents such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFO), full-length E (EFL) exhibited bands near the pentamer molecular weight 20, but monomers were also present 23. Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) data similarly showed a range of oligomeric states that depend on the detergents and the protein length: ETM mainly exists in a monomer-pentamer equilibrium 24 while a longer construct, ETR (residues 8–65), forms monomers to pentamers 21. Compared to ETR, EFL shows a higher propensity towards pentamers 20, although dimers still remain 17, and have been observed to be the main species in native mass spectrometry experiments 25. Since detergents have the potential to perturb the native structure of membrane proteins 26, we recently employed 19 F solid-state NMR (ssNMR) experiments to measure the oligomeric state of ETM in phospholipid bilayers. In both ERGIC-mimetic membranes and DMPC/DMPG model membranes, 19 F NMR data of ETM showed equilibrated spin-echo intensities of ~0.20, indicating that ETM on average forms pentamers 27–28. Interestingly, in phosphatidylinositol-containing membranes, the spin-echo intensities decrease further, suggesting that these pentamers cluster into larger aggregates. This pentamer state was used in the high-resolution structure determination of ETM at neutral pH 29 and at acidic pH in the presence of calcium ions 28, which are enriched in the ERGIC lumen. While these ssNMR data established the ETM structure in lipid bilayers, the oligomeric state and high-resolution structures of ETR and EFL have not been determined. A recent ssNMR study found that the cytoplasmic domain of membrane-bound ETR formed β-sheet conformation 18. If ETR is pentameric in lipid bilayers, then five subunits of these β-sheets would assemble into a water-soluble β-barrel.

Small-molecule binding to membrane proteins provides an effective reporter of functionally relevant protein structure and dynamics. Ligand binding to internal pockets of large multi-span membrane proteins such as G-protein coupled receptors has been well studied 30. In comparison, small-molecule binding to viroporins is much less understood due to the difficulty of solving the structures of small virus membrane proteins by crystallography and cryoelectron microscopy. To date, the best known example of drug interactions with a viroporin is amantadine binding to the influenza M2 protein 31–32. Like E, M2 is a multifunctional protein: it forms a tetrameric proton channel that is important for influenza uncoating 33–34 and it mediates virus budding and release 35–36. Solid-state and solution NMR data conclusively showed that the pharmacologically relevant binding site of amantadine and rimantadine is inside the channel pore 37–41. Binding changes the protonation equilibria of the proton-selective histidine 42–43, thus inhibiting proton conduction. At higher drug concentrations, a second binding site, on the lipid-facing surface of M2, is also populated 31, 37–38, 44. These results provide an example that small molecules can bind viroporins with multiple modes and at multiple sites in lipid bilayers.

Another class of viroporin inhibitors are amilorides, among which 5-(N,N-hexamethylene) amiloride (HMA) is known to inhibit the ion channel activities of the HIV viral protein U (Vpu) as well as the SARS-CoV E protein 9. Using ssNMR, we recently measured HMA distances to ETM residues 45. The data indicate that HMA contacts residues at the helix-helix interface and on the lipid-facing surface of the C-terminal half of ETM. Bound to this region, HMA perturbed the chemical shifts of the N-terminal TM residues 21, 23, 29, 46, suggesting an allosteric inhibition mechanism. These data indicate that HMA interacts with E in a distinct manner from amantadine’s interaction with influenza M2.

The multi-functional nature, environment-sensitive oligomerization, and membrane-side HMA binding of the E protein, open the question of whether the protein can adopt different structures for different functions and can have different modes of interactions with inhibitors. Here we report a ssNMR study of the interactions of a new inhibitor, AVIDD-352, with two E protein constructs. In VLP assays, AVIDD-352 (abbreviated as AV-352 below) shows moderately stronger inhibitory activities than HMA. We investigate both ETM (8–38) and a longer construct, E(1–41) (termed ENTM below), which contains seven ectodomain residues (1–7) and three cytoplasmic residues (39–41) in addition to the TM domain. We find that ENTM forms an average oligomer size of dimers in lipid bilayers, in qualitative difference from ETM. We characterize the association of AV-352 with ENTM and ETM using a novel two-dimensional (2D) sensitivity-enhanced rotational-echo double-resonance (REDOR) technique 47–48. REDOR is one of the most versatile and accurate ssNMR methods for measuring intermolecular distances 49. We recently introduced 2D 13 C- 13 C (CC) resolved 13 C- 19 F 50 and 1 H- 19 F REDOR 51 and 2D 1 H- 15 N resolved 1 H- 19 F REDOR 52 techniques to measure internuclear distances with high throughput. Here we improve upon the previous 2D CC 13 C- 19 F REDOR technique to obtain higher sensitivity and accelerate protein-drug contact measurements. Using this technique, we show that AV-352 binds to dimeric ENTM with higher stoichiometry and closer proximity than to pentameric ETM. The observation of multiple assemblies of the E protein has important implications about the mechanism of action of this structural protein of SARS-CoV-2.

## Materials and Methods

### Expression and purification of 13 C, 15 N-labeled ENTM and ETM

 13 C, 15 N-labeled SARS-CoV-2 ETM (8–38) or ENTM (1–41) C40S (Fig. 1a) were expressed and purified as described before 28, 53. Briefly, E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells were transformed with a His 6 -SUMO-ETM (or ENTM) fusion protein gene. Cells were grown at 37°C in M9 media containing 13 C-labeled D-glucose and 15 NH 4 Cl. Protein expression was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG and proceeded for 16 hours at 18°C under 200 rpm shaking. Cells were harvested, suspended in a pH 8 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgSO 4) and treated with lysozyme, 1% Triton-X, and benzonase nuclease. Cells were lysed by sonication on ice with 2 s “on”, 25 s “off” and 30% amplitude for 5 minutes of total “on” sonication time. The cell debris and insoluble protein fraction was removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was purified using a Ni 2+ affinity column (Fig. S1a). Eluted His 6 -SUMO-ENTM was cleaved for 16 hours at 4°C with SUMO protease in the presence of 1–2 mM of a reducing agent such as tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) or dithiothreitol (DTT). The cleavage reaction mixture was then purified by reverse-phase HPLC using an Agilent Zorbax 300SB preparative column (Fig. S1b) with acetonitrile: isopropanol (1: 1) as the mobile phase (solvent B) and water as solvent A. The protein eluted at 44 minutes with 89% solvent B. MALDI-TOF mass spectrum of the main fraction confirmed the identity and purity of the protein (Fig. S1c). The yield of ENTM was ~16 mg per liter of M9 culture.

### Synthesis of F23-labeled ENTM

4- 19 F-Phe23 labeled ENTM was synthesized using Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis. The amino acid sequence is MYSFVSEETG TLIVNSVLLF LAFVVFLLVT LAILTALRLS A-NH2. Synthesis was performed on TantaGel S Ram resin (0.25 mmol/g, 0.1 mmol scale) with an automated microwave peptide synthesizer (Biotage Alstra + Initiator). Deprotection was conducted at 70°C for 5 minutes using 20% 4-methylpiperidine in DMF (4.5 mL), and each coupling step was repeated twice for 5 minutes at 75°C using Fmoc-protected amino acids (5 eq), HCTU (4.98 eq), and DIPEA (10 eq) in DMF at a final concentration of 0.125 M. The peptide was cleaved from the resin after a 3-hour treatment with a 95% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) solution (10 mL) containing 2.5% TIPS and 2.5% DTT. The crude peptide was obtained by evaporating the TFA with nitrogen gas and precipitating with cold diethyl ether. The crude peptide was purified using reverse-phase HPLC (PRP-1 column, 10 μm, 21.2 × 250 mm) with a mobile phase of 0.1% ammonium hydroxide (NH 4 OH) in water (A) and 0.1% NH 4 OH in acetonitrile (B), a flow rate of 12 mL/min, and a gradient of 35% B for 5 minutes, followed by 35–100% B over 15 minutes. The peptide eluted at 22 minutes. Analytical HPLC (Rt 32 min, PRP-1 column, 7 μm, 4.1 × 250 mm, 0.1% NH 4 OH in water (A) and 0.1% NH 4 OH in acetonitrile (B, a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and a gradient of 5–100% B over 30 minutes) and MALDI (observed m/z: 4512.65, calculated m/z: 4511.39 for MNH 4 + (C214H349N47O55SF+) confirmed the identity and purity of the peptide.

### SARS-CoV-2 VLP assay

Compounds of interest were added to 96-well plates using an Echo acoustic dispenser. 293T cells were then seeded onto the compounds at a concentration of 50,000 cells per well in 150 μL final volume. The next day, the cells were transfected with plasmids to generate SARS-CoV-2 VLPs as previously reported 7, with the modification that separate M and E-encoding plasmids were used rather than an M-IRES-E plasmid. Each well received 187 ng of VLP plasmids, with mass ratios as follows: M (0.2), T20 (1), S (0.0125), E (0.05), N (0.67). The DNA was diluted in 50 μL of Opti-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher) per 1 μg of DNA. Separately, a polyethylenimine (PEI) mixture was generated that contained 1 μg of PEI in 50 μL of Opti-MEM per 1 μg of DNA. These solutions were combined and incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes. The transfection mixture was then added to the 293T cells. After 48 hours, supernatants were harvested and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. Per well, 50 μL of VLP supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of a suspension containing 50,000 293T-Ace2-TMPRESS2 cells. After 24 hours, media was removed and cells were lysed with Passive Lysis buffer (Promega) and rocked at room temperature for 20 minutes. Then, 30 μL of luciferase assay buffer (Goldbio) was added to each well using a TECAN plate reader, mixed, and luciferase activity was immediately measured.

### Preparation of membrane samples for solid-state NMR

Purified ENTM and ETM were reconstituted into lipid bilayers in organic solvents. The protein was dissolved in hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) at 1 mg/mL while lipids were dissolved in chloroform at 5 mg/mL. For most of the membrane samples in this study, we used a binary membrane consisting of 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC) and 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-rac-glycerol) (DMPG) at a molar ratio of 7 : 3. For a small number of experiments, we used a complex membrane containing POPC, POPE, POPS and cholesterol. For simplicity, we denote this lipid mixture as POPX/cholesterol. Protein and lipid solutions were combined to give a homogeneous transparent solution. The organic solvents were then removed under a stream of nitrogen gas (RT, 1 h), and residual solvent was further removed under house vacuum (50 mBar, RT, 4 h) followed by overnight lyophilization. The dry protein-lipid film was resuspended in 3 mL pH 4.5 acetate buffer containing 25 mM acetate, 25 mM NaCl, 0.07 mM NaN 3, and 2 mM TCEP. The suspension was vortexed and sonicated five times (2 s each), then incubated for 1 h at RT with gentle rocking. The suspension was frozen in liquid nitrogen until solid (90 s) and thawed in a 42°C water bath until warmed (4 min). This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated 10 times to produce multilamellar vesicles. An appropriate amount (15–75 μL) of 5.5 mg/mL DMSO stock solution of AV-352 was diluted to 1 mL with the pH 4.5 acetate buffer, then added to the proteoliposome solution and incubated for 1 h at RT. Membrane samples were prepared at formal protein monomer / drug molar ratios (P : D) of 2 : 0.8 or 2 : 4 (Table S1). The mixed protein-drug-lipid suspension was pelleted at 135,000 × g at 4°C for 1 h to obtain a membrane pellet. To remove residual HFIP, the pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of buffer, 500 μL at a time, by vortexing and minimal sonication. The solution was subject to two more freeze-thaw cycles and then centrifuged overnight at 135,000 × g at 4°C to give an off-white membrane pellet. The supernatant was removed and used for solution 19 F NMR experiments. The pellet was dried in a desiccator at RT until the sample reached a hydration level of ∼40% (w/w) water relative to the total mass. The membrane pellet was packed into magic-angle-spinning (MAS) rotors by spinning at 5000 × g using a benchtop Beckman Coulter swinging-bucket rotor.

The 13 C, 15 N-labeled ETM sample was initially prepared as described above at a formal P : D ratio of 5 : 1. After initial measurements, more drug was added by resuspending the membrane pellet in 3 mL buffer, then adding additional drug diluted in buffer. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h, then subject to two freeze-thaw cycles. The proteoliposomes were pelleted by centrifuging overnight at 135,000 × g at 4°C and dried to ∼40% (w/w) water.

### Solid-state NMR experiments

Magic-angle-spinning (MAS) ssNMR experiments were carried out on a 14.1 Tesla Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer with a 1 H Larmor frequency of 600 MHz using a Bruker 1.9 mm HFX probe. Static 31 P NMR spectra were measured on a 400 MHz spectrometer using a 1 H/ 31 P double-resonance probe. 1 H chemical shifts were referenced internally to the DMPC Hγ chemical shift at 3.264 ppm on the TMS scale. 19 F chemical shifts were referenced externally to the 5- 19 F-trptophan signal at −122.1 ppm on the CF 3 Cl scale. 13 C chemical shifts were referenced externally to the adamantane CH 2 signal at 38.48 ppm on the TMS scale. Unless otherwise specified, all temperatures refer to the sample temperature, which was estimated from the bulk water 1 H chemical shift using the equation T (K) = 96.9 × (7.83 ppm – δ H2O) 54. Typical radiofrequency (rf) field strengths were 70–130 kHz for 1 H, 50–65 kHz for 13 C, and 50–85 kHz for 19 F (Table S2).

 13 C- 19 F REDOR experiments were conducted under 24 kHz MAS using a 2D CC correlation experiment in which a DREAM dipolar recoupling sequence 55 was implemented for 13 C- 13 C polarization transfer. We call this experiment CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ (see Supporting Information for pulse sequence) and tested it on 13 C, 15 N, 2 H (CDN)-labeled GB1 that contains a single 19 F label at 5- 19 F-Trp43 56. The 2D experiment starts with 1 H excitation and 1 H- 13 C cross polarization (CP), followed by 13 C- 19 F dipolar recoupling using a frequency-selective REDOR module in which a train of rotor-synchronized 19 F 180° pulses is interrupted by a central 500 μs 13 C Gaussian 180° pulse. This 13 C soft pulse selects either the Cα signals between 50 and 70 ppm or the sidechain 13 C signals between 10 and 28 ppm, by placing the 13 C carrier frequency either at 61.5 ppm or at 17.3 ppm, respectively. The 13 C- 19 F REDOR mixing times were 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 ms. For all except the 5 ms mixing time, 13 C t 1 evolution was implemented within the REDOR mixing period in a constant-time fashion 57, by shifting the 13 C 180° pulse from the center of the REDOR period to the beginning. The shift was one rotor period between successive t 1 points, thus the effective t 1 dwell time was two rotor periods, which correspond to an indirect dimension spectral width of 12 kHz. For the 5 ms REDOR mixing time, the indirect dimension was evolved regularly after the REDOR period to 6.7 ms. Following the constant-time t 1 -REDOR period, 13 C magnetization transfer was achieved using a 3.5 ms adiabatic tangent DREAM pulse whose rf amplitude was centered at ω r /2 (12 kHz) and ranged from 8 to 16 kHz. 1 H decoupling using the SPINAL sequence 58 at 130 kHz was applied during the REDOR mixing period and acquisition period, while continuous-wave (CW) 1 H decoupling was applied during the DREAM period. Two CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ spectra were measured for each REDOR mixing time, a control spectrum (S 0) without 19 F pulses and a dephased spectrum (S) with 19 F pulses. The CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ experiment was compared with the previously reported radiofrequency-driven recoupling (RFDR) 2D 13 C- 19 F REDOR experiment to assess spectral sensitivities 50, 59.

2D CC spectra of membrane-bound ENTM and ETM were measured under 14 kHz or 10.5 kHz MAS using a combined R2 n ν -driven (CORD) sequence for 13 C spin diffusion 60. Water-edited 13 C spectra were measured using a 2.1 ms (30 t r) water-selective 1 H spin echo consisting of a 2.0 ms (28 t r) Gaussian 180° pulse and one additional rotor period on each side 61. This 1 H T 2 filter was followed by a 1 H mixing period, which ranged from 10 μs to 100 ms in the one-dimensional (1D) water-edited experiments and 9 ms in the 2D water-edited CC experiment. Water magnetization transfer curves were obtained from integrated intensities of the Cα band of the 1D spectra normalized by the maximum intensities. The Cα-Cβ cross peak intensities in the water-edited 2D CC spectra measured with 9 ms 1 H spin diffusion were divided by the intensities of the unedited 2D CC spectra (I 9 ms / I 2D CC). This ratio was then divided by the intensity ratio of the 100 ms 1D water-edited spectrum relative to the 13 C CP spectrum (I 100 ms, 1D /I CP). In this way, we obtain the 9 ms versus 100 ms water-transferred intensity ratio, I 9 ms / I 100 ms = (I 9 ms / I 2D CC) / (I 100 ms, 1D /I CP).

2D 1 H- 19 F heteronuclear correlation (HETCOR) spectra were measured under 10 kHz MAS at 303 K to assess the depth of insertion of AV-352 in the membrane. The 1 H T 2 filter was four-rotor period (400 μs) and the 1 H mixing time was 100 ms to transfer the water and lipid 1 H magnetization to the drug.

 19 F CP centerband-only detection of exchange (CODEX) experiments of 4F-Phe23 labeled ENTM were conducted under 18 kHz MAS at a sample temperature of 245 K 27–28. The 19 F chemical shift anisotropy (CSA) recoupling period was 2τ r = 0.11 ms for each of the two rotor-synchronized pulse trains. A triggering pulse was used for each recoupling period to maintain rotor synchronization. 1 H CW decoupling at an rf field strength of 100 kHz was applied during the CSA recoupling period. The mixing time t m between the two CSA recoupling periods ranged from 250 ms to 3 s. To compensate for T 1 relaxation, we conducted the experiment in pairs: in the S 0 experiment, a short t z period of 10 μs occurred after the first CSA recoupling period while the long t m period was applied after the second CSA recoupling period. In the S experiment, t m preceded the short t z period. The CODEX intensity ratio, S/S 0, between the exchange (S) and control (S 0) spectra was obtained from the integrated intensities of the 19 F centerband. The measured S/S 0 values were simulated using an exchange matrix algorithm 27, 62 to obtain the inter-fluorine distances and the number of 19 F spins in close proximity. During 19 F detection, 1 H two-pulse phase modulation (TPPM) 63 decoupling at an rf field of 100 kHz was applied.

All ssNMR spectra were processed using the TopSpin 3.6.4 software. S/S 0 values report either the ratios of integrated intensities (for CODEX) or peak heights (for REDOR). The uncertainty σ was propagated from the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the spectra using σ = (S/S 0)[(SNR S0 −2 + SNR S −2) 1/2], and corresponds to one standard deviation.

### 19 F solution NMR experiments

 19 F solution NMR spectra were measured to obtain the fraction of AV-352 that remained in the supernatant after centrifuging the membrane. 40 μL of D 2 O was added to 600 μL of the supernatant from each membrane sample, and the spectra were measured on a 600 MHz Avance NEO spectrometer equipped with a quadruple-resonance HFXY cryoprobe. All spectra were measured at 25°C. The integrated intensity of the 19 F peaks in each spectrum was compared to a standard sample prepared by diluting a known quantity of AV-352 DMSO stock solution to a total volume of 600 μL with buffer. The intensity was scaled by the total volume of the supernatant of each membrane sample to estimate the amount of drug that partitioned to the membrane pellet.

## Results

### AV-352 inhibits the formation of virus-like particles

A high-throughput assay utilizing SARS-CoV-2 virus-like particles (SC2-VLPs) was developed for rapid analysis of mutations within the structural proteins of the virus and for identifying potential antiviral drugs 7. SC2-VLPs package and deliver exogenous RNA transcripts to enable the safe analysis of viral assembly, budding, stability, maturation, entry, and genome uncoating. SC2-VLPs are generated by co-transfecting 293T packaging cells with four plasmids encoding the structural proteins and a fifth plasmid encoding luciferase mRNA linked to a SARS-CoV-2 packaging signal (T20). Robust luciferase expression was observed in receiver cells only in the presence of all four structural proteins (S, M, N, and E) as well as the T20-containing reporter transcript. AV-352 and the reference compound HMA (Fig. 1b) were evaluated using this assay. HMA yielded an IC 50 of 10.4 μM, in line with its general potency reported in the literature using other assays 23 64. In comparison, AV-352 showed moderately improved potency, with an IC 50 of 4.2 μM (Fig. 1c). The trifluoromethyl group of AV-352 provides an excellent NMR probe to investigate the binding of this class of compounds to E.

### Oligomerization of SARS-CoV-2 ENTM in lipid bilayers

We first characterized the conformation of ENTM using 2D CC experiments 53. Although ENTM is longer than ETM by only ten residues, its ssNMR spectra show markedly broader lines than ETM (Fig. 2a, b). ETM exhibits 13 C linewidths of about 0.5 ppm, giving single-site resolution in the 2D spectra 65–66. In contrast, ENTM exhibits 13 C linewidths of about 1.5 ppm, thus only residue-type resolution is available in the 2D spectra. Compared to ETM, ENTM shows several additional cross peaks in the aliphatic and carbonyl region (Fig. S2), which can be tentatively assigned to Ser6 or Ser 3, Val5 and Glu7 in the ectodomain. These chemical shifts correspond to the random coil conformation, indicating that the ectodomain is unstructured. In addition, ENTM exhibits chemical shift perturbation or intensity changes for several N- and C-terminal residues of the TM domain, including T9, G10, I13, T35 and R38 indicating that the addition of the ectodomain and cytoplasmic residues perturbed the conformation of the residues that link the TM domain with the extra-membrane domains.

The substantial linewidth increase of ENTM compared to ETM prompted us to investigate whether ENTM undergoes pronounced motion that is absent in the shorter construct. 13 C cross-polarization and direct-polarization spectra indicate that the linewidths of membrane-bound ENTM are relatively insensitive to temperature between 310 K and 290 K (Fig. S3). In addition, the Cα T 2 relaxation times range from 4 ms to 11 ms for various Cα signals, and are similar between 290 K and 310 K. These observations indicate that line broadening of ENTM at these moderate temperatures are not due to dynamic disorder but are due to static conformational distribution of the protein.

We next investigated whether ENTM oligomerizes in the same way as ETM. We recently showed that ETM forms pentamers in a variety of lipid membranes and at both neutral and acidic pH 27–28. This pentameric state was determined using the 19 F CODEX technique 67–68, which measures the intensities of a 19 F spin echo after spin diffusion among all members of an oligomer. The equilibrium value is equal to 1/N for an N-oligomer. Similar to the ETM study, we introduced a single 4- 19 F label at the native Phe23 in synthetic ENTM and measured the 19 F CODEX echo intensities. In DMPC/DMPG bilayers at a protein/lipid (P : L) molar ratio of 1 : 30, the ENTM 19 F CODEX intensities equilibrated to 0.52±0.11 at a mixing time of 2 s, indicating an average oligomer size of dimer (Fig. 2c). Under the same conditions, ETM exhibits an equilibrium CODEX intensity of 0.22 ± 0.04, corresponding to a pentamer (Fig. 2d).

To investigate whether the dimer state of ENTM is specific to the acidic pH, the DMPC/DMPG membrane, or the low P : L ratio (1 : 30) used for the above experiment, we conducted the CODEX experiment on two additional samples. At a higher P : L of 1 : 15 in the DMPC/DMPG membrane, the CODEX echo intensities plateaued to ~0.38, consistent with an average state of dimers at this concentration (Fig. S4a, Table S3). In a cholesterol-containing lipid membrane at pH 7.5 at a P : L of 1 : 30, the echo intensities plateaued to ~0.40 at 2 s (Fig. S4b). These results consistently indicate that ENTM forms small oligomers with an average size of dimers at both high and low pH, and in both model membranes and complex cholesterol-containing membranes. Thus, the addition of the ectodomain to the TM domain qualitatively changed the mode of self-assembly of the E protein.

### Conformation and dynamics of AV-352 in E-containing lipid membranes

The distinct oligomerization of ENTM and ETM suggests that AV-352 may interact with the two proteins in different ways. To investigate the drug binding mode, we first quantified drug partitioning into the proteoliposome by measuring the 19 F ssNMR spectra of the membrane-associated drug and 19 F solution NMR spectra of the drug in the supernatant after pelleting the membrane. For all membrane samples, we maintained the same P : L ratio of 1 : 15 while varying the P : D ratio. The drug-bound ENTM samples were prepared with a total P : D of 2 : 0.8 or 2 : 4. We denote the protein : drug molar ratio to reflect the oligomer size of the protein.

We quantified the intensity of the solution 19 F signal of the drug in the supernatant by comparison with the intensity of a known amount of AV-352 in buffer (Fig. S5, Table S4). For the protein-free membrane, 23% of the drug remained in the supernatant while 77% partitioned into the membrane. For the ENTM-containing membranes, the 19 F solution NMR spectra also indicate that only a small fraction of AV-352 remained in the supernatant while the majority associated with the membrane. At P : D ratios of 2 : 0.8 and 2 : 4, 84% and 71% of drug partitioned into the membrane, respectively, giving effective membrane-bound P : D ratios of 2 : 0.7 and 2 : 3. In comparison, the ETM-containing membrane shows a larger fraction (~60%) of drug in the supernatant (Fig. S5e), giving a membrane-bound P : D ratio of 5 : 4 from the total P : D of 5 : 10. For clarity, below we use the membrane-bound P : D ratios to refer to the different samples. The 19 F solution NMR spectra of AV-352 resolve two peaks that are 0.07 ppm apart (Fig. S5a). The reason for this peak splitting is unclear. We hypothesize that it might result from torsional angle distributions that lead to slightly different O-CF 3 conformations. Since the two 19 F peaks are well within the 19 F linewidths of the membrane-bound drug, we do not evaluate them further in analyzing the protein-drug interactions in the lipid membrane.

To investigate the dynamics of membrane-associated AV-352, we measured the 19 F MAS spectra of AV-352 as a function of temperature in protein-containing DMPC/DMPG membranes. In the ENTM-containing gel-phase membrane at 246 K, the 19 F spectrum exhibits an isotropic chemical shift of −58.3 ppm. Under 7 kHz MAS, high sideband intensities are observed, which are fit to give a 19 F chemical shift anisotropy (δ) of 27 ppm and an asymmetry parameter (η) of 0.2 (Fig. 3a). The rigid-limit 19 F CSA of trifluoroethanol is 87 ppm 69. If fast methyl three-site jump is the only motion, then the 19 F CSA should be scaled down three-fold to 29 ppm 70. Thus, the measured CSA of the OCF 3 group of AV-352 is consistent with methyl three-site jumps being the dominant motion of this functional group in the membrane at this temperature. We also measured a static 19 F spectrum of the drug at 285 K, above the water freezing point but below the main phase transition temperature (295 K) of the DMPC/DMPG membrane. Even without sample spinning, the 19 F spectrum shows a narrow peak with a width of 4.0 ppm (Fig. 3b), indicating that AV-352 undergoes large-amplitude reorientations in addition to methyl three-site jumps under these conditions. Thus, AV-352 dynamics is sensitive to both water dynamics and the membrane dynamics. Consistent with the narrow static lineshape, 19 F MAS spectra of the drug from 248 K to 290 K show monotonically decreasing isotropic linewidths from 2.5 ppm to 0.5 ppm (Fig. 3c), indicating increasing dynamics at higher temperature.

Interestingly, in ETM-containing lipid membranes, AV-352 exhibits two 19 F signals at −56.6 ppm and −58.2 ppm (Fig. 3d), whose integrated intensity ratio is about 1 : 2. We attribute the sharp −56.6 ppm signal to drug that mainly interacts with lipids and the broad −58.5 ppm peak to protein-interacting drug. This assignment is based on the linewidth difference and dephasing observed in 19 F- 13 C REDOR (vide infra).

To assess whether drug binding affects the morphology of the lipid membrane, we measured static 31 P NMR spectra (Fig. S6). The drug-bound DMPC/DMPG membrane shows a predominantly uniaxial 31 P powder pattern that is indicative of lamellar bilayers. A small and broad isotropic peak is also present, whose intensity is similar to that in the control DMPC/DMPG membrane, indicating that this feature most likely results from small vesicles produced by sonication and freeze-thawing rather than by the drug. Addition of ENTM to the membrane retained the uniaxial lineshapes both in the absence and presence of the drug. Thus, neither AV-352 or ENTM causes noticeable curvature to the lipid membrane.

### Stoichiometry of AV-352 binding to E from 19 F- 13 C REDOR experiments

To investigate protein-drug proximity, we measured 19 F- 13 C REDOR spectra between the 19 F-containing AV-352 and 13 C-labeled protein. We carried out these REDOR experiments by detecting the 19 F signal of the drug first and the 13 C signals of the protein next 71. By observing dipolar dephasing of the drug 19 F signal by the 13 C-labeled protein, we obtain information about the number of drug molecules that are in molecular contact with the protein. By observing dipolar dephasing of the 13 C signals of the protein by the fluorinated drug, we obtain information about the fraction and locations of the protein residues that are in close contact with the drug.

Fig. 4a shows the 19 F-detected 19 F- 13 C REDOR spectra of three drug-complexed samples: two ENTM-containing samples with membrane P : D ratios of 2 : 0.7 and 2 : 3, and the ETM sample with a membrane P : D of 5 : 4. For both ENTM samples, most of the drug 19 F intensities are dephased by the protein 13 C sites. The S/S 0 intensity ratios decreased to 0.06 for the 2 : 0.7 sample by a REDOR mixing time of 3 ms and to 0.12 for the 2 : 3 sample by 6 ms (Fig. 4b). Thus, about 90% of the drugs are in molecular contact with 13 C-labeled ENTM in the two samples (Table S5). The fact that the REDOR dephasing is faster for the 2 : 0.7 sample than the 2 : 3 sample indicate that the first equivalent of drug is in closer contact with the protein than the second and third equivalent of drug. These data thus suggest that ENTM possesses multiple drug-interacting sites with different affinities. We also measured the 19 F- 13 C REDOR spectra of a drug-only sample where no protein is present. No dipolar dephasing is observed (Fig. 4a, b), confirming that lipid natural abundance 13 C sites do not cause noticeable dephasing to the CF 3 group of the drug within the mixing times used.

The 19 F- 13 C REDOR spectra of the ETM sample differs qualitatively from the ENTM samples. The sharp −56.5 peak shows little dipolar dephasing, while the broad −58.4 ppm peak shows a similar extent of dephasing as ENTM (Fig. 4a, b). Thus, these ETM spectra support the assignment of the two 19 F peaks to a mobile fraction of lipid-bound drugs and an immobile fraction of protein-bound drugs. Since the ETM-bound drug represents about two thirds of all drugs in the membrane, the stoichiometry of ETM to bound drug is about 5 : 2.7. Taken together, these drug-detected 19 F- 13 C REDOR spectra indicate that ENTM contains more drug binding sites than ETM does: each ENTM dimer can interact with three drug molecules whereas each ETM pentamer interacts with only ~2.7 drugs, corresponding to ~50% occupancy. These REDOR-derived number of bound drugs per protein differs from the inhibitory concentration measured in solution-based assays. Our membrane samples were prepared with an initial drug concentration of 50–150 μM in solution. Even if this is in the saturation limit, the number of bound drugs per protein can vary with the protein constructs due to the different protein structures.

### Sensitivity-enhanced 2D 13 C- 19 F REDOR experiment to measure protein-drug contacts

To investigate the drug-binding location in the E protein, we next turned to 13 C-detected 13 C- 19 F REDOR experiment. Fig. 4c shows 1D 13 Cα- 19 F REDOR spectra at a mixing time of 20 ms. Both ENTM samples show dipolar dephasing of the 65-ppm Val Cα peak and the 55-ppm Leu Cα peak. The TM domain of E contains five Val residues and nine Leu residues, thus the dephasing of these two peaks do not give site-specific information about the drug binding location. However, the 13 C spectrum also shows dipolar dephasing of the Phe Cα signal at 59 ppm. There are only three Phe residues in the TM domain, all clustered in the middle of the peptide. Thus, this observation suggests that AV-352 is localized to the middle of the TM helix.

To resolve the protein-drug contacts with higher site resolution, we next conducted the 2D CC-resolved 13 C- 19 F REDOR experiment. Our original experiment used the RFDR sequence to transfer the 13 C magnetization and establish cross peaks in the 2D spectra 50, 59 (Fig. 5a). This RFDR polarization transfer has low efficiency. Moreover, substantial 13 C T 2 relaxation occurs during the REDOR mixing time. The 13 C T 2 relaxation times of 13 C-labeled membrane proteins after suppressing 13 C- 13 C J-coupling typically range from 5 ms to 10 ms (Fig. S3). Thus, at a REDOR mixing time of 20 ms, the S 0 intensity is less than 14% of the full intensity. To increase the experimental sensitivity, we adopted the DREAM sequence 55, which uses an adiabatic tangent pulse to transfer 13 C polarization with higher efficiency. In addition, we embedded the 13 C t 1 evolution period within the REDOR mixing period in a constant-time fashion 57. By moving the selective 13 C 180° pulse from the center of the REDOR period to the beginning, the 13 C magnetization evolves under the chemical shift interaction and variable degree of 13 C- 13 C J-coupling. The 13 C 180° pulse can be used to selectively detect the backbone Cα peaks or the sidechain methyl peaks. The constant-time t 1 evolution reduces both the transverse time experienced by the 13 C magnetization and the 1 H decoupling time, thus increasing experimental efficiency. We term this technique CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ (carbon aliphatic selection with indirect fluorine off-diagonal REDOR intensity informs assignment via DREAM specific transfer) (Fig. 5b).

We first demonstrate this CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ experiment on 13 C, 15 N, 2 H-labeled GB1 that contains a single fluorine at 5- 19 F-Trp43 50. At a REDOR mixing time of 8 ms, we observed difference intensities in the 2D spectra for W43-proximal residues such as V54, W32 and E27 (Fig. 5c, d), consistent with previously measured 2D RFDR-REDOR spectra 50. Cross sections of the 2D spectra indicate that the S 0 intensities of the cross peaks in the CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ spectra are 2–3 fold higher than the S 0 intensities of the RFDR-REDOR spectra (Fig. 5e). A 2D spectrum measured with variable t 1 evolution and DREAM transfer showed that the constant-time 2D DREAM spectrum only slightly increased the signal intensity compared to the variable-t 1 spectrum for a REDOR mixing time of 8 ms (Fig. S7). Thus, for the well ordered GB1 with long 13 C T 2 relaxation times, the constant-time t 1 evolution does not provide significant sensitivity improvement, but it still reduces the 1 H decoupling time and thus facilitates the experiment by shortening the recycle delay. In addition, the RFDR sequence achieves broadband magnetization transfer, which distributes the source 13 C magnetization in the indirect dimension over multiple cross peaks in the direct dimension. In comparison, the DREAM sequence achieves band-selective magnetization transfer, which reduces the number of cross peaks per 13 C site, thus increasing the sensitivity. Overall, these data indicate that the higher sensitivity of the CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ experiment over RFDR-REDOR mainly results from the more efficient polarization transfer of DREAM over RFDR.

We applied the CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ experiment to drug-complexed ENTM in the DMPC/DMPG membrane. At both P : D ratios, we observed clear REDOR difference intensities at 20 ms mixing, indicating that AV-352 is in molecular contact with the protein (Fig. 6a, b). The high sensitivity of the experiment allowed each pair of 2D spectra to be measured in ~10 hours (Table S6), accelerating the measurement of REDOR dephasing. At the P : D ratio of 2 : 0.7, Leu and Val Cα-Cβ cross peaks show difference signals. When the drug concentration increased to P : D = 2 : 3, additional difference intensities are observed at Ala, Ile, and Phe Cα-Cβ cross peaks (Fig. 6c). We also shifted the carrier frequency of the selective 13 C 180° pulse to the sidechain region to observe sidechain contacts with the drug. Difference intensities are observed for Thr, Ala and Val. The Thr Cγ2-Cα cross peak is informative, as the Cα-selective spectra do not exhibit the Thr Cα-Cβ peak due to the similar chemical shifts of these two carbons, which prevent refocusing of the Cα-Cβ J-coupling.

Together, the CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ spectra yielded an average S/S 0 ratio of 0.93 for the Cα band at 20 ms mixing for the 2 : 0.7 sample and a lower value of 0.82 for the 2 : 3 sample. Thus, the average protein-drug distances decrease with increasing drug concentration. Fig. S8 shows the residue-type resolved REDOR dephasing of the ENTM samples at the two P : D ratios. Even without single-site resolution, it can be seen that dipolar dephasing is not identical for all residue types: the Ala Cα and Cβ signals show lower S/S 0 values than the other residues for both samples. There are only three Ala residues in the TM domain: Ala22, Ala32 and Ala36. Thus, the larger REDOR dephasing of the Ala residues compared to other residues, which are more symmetrically distributed between the N- and C-terminal halves of the TM domain, indicate that AV-352 preferentially binds the C-terminal half of the TM segment.

### Membrane and water interactions of AV-352

To investigate the effect of AV-352 binding on the hydration of E, we measured water-edited 13 C spectra of ENTM in the presence of different amounts of bound drug. Fig. 7a shows water-transferred ENTM 13 C spectra with 9 ms and 100 ms 1 H mixing. Compared to the 100 ms spectra, higher drug concentration (P : D = 2 : 3) suppressed the water-transferred protein intensities compared to the lower drug concentration (P : D = 2 : 0.7) (Fig. 7b). This water accessibility decrease is observed for various residues based on the water-edited 2D CC spectra (Fig. 7c, Table S7). The polar acyl guanidinium moiety of the drug likely facilitates water magnetization transfer to the protein through chemical exchange 72–73; despite this effect, the water-transferred protein intensity is lower under higher drug concentrations. Therefore, these data provide evidence that AV-352 binding dehydrates the protein; moreover, multiple drugs combine to dehydrate the protein more severely than one drug alone, even though the first equivalent of drug binds ENTM more tightly than subsequent equivalents.

Finally, we probed the depth of insertion of AV-352 using a 2D 1 H- 19 F HETCOR experiment. The 2D spectra show clear cross peaks of the drug with both water and lipid acyl chains (Fig. 7d), suggesting that AV-352 lies at the membrane-water interface. These HETCOR spectra and the water-edited protein spectra together indicate that AV-352 binds E from the membrane side, and binding dehydrates the protein.

## Discussion

### AV-352 binding to E depends on the oligomeric structure of the protein

The data presented here reveal two unexpected aspects about the SARS-CoV-2 E protein: we found that ENTM forms dimers instead of pentamers in lipid bilayers, and the inhibitor AV-352 has different binding stoichiometries for different oligomeric states (Fig. 8). Previous studies of E in detergents found multiple oligomers that are smaller than pentamers, but interpretation of this observation is confounded by the fact that denaturing detergents such as SDS generate monomer bands or a subset of the subunits of an oligomer 21, while detergents such as alkyl phosphocholines 24 have a tendency to distort the native structure of membrane proteins 26, 74. In comparison, lipid bilayers provide a more native environment for membrane proteins. For example, the tetramer state of the influenza M2 protein is stabilized in lipid bilayers over detergent micelles 75–76. Our recent 19 F spin diffusion NMR data found that ETM forms pentamers in a variety of lipid bilayers, 27 supporting the conclusion that pentamers are an energetically stable state of the transmemebrane domain. Thus, the current result that the pentamer state of E is destabilized upon the addition of seven ectodomain residues and three cytoplasmic residues is surprising. In the presence of these extra-membrane residues, ENTM assembled into an average oligomer size of dimers in lipid bilayers. This dimer is observed at both acidic pH and neutral pH, thus it cannot be attributed to pH-dependent effects. Among the ectodomain residues 1 MYSFVSE 7, the only charged residue is E7. Although this gives rise to two consecutive glutamate residues, both residues should be predominantly protonated and neutral at pH 4.5. Moreover the measured chemical shifts indicate that the N-terminal segment is unstructured 28, suggesting that electrostatic repulsion is unlikely to be the cause for pentamer dissociation. We observed dimers in both model DMPC/DMPG membranes and in cholesterol-containing complex membranes, thus the dimer also cannot be attributed to unusual lipid interactions. Instead, these data suggest that the dimer state is intrinsic to the amino acid sequence of ENTM (1–41). We do not have data for whether this dimer state will persist in full-length E; but recent AUC sedimentation velocity data show a mixture of pentamers and dimers for EFL 17, suggesting that the dimer may be present in membrane-bound EFL.

A dimeric E is unlikely to support ion channel activity, thus the current finding opens the question of the potential function of E dimers in lipid membranes. In addition to ion channel activities, E interacts with the membrane protein M to mediate virus assembly 77–78 and regulate the intracellular localization of the S protein 79. E and M are necessary for forming virus-like particles in cells 7, 80–81. The SARS-CoV-2 M protein is dimeric 82–83 and contains a TM helical bundle that is connected to a β-sheet rich cytoplasmic domain. We speculate that dimeric E might interact with M more readily compared to pentameric E. The E protein is also thought to cause membrane curvature to assist in virus budding and release, in analogy to the influenza M2 protein 35. Small-angle X-ray scattering data 84 showed that E condenses the surrounding lipid bilayer, which may in turn cause membrane curvature. The oligomeric structure of E for this curvature induction function has not been studied, and dimeric E could play a role in this function. Finally, it is conceivable that the dimer state might correspond to a storage form of the protein that might be activated to convert to pentamers in response to chemical signals and protein-protein interactions. If this is the case, then small-molecule drugs such as AV-352 might act by shifting the equilibrium between the different oligomeric states.

The coexistence of two oligomer states with different functions has been reported for the E protein of infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) 85, an avian coronavirus. There, sucrose gradient analysis showed a higher molecular-weight pool that represent homo-oligomers up to a hexamer, which is required for producing virus-like particles. In comparison, a low molecular-weight pool that may be monomers or dimers is associated with disruption of the secretory pathway of the cell by the E protein. Future experiments will be required to elucidate the oligomeric structure of full-length E of SARS-CoV-2, and if multiple states are present, to understand the different functions of these different oligomers.

AV-352 interacts differently with the dimeric ENTM versus the pentameric ETM. Drug 19 F-detected REDOR spectra show that up to ~3 equivalents of drug bind the dimeric ENTM (P : D = 2 : 3) while ~2.7 equivalents of drug bind the pentameric ETM (5 : 2.7). Thus, more drug binds each subunit of ENTM than ETM. Moreover, the bound AV-352 is in closer contact with ENTM than with ETM, as shown by the faster 19 F- 13 C REDOR dephasing (Fig. 4b). Between the two drug concentrations used for ENTM, the different REDOR dephasing indicates that the first equivalent of drug associates more tightly with the protein than the second and third drug molecules do. 13 C-detected REDOR data show that the Ala residues are more strongly dephased by the drug than other residue types, implying that the drug is localized to the C-terminal half of the TM domain. Although the lack of single-site resolution of the ENTM spectra prohibited the extraction of site-specific distances, the qualitative finding that AV-352 interacts more strongly with the C-terminal half of the TM segment is in good agreement with the binding location of HMA in ETM 45. We hypothesize that the C-terminal localization of the amiloride compounds might reflect the preferential interaction of the acyl guanidinium moiety with the membrane surface negatively charged lipid headgroups. The AV-352 CF 3 group shows cross peaks with both water and lipid chain protons, suggesting that the drug lies at the membrane-water interface. This is consistent with the amphipathic nature of the molecule. Increasing the drug concentration reduced the water accessibility of ENTM (Fig. 7b), suggesting that the bound drug molecules shielded the N- and C-terminal residues of the protein from water.

Our recent study of HMA interaction with ETM found that HMA binds ETM at a lower stoichiometry of one drug per pentamer (Fig. 8) 45. This was manifested in the drug-detected REDOR data. Since the binding locations of HMA and AV-352 are qualitatively similar, both at the C-terminal half of ETM, the currently observed higher stoichiometry of AV-352 to ETM might contribute to the moderately improved IC 50 of AV-352 than HMA. Several possible mechanisms could explain the differences in stoichiometry between AV-352 and HMA. Both compounds share an acyl guanidinium group, but HMA has an additional polar pyrazine ring that is absent in AV-352. This pyrazine ring makes HMA less hydrophobic and less amphipathic than AV-352. The C-terminal residues of ETM are predominantly hydrophobic except for T35 and R38. Thus, the longer hydrophobic segment and higher amphipathic character of AV-352 might facilitate its interaction with ETM compared to HMA. The higher stoichiometry of AV-352 to the ETM pentamer than HMA might tighten the helical bundle more effectively, thus shifting the protein conformational equilibrium toward the closed state. Future experiments that map the precise binding location of AV-352 and that examine more potent inhibitors will be useful for testing this hypothesis and elucidating the mechanism of action of these amiloride E inhibitors.

The dramatic line broadening of the E protein from a TM-only construct to the ectodomain-containing construct points to the significant disordering effect by the water-soluble domains to the TM helical bundle. Our variable-temperature 13 C spectra and 13 C T 2 measurements (Fig. S3) indicate that the broad linewidths of ENTM reflect static rather than dynamic disorder. Similar disordering of TM helices by extra-membrane residues have been observed in the cytoplasmic-containing ETR (8–65) 18 and the cytoplasmic-containing influenza M2 protein (residues 21–97) 86–87. Several factors can contribute to this conformational heterogeneity. A well-ordered TM helical bundle has C n rotational symmetry, with identical helix tilt angles and rotation angles for all the subunits of the assembly. Any orientational differences between helices will cause different helix-helix packing interfaces and break the C n symmetry, giving different chemical shifts for the multiple subunits of the bundle. The dynamically disordered water-soluble domains could impart conformational disorder to the TM domain through the linker residues by slightly twisting the helices to break the C n symmetry. A homo-oligomeric single-span helical bundle lacks heterotypic protein-protein interactions that are present in multi-span membrane proteins, thus they are more susceptible to structural perturbation by extra-membrane residues. In addition, coronavirus E proteins have unusually hydrophobic amino acid sequences for the TM domain. The scarcity of polar residues means that the thermodynamic stability of the homo-oligomeric assembly may be marginal, and the addition of water-soluble domains might easily tip the balance between different helix-helix assemblies. This conformational plasticity might serve functional purposes, by allowing coronaviruses to switch their E proteins between different functions.

### Sensitivity-enhanced 2D 13 C- 19 F REDOR and dual detection of protein and ligand in REDOR experiments

Two aspects of the REDOR NMR experiments are crucial for accurate description of AV-352 binding to the E protein in lipid membranes. First, the CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ technique yielded 2–3 fold higher sensitivity than the RFDR-REDOR experiment 50, 60, thus speeding up the measurement of protein-drug contacts. Tests on the model protein GB1 indicate that the higher sensitivity is mostly due to the more efficient magnetization transfer by the DREAM sequence 55 and its band-selective nature, which restricts the number of cross peaks to one for each 13 C source spin. The constant-time t 1 evolution 57 reduces the effects of 13 C T 2 relaxation, which also enhances the sensitivity; its benefit will be more pronounced for proteins with short 13 C T 2 relaxation times and for long REDOR mixing times. As a result of this higher experimental sensitivity, we measured five pairs of 2D REDOR S 0 and S spectra within two days, on samples that contained only ~2.6 mg of protein and ~0.3 mg of drug. This CALIFORNIA DREAMIN’ experiment is broadly applicable to the investigation of protein-ligand interactions.

The detection of intermolecular dipolar couplings via both the drug and the protein is essential for the accurate identification of the stoichiometry and binding site of AV-352 in the E protein. Although the trifluoromethyl group of the drug is a potent dipolar dephaser that facilitates quantitative distance measurements 36, the ligand binding stoichiometry to a protein is often unknown, especially when the ligand is lipophilic and interacts with both lipids and the protein. Analysis of intermolecular REDOR dephasing to extract quantitative distances requires the knowledge of the binding stoichiometry. Previously, we determined the amantadine binding stoichiometry to the influenza M2 protein by detecting the 2 H NMR signals of amantadine as well as the 13 C signals of M2 31. We also investigated cholesterol interactions with HIV gp41 trimers by detecting both cholesterol 13 C signals as dephased by fluorinated protein and protein 19 F signals as dephased by 13 C-labeled cholesterol 88. This dual observation of the protein and ligand allowed the extraction of both the number and the location of cholesterol molecules in each gp41 trimer.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, the current data provides evidence that the ectodomain of the SARS-CoV-2 E protein changes the pentameric assembly of the TM domain to cause an average oligomer state of dimers in lipid bilayers. The new amiloride inhibitor, AV-352, binds the dimeric E with higher stoichiometry than the pentameric ETM. Drug binding reduced the water accessibility of the protein in the lipid membrane. These results indicate that the ectodomain of E has the propensity to reduce the self-assembly of the TM domain. Whether the dimer state persists in full-length E, and whether multiple oligomeric states of the full-length protein coexist in the virus-infected cell to carry out distinct functions will require future investigation. The potential existence of multiple conformations of E suggests that the protein might adopt different structures for different functions, and antiviral drugs might inhibit E by shifting the conformational equilibrium among these different states.
