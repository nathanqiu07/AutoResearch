# Circulating cholesterol fuels SARS-CoV-2 replication via ORF3a

## Abstract

High levels of “bad” cholesterol increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and are also linked to more severe COVID-19, though the reason has been unclear. We show that low-density lipoprotein (LDL) directly enhances SARS-CoV-2 RNA replication, especially in the Beta variant. The viral accessory protein ORF3a is both necessary and sufficient to increase cholesterol uptake in cell culture and hamster models. ORF3a stabilizes the host LDL receptor by displacing the PCSK9 chaperone, leading to greater cholesterol entry into cells. While wild-type ORF3a disrupts endosomal acidification and limits cholesterol recycling through interaction with VPS39 (part of the HOPS complex), the Beta variant does not, allowing more efficient cholesterol recovery and stronger LDL responsiveness. Overall, our findings show that SARS-CoV-2 can directly link its replication to host cholesterol levels via ORF3a. This mechanism may help guide antiviral strategies, particularly for individuals with high cholesterol.

Viruses modify cellular membranes to generate replication organelles for protected viral factories. This study reveals that SARS-CoV-2 uses its accessory protein, ORF3a, to feed this demand for membranes by inducing cholesterol uptake via LDLR-mediated endocytosis.

High extracellular cholesterol supports increased SARS-CoV-2 infection. SARS-CoV-2 increases LDL uptake via its ORF3a protein. Beta variant SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a optimizes LDL uptake and hydrolysis.

## Introduction

People with comorbidities like old age, obesity, and type 2 diabetes have an increased likelihood of severe COVID-19 disease and morbidity (Bhaskaran et al, 2021; Bonetti et al, 2003; Collaboration NCDRF 2016; Cummings et al, 2020; Gao et al, 2021; Hamer et al, 2020; Klop et al, 2013; Tamara and Tahapary, 2020). One reason might be hypercholesterolemia, as elevated levels of circulating cholesterol-rich low-density lipoprotein (LDL) correlate with worse patient outcomes. In support, individuals on statin treatment, which lowers circulating LDL, showed less severe disease (Daniels et al, 2020; Tan et al, 2020; Zhang et al, 2020), while individuals with a common genetic variant of apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4), which increases circulating LDL, experienced increased SARS-CoV-2 morbidity and mortality (Ostendorf et al, 2022). Fenofibrate, which induces lipoprotein lipase, has been shown to decrease virally induced lipid accumulation (Ehrlich et al, 2023) and viral entry (Davies et al, 2021). The same study used fenofibrate to treat patients infected with SARS-CoV-2, and they observed increased patient survival (Ehrlich et al, 2023). These clinical observations are replicated in hamster models of diet-induced obesity, which underscore the pathogenic role of LDL in SARS-CoV-2 infection (Ostendorf et al, 2022; Port et al, 2021; Safdari Lord et al, 2022). However, how hypercholesterolemia contributes to increased SARS-CoV-2 infection remains unknown.

In addition to the correlation between COVID-19 severity and circulating cholesterol, there is also evidence that SARS-CoV-2 infection depends on intracellular cholesterol levels. Multiple genome-wide CRISPR screens and viral-host interaction studies have identified intracellular cholesterol homeostasis as a key host pathway for SARS-CoV-2 infection (Gordon et al, 2020a; Gordon et al, 2020b; Hoffmann et al, 2021; Schneider et al, 2021; Stukalov et al, 2021; Wang et al, 2021a; Wei et al, 2021). Intracellular cholesterol supports the entry of several coronaviruses, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and SARS-CoV-2 into cells (de Wilde et al, 2014; Li et al, 2007; Lu et al, 2008; Sanders et al, 2021; Wang et al, 2023). This dependence on intracellular cholesterol has been leveraged by therapeutic strategies using methyl-β-cyclodextrins, which bind and physically remove cholesterol from the plasma membrane (Sanders et al, 2021), or 25-hydroxycholesterol, which sequesters membrane cholesterol into intracellular lipid droplets (Abrams et al, 2020; Wang et al, 2020; Zang et al, 2020); both decreasing viral replication in cell culture models.

In most tissues, cholesterol homeostasis is maintained through exogenous lipoprotein uptake or de novo synthesis (Ikonen, 2008). But some tissues, including lungs, rely on circulating lipoprotein complexes to provide 80% of their cholesterol (Brown and Goldstein, 2009; Fessler and Summer, 2016; Gowdy and Fessler, 2013; Ikonen, 2008; Nistor and Simionescu, 1986; Turley et al, 1981). LDL takes up dietary cholesterol or cholesterol synthesized in the liver (Gotto, 1990; Grundy, 1983) and delivers it to tissues. Conversely, high-density lipoproteins (HDL) act as a recipient of excess intracellular cholesterol and shuttle it to the liver for excretion (Ikonen, 2008). Several observations suggest a link between cholesterol uptake via LDL and SARS-CoV-2 infection. First, protein-protein interaction studies have found that SARS-CoV-2 proteins interact with the LDL receptor (LDLR) and with Neimann-Pick type C2 (NPC2), a chaperone that shuttles free cholesterol from the late endosome to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) or the plasma membrane (Gordon et al, 2020b; Stukalov et al, 2021). Second, genome-wide CRISPR screens showed that LDLR, NPC1, and an acid hydrolase (LIPA) were important host factors for SARS-CoV-2 infection (Daniloski et al, 2021; Schneider et al, 2021; Wang et al, 2021a).

Given the lung’s reliance on LDL uptake for cholesterol homeostasis, and the fact that obese patients, who have increased circulating LDL, are highly susceptible to severe COVID-19, we hypothesize that LDL uptake by lung cells facilitates SARS-CoV-2 infection. Previous studies have focused on how the depletion of cellular cholesterol decreased infection, but not on the mechanism by which the virus uses cholesterol during infection. Critically, how extracellular LDL levels affect viral infection and whether the virus changes intracellular cholesterol metabolism remain unknown. Here, we use LDL uptake assays to demonstrate that the virus benefits from increased extracellular cholesterol at the genomic replication step. Subsequently, we show that the viral protein ORF3a increases LDL uptake, likely through its interaction with LDLR. ORF3a is a putative channel protein normally associated with deacidification of endosomes (Kern et al, 2021; Miao et al, 2021; Michelucci et al, 2025; Zhang et al, 2022a; Zhang et al, 2021). We demonstrate that specific point mutations in ORF3a, associated with increased viral pathogenesis (Nagy et al, 2021) and found in the Beta Variant (i.e., S171L), not only increased LDL uptake, but also endosomal acidification. We propose that SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a induces LDL uptake to compensate for the inefficient LDL hydrolysis resulting from ORF3a-induced deacidification. Overall, our findings may explain the high prevalence of severe COVID-19 among individuals with high levels of circulating cholesterol.

## Results

### Extracellular LDL enhances infection by pathogenic SARS-CoV-2 variants

To test whether SARS-CoV-2 infection directly benefits from extracellular cholesterol, we incubated A549 lung epithelial cells overexpressing the ACE2 receptor (A549-ACE2) or primary human airway organoids (HAOs) with commercially available, serum-isolated lipids—LDL, HDL, very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), or oleic acid, the main precursor for triglycerides—before infecting them with SARS-CoV-2 USA-WA1/2020 (WT) (Fig. 1). The addition of LDL increased viral RNA production in A549-ACE2 cells (Fig. 1A) and in HAOs (Fig. 1B). LDL and VLDL treatment, but not HDL or oleic acid treatment, increased the number of cells with viral RNA and enhanced the overall signal for viral RNA replication across cells (Figs. 1C–F and EV1A–C). At the concentration used, oleic acid induces triglyceride synthesis, without inducing lipid droplet storage, and does not increase infection. As HDL is primarily a recipient cholesterol complex that provides cholesterol via a low-throughput “nibbling” mechanism, these findings suggest that the uptake of cholesterol, rather than its removal, is important for viral infection. To test this hypothesis, we inhibited proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a protein that targets LDLR for degradation, by treating cells with the small molecule inhibitor (R)-IMPP (Petersen et al, 2016). PCSK9 inhibition increased LDLR abundance as expected and enhanced SARS-CoV-2 infection (Figs. 1G and EV1D). Supporting the importance of the cholesterol moiety of LDL, cells treated with purified cholesterol carried by methyl-beta cyclodextrins also showed an increase in SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. EV1E). Together, these results demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 preferentially exploits cholesterol uptake over other lipids to promote infection.

Interestingly, LDL increased infection across all variants of SARS-CoV-2 tested (Fig. 1H). Notably, the Beta variant showed the strongest enhancement in infection, viral particle production, lipid droplet accumulation, and viral RNA accumulation compared to WA1 (Figs. 1H and EV1F–H). During its circulation, the Beta variant caused greater mortality than the ancestral WA1 and Alpha strains (Abu-Raddad et al, 2022; Cohen et al, 2022; Radvak et al, 2021; Tarres-Freixas et al, 2022). In contrast, the Omicron variant showed less infection enhancement in response to LDL (Fig. 1H). Our findings suggest a possible strategy employed by the Beta variant to increase its own replication.

### SARS-CoV-2 RNA replication is boosted by exogenous LDL

The increased pathogenicity of the Beta variant is in part due to its Spike protein, which increases syncytia formation, and hence viral entry (McGrath et al, 2022; Rajah et al, 2021). However, a chimeric virus carrying the Beta Spike in a WT background failed to recapitulate the pathogenic effects of the Beta variant in a mouse model, suggesting that viral proteins other than Spike also contribute to its pathogenicity (McGrath et al, 2022; Rajah et al, 2021). The Beta variant differs from the WT strain at 18 sites: five in ORF1ab, two in ORF3a, and one each in envelope, ORF8, and nucleocapsid, in addition to eight in Spike. We hypothesized that its increased sensitivity to LDL was due to one or more of the ten mutations outside Spike.

To determine at which step of the viral life cycle LDL benefits infection, we used several subgenomic infection systems. To measure viral entry, we used pseudotyped viruses (PTV), which are replication-defective lentiviral particles that express the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein and deliver luciferase RNA to recipient cells through the ACE2 receptor (Deng et al, 2021). LDL exposure did not increase the entry of these PTVs into target cells that expressed only ACE2 (A549-ACE2) or cells that expressed ACE2 along with the transmembrane protease, serine 2 (TMPRSS2) enzyme necessary for Spike priming (HEK293T-ACE2/TMPRSS2 cells) (Figs. 2A and EV2A). This finding contrasts with previous studies that primarily focused on viral entry (Wang et al, 2023), which found that cholesterol-rich lipoproteins increase viral infection. Experimental setups with different lipoproteins, cell models, and viral systems may explain the differences in our observations. Then, we used virus-like particles (VLP) (Syed et al, 2021), which express all viral structural proteins and model the assembly and egress of authentic SARS-CoV-2 particles. LDL treatment did not affect VLP production, as measured by comparable delivery of luciferase RNA into target cells (Fig. 2B). In both systems, spike expression was required for luciferase signal as bald (without spike) particles were unable to deliver cargo into target cells (- Ctl).

Finally, to measure viral genomic replication, we used a replicon system for SARS-CoV-2 in which the envelope (E) protein is deleted and the accessory proteins ORF7a and ORF7b are replaced with a secreted nanoluciferase (Taha et al, 2023). Treatment with LDL increased the nanoluciferase signal in the cultures transfected with the replicon, indicating that RNA replication was stimulated by LDL treatment independent of viral entry or assembly (Figs. 2C and EV2B). In support of this, replicon-infected, LDL-treated cells contained over larger double-stranded RNA (dsRNA; green) complexes (Fig. EV2C) and more viral RNA compared to untreated, infected cells (Fig. EV2D). Additionally, LDL-treated, WT virus-infected A549-ACE2 cells had increased dsRNA staining compared to untreated cells, illustrating increased viral replication complexes and reinforcing our replicon data (Fig. EV2E). A previous study demonstrated the reorganization and colocalization of Filipin and dsRNA staining during HCov-229E infection (Trimarco et al, 2021), suggesting that coronaviral RNA replication requires increased cholesterol. We confirmed that LDL affected a post-entry step by treating A549-ACE2 cells with LDL after they had been exposed to infectious virus for 1 h. Post-infection LDL treatment led to a 3-fold increase in viral replication for the WA1 strain and a fourfold increase for the Beta strain after 72 h, supporting the model that LDL promotes sustained viral replication (Figs. 2D and EV2F,G).

Finally, to examine ultrastructural changes during viral replication, we examined intracellular membranes that function as hubs for viral RNA replication using correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM). SARS-CoV-2 replication occurs in the double membrane vesicles (DMVs) (Knoops et al, 2008) and causes substantial reorganization of host intracellular membranes (Fig. 2E). We observed an increased abundance and density of DMVs, and an increased number of small open double-membrane spherules, and assembling virions (Maier et al, 2013; Snijder et al, 2020) in the infected, LDL-treated cells compared to untreated controls (Fig. 2F). Double membrane spherules remain elusive (Maier et al, 2013; Snijder et al, 2020). The enrichment of these membranous structures during LDL treatment supports the model in which LDL-derived cholesterol during infection enhances the formation of intracellular membranes important for viral RNA replication.

### SARS-CoV-2 infection and expression of ORF3a alone enhance LDL uptake

Our data demonstrate that LDL and cholesterol enhance SARS-CoV-2 infection, we next examined whether SARS-CoV-2 infection, with or without LDL addition, altered other lipids of A549-ACE2 cells using thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Strikingly, esterified cholesterols were enriched in infected cells compared to uninfected controls, regardless of LDL treatment (Fig. 3A). Cholesterol esters are the more stable storage form of cholesterol, found in lipid droplets and transported in LDL particles. To determine if this enrichment is caused by enhanced cholesterol uptake, we treated cells with fluorescently labeled LDL (DiI-LDL) and measured intracellular fluorescence. Infection of A549-ACE2 cells increased LDL uptake by ~20% relative to uninfected controls, supporting the model that viral infection optimizes LDL uptake for infection enhancement (Fig. 3B). Consistent with this model, infected cells expressed higher levels of key cholesterol uptake genes, such as the LDLR and NPC1, suggesting that viral replication requires replenishment of cellular cholesterol through LDL uptake (Fig. EV3A). Small molecule inhibition of oxysterol-binding protein (OSBP) prevents movement of cholesterol between the ER and Golgi apparatus, a function required for normal cholesterol homeostasis, and lowers coronavirus replication (Ma-Lauer et al, 2024). Combined with our data, this suggests that LDL uptake maximizes infection by maintaining viral replication organelles.

To identify the viral factor responsible for enhanced LDL uptake, we expressed SARS-CoV-2 proteins individually in A549 cells and measured the relative rate of DiI-LDL uptake (Fig. 3C). While some proteins significantly decrease DiI-LDL uptake, SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a expression alone increased LDL uptake by over 30%, similar to what was observed during viral infection (Fig. 3B,C). SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a is a small glycosylated transmembrane protein that forms a homo-tetrameric ion channel, which inhibits the fusion of late endosomes and lysosomes, preventing their acidification, and assisting in viral egress (Bignon et al, 2022; Chen et al, 2021; Ghosh et al, 2020; Hayn et al, 2021; Kern et al, 2021; Miao et al, 2021; Michelucci et al, 2025; Oostra et al, 2006; Solvik et al, 2022; Zhang et al, 2022a). It was determined that ORF3a interferes with the GTP hydrolysis cycle of Rab7, which benefits virion production (Walia et al, 2024). Additionally, ORF3a is a major viral pathogenesis factor as it downregulates MHC-I and inhibits JAK/STAT signaling to dampen the antiviral response (Arshad et al, 2023; Ghosh et al, 2020; Miao et al, 2021; Wang et al, 2021b; Xu et al, 2022; Zhang et al, 2021). Most notably, viruses with an orf3a deletion cause lower mortality in animal models (Liu et al, 2022). Our data show a new function of ORF3a–enhanced LDL uptake–that may be linked to its role in pathogenesis.

### ORF3a interacts with mature LDLR in endosomes

In an unbiased affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS) study, ORF3a was shown to bind to a number of host proteins, including the LDLR (Stukalov et al, 2021) (Fig. 3D). We confirmed this interaction by co-expressing FLAG-tagged ORF3a and GFP-tagged LDLR in HEK293T cells. LDLR internalizes with LDL through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, where dissociation and cholesterol recovery are induced by acidification of the endosomal lumen (Davis et al, 1987; Vos and van de Sluis, 2021). Indeed, confocal microscopy confirmed that SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a and host LDLR colocalized at intracellular endosomes (Fig. 3E). After immunoprecipitation, we identified LDLR as a bona fide ORF3a-interacting protein (Fig. 3F). Notably, expression of ORF3a alone induced the appearance of higher molecular bands for LDLR, indicating the accumulation of the mature receptor (Cummings et al, 1983; Davis et al, 1986; Ishibashi et al, 1993; Kulseth et al, 2010; Li et al, 2004; Maxwell et al, 2005; Tveten et al, 2013). This mature form of LDLR was specifically co-immunoprecipitated by ORF3a, suggesting that ORF3a is not interacting with newly synthesized LDLR in the ER, but rather induces the accumulation of mature LDLR in the cell. In support of this model, we found that expression of ORF3a prevented binding of LDLR to PCSK9, a chaperone targeting mature LDLR for degradation (Maxwell et al, 2005), thus enhancing its half-life in cells expressing ORF3a (Fig. EV3B,C).

In contrast, interactions of LDLR with components of the COMMD/CCDC22/CCDC93 (CCC) and Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein and SCAR homologue (WASH) complexes, involved in the recycling of internalized LDLR and other receptors back to the cell surface (Bartuzi et al, 2016), were not altered by ORF3a expression (Fig. EV3D). We confirmed that LDLR is a key component of the viral life cycle by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knock-out of LDLR, NPC1, and NPC2 in A549-ACE2 cells, which decreased viral infections (Fig. EV3E).

### ORF3a is required for effective SARS-CoV-2 replication in vitro and in vivo

To test the role of ORF3a in mediating LDL’s effects on infection, we generated an infectious clone (Taha et al, 2023) of the ancestral WA1 strain of SARS-CoV-2 harboring a mutation to abrogate ORF3a expression. Unlike previous studies, which delete the entire orf3a RNA (Liu et al, 2022), we introduced a premature stop codon at the first amino acid position (M*), blocking ORF3a translation, while minimizing potential changes to viral genomic structure. When the mutant and WT virus-infected A549-ACE2 cells were pretreated with LDL, we noted a decrease in LDL responsiveness in the absence of ORF3a expression, confirming the critical role of ORF3a in LDL-mediated enhancement of viral infection (Fig. 3G).

To examine this effect in vivo, we infected Golden Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), which were preconditioned with either a normal or a high cholesterol (0.5% cholesterol and 15% lard) diet for 2 weeks prior to infection with WT and ORF3a-STOP infectious clones (Figs. 3H and EV4A). WT-infected animals responded to the high cholesterol diet with a significant increase in viral infection and replication, while animals infected with the ORF3a-STOP virus did not, confirming the critical role of ORF3a and circulating cholesterol in enhanced viral replication in vivo (Figs. 3H and EV4B–D). Notably, the ORF3a-STOP virus propagated less efficiently in animals on a normal cholesterol diet, underscoring ORF3a’s critical role in viral infection (Fig. EV4B) (Zhang et al, 2021).

### ORF3a from the Beta variant changes the acidification of LDL-positive endosomes

The Beta variant, which responded to LDL treatment with the largest increase in viral replication (Fig. 1H), contains two unique point mutations in ORF3a: Q57H and S171L (Fig. 4A). The WT- and Beta-ORF3a proteins effectively co-immunoprecipitated and colocalized with LDLR, as did the ORF3a proteins carrying either one of the two Beta point mutations (Fig. EV5A,B). In contrast, LDL uptake depended on the ORF3a genotype, with Beta and S171L ORF3a proteins enhancing cholesterol accumulation compared to control cells (Fig. EV5C). Similarly, we found that Calu-3 cell lines that stably expressed the WT or mutant ORF3a proteins enhanced infection by the ORF3a-STOP virus, with the Beta variant and the S171L ORF3a mutants showing the largest increases (Fig. 4B). Strikingly, the ORF3a genotype did not change the surface presentation of LDLR, supporting the model that receptor recycling through the CCC/WASH complexes remains unaltered (Fig. EV6).

We hypothesized that the enhanced LDL uptake and viral fitness conferred by Beta ORF3a was due to a secondary step downstream of LDLR engagement and recycling. To determine if Beta ORF3a bound host proteins differ from WT, we performed AP-MS using both proteins. This analysis identified VPS39–a member of the HOPS complex (Miao et al, 2021) and previously identified as a host interaction partner of ORF3a (Gordon et al, 2020b)–as highly enriched for binding to WT, but not Beta ORF3a (Fig. 4C). This was confirmed in co-immunoprecipitation experiments where binding of the FLAG-tagged Beta ORF3a to VPS39 was abrogated (Fig. 4D). Importantly, the S171L mutation alone weakened the interaction markedly, confirming that VPS39 binds to the C-terminus of ORF3a (Miller et al, 2023) (Fig. 4D).

As previously described, a function of the interaction of ancestral ORF3a and VPS39 is the deacidification of host endosomes (Chen et al, 2021)—an activity that likely diminishes cholesterol hydrolysis from LDL—we tested whether the point mutations in Beta ORF3a affect endosome acidity. A549 cells expressing the different ORF3a genotypes were treated with LDL-pHrodo red, an LDL particle with an intercalating dye that fluoresces in an acidic environment (Kemmer et al, 2015; Kitano et al, 2020; Zhang et al, 2022b). While LDL puncta were visible under all conditions, including in control cells transduced with the empty vector alone, WT ORF3a decreased the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the puncta, consistent with its known deacidification activity (Fig. 4E,F). In contrast, the Beta and S171L mutants significantly increased the MFI of LDL-pHrodo Red, supporting the model that enhanced acidification of LDL-positive endosomes and more efficient LDL hydrolysis led to optimized LDL uptake by Beta variant mutants (Fig. 4E,F).

## Discussion

Our findings support a model in which the SARS-CoV-2 accessory protein ORF3a acts as an LDL uptake factor facilitating the availability of cholesterol for viral RNA replication. We further show that natural viral evolution in the Beta lineage has optimized the full usage of circulating cholesterol for enhancing viral infection (Fig. 4G). During infection with the WT virus, ORF3a binds and stabilizes LDLR at host endosomes by decreasing PCSK9 interactions, which leads to an increase in LDL uptake and enhanced genomic replication. However, due to endosomal deacidification by the WT ORF3a, the LDLR does not release its cholesterol optimally for cellular uses. In contrast, during Beta variant infection, the mutant ORF3a binds LDLR and restores endosome acidification, leading to optimized cholesterol uptake to better support viral infection. This provides a molecular link between physical conditions with high circulating cholesterol, such as hypercholesterolemia, obesity and severity of COVID-19, which was clinically striking in the early days of the pandemic (Bhaskaran et al, 2021; Bonetti et al, 2003; Collaboration NCDRF 2016; Cummings et al, 2020; Gao et al, 2021; Hamer et al, 2020; Klop et al, 2013; Tamara and Tahapary, 2020).

Previous studies have demonstrated that ORF3a localizes to lysosomes, damages their membrane stability, and prevents the acidification of endosomes (Bignon et al, 2022; Chen et al, 2021; Hayn et al, 2021; Kern et al, 2021; Miao et al, 2021; Michelucci et al, 2025; Solvik et al, 2022). Our data, combined with these findings suggest that ORF3a interacts with LDLR when ORF3a-containing, damaged lysosomes attempt to fuse with LDLR-containing incoming endosomes, but that the fusion remains ineffective for LDL hydrolysis. Alternatively, since SARS-CoV-2 has been proposed to cause lysosomal exocytosis for viral egress, the interaction of ORF3a with LDLR could occur at the plasma membrane before endocytosis (Chen et al, 2021; Ghosh et al, 2020). However, since we observe ORF3a and LDLR colocalizing intracellularly, we favor the hypothesis of an endosomal interaction.

Our data show that (1) SARS-CoV-2 infection increases with PCSK9 inhibition, (2) ORF3a enriches LDLR, (3) ORF3a decreases the PCSK9-LDLR interaction, and (4) ORF3a decreases endosomal acidification; however, the mechanisms by which ORF3a prevents the PCSK9-LDLR interaction remain unknown. We propose three complementary mechanisms. First, ORF3a-driven LDLR enrichment may crowd out PCSK9 binding in the endosomes; or ORF3a may sterically hinder the LDLR beta-propeller conformation, directly dislodging PCSK9 (Kwon et al, 2008; Lo Surdo et al, 2011). Second, since PCSK9 binding to LDLR blocks the conformational change required for SNX17-mediated recycling in acidic endosomes, ORF3a’s pH elevation may itself reduce PCSK9 binding (Guan et al, 2025). Third, ORF3a-induced dense body formation sequesters membranes and proteins from early endosomes and the trans-Golgi (Hartmann et al, 2025), two sites of known-PCSK9-LDLR interactions, likely disrupting structures that normally facilitate receptor degradation. Regardless of the precise mechanism, we propose that the ORF3a-LDLR interaction in the endosomes is primarily the site of PCSK9 inhibition.

SARS-CoV ORF3a is 73% identical to its SARS-CoV-2 counterpart, suggesting that it may also benefit from increased extracellular LDL (Zhang et al, 2022a). It also rearranges cellular membranes, interferes with intracellular trafficking, and is a proposed channel protein, suggesting that it could also enhance LDL uptake (Freundt et al, 2010; Lu et al, 2006; Yu et al, 2004). However, one major difference between the two proteins is an unstructured loop present in SARS-CoV-2 ORF3a that includes residue S171–a region that interacts with VPS39 and allows it to prevent lysosomal fusion or endosomal acidification (Miller et al, 2023). Future studies are needed to delineate the relevance of this newly defined viral-host interaction (ORF3a-LDLR) across different coronaviruses.

How the need for endosome acidification and deacidification is balanced during infection requires further studies. Notably, the ion channel functionality of ORF3a has been recently called into doubt (Miller et al, 2023). Instead, ORF3a has been suggested as a water-permeable channel that induces lysosomal swelling as a mechanism of deacidification (Michelucci et al, 2025). How S171L changes the water permissibility of ORF3a remains unknown and may explain its opposing deacidification profile in the Beta variant. Deacidification is thought to benefit viral egress (Chen et al, 2021; Ghosh et al, 2020) and that might explain why Q57H and S171L mutations were not maintained in other viral variants as they hinder this process. In addition, we speculate that as LDL uptake and hydrolysis may be linked to ORF3a’s role as a pathogenesis factor, the virus may not have evolved to strengthen this function as it is not beneficial for viral attenuation and survival of the host.

Our TEM experiments of infected cells showed potential morphological changes to endo/lysosomal structures (Fig. 2E,F). This is in line with previous reports which: (1) determined that the formation of ORF3a-induced dense bodies is dependent on the c-terminal domain containing S171L; however, how these structures relate to intracellular cholesterol requires further studies (Hartmann et al, 2025). (2) Another study identified ORF3a-Filipin III-LAMP1 positive structures and determined that NPC2 trafficking and subsequent liberation of lysosomal cholesterol was impaired by ORF3a (Goodson et al, 2026). We further show that different Orf3a genotypes affect endosomal/lysosomal acidification, which is a major function of that subcellular compartment (Fig. 4E,F). These observations strengthen our interpretation that the Beta variant has optimized LDL uptake and hydrolysis to improve its replication.

The decreased enhancement of LDL-mediated infection in the Omicron variant, which is less pathogenic but more transmissible, supports this model. The Omicron BA.1 variant has WT ORF3a and none of its subvariants carries the ORF3a mutations found in the Beta variant. However, a recent report determined that Omicron decreases ORF3a expression at the RNA and protein levels relative to the ancestral strain, which might explain the decrease in Omicron sensitivity to LDL (Bouhaddou et al, 2023). Notably, the T233I mutation in ORF3a in Omicron BA.2 and all its subvariants, including XBB.1.5 is an interesting candidate and for future studies.

Beyond ORF3a, other SARS-CoV-2 proteins (NSP1, NSP2, NSP6, NSP12) significantly decreased LDL uptake (Fig. 3C). Additionally, some viral proteins were not tested including NSP3, NSP9, NSP11, NSP16, and ORF7a, further exploration into their effects on LDL uptake is required. Yet during infection, LDL uptake is increased overall suggesting that the ORF3a effect is dominant. Notably, this effect may not exclusively be due to ORF3a which may work in concert with other viral proteins to enhance this benefit. Future studies should investigate how these viral proteins interact to collectively shape LDL uptake during infection.

### Ethics statement

All animal experiments and procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and policies of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), under approved protocol #AN209208-00. All studies involving live SARS CoV-2 and infected Golden Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus) were performed in the Gladstone Animal Biosafety Level 3 (ABSL-3) facility under protocols approved by the UCSF Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC) and authorized under Biosafety Use Authorization (BUA) #BU184109-05A.

### Cell lines

A549, HEK293T, HEK293T-ACE2/TMPRSS (Deng et al, 2021), BHK21, and Vero-ACE2/TMPRSS2 (Taha et al, 2023) cells were cultured in complete DMEM (DMEM with 10% Serum Plus II, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% glutamine). A549-ACE2 cells were made as described (Wang et al, 2021a) and cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10 µg/mL blasticidin. Calu-3 cells were cultured in Advanced MEM (MEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% GlutaMax). Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

### Culture of human airway organoids (HAOs)

To generate HAOs, input cells were used from whole-lung lavages or from digestion of whole lungs. HAOs were generated from these cells as previously described (Sachs et al, 2019; Zhou et al, 2018). Briefly, single cells were suspended in 65% reduced growth factor BME2 (Basement Membrane Extract, Type 2, Trevigen, catalogue number 3533-001-02). From this mixture, 50 µL drops containing 1000–40,000 cells were seeded in 24-well suspension culture plates (GreinerBio-one, catalogue number 662-102). Drops were incubated at 37 °C for >20 min and solidified. After this, 500 µL of HAO media was added to each well. HAO media is organoid basal media (Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% Glutamax, and 10 mM HEPES) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) R-spondin1 conditioned media, 1% B27 (Gibco), 25 ng/mL Noggin (Peprotech), 1.25 mM N-Acetylcysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 mM Nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich), 5 nM Herefulin beta-1 (Peprotech), and 100 µg/mL Primocin (InvivoGen). HAO media was further supplemented with 5 µM Y-27632, 500 nM A83-01, 500 nM SB202190, 25 ng/mL FGF-7, 100 ng/mL FGF-10 (all from Stem Cell Technologies), and HAO media was replaced every 3–4 days.

After 14–21 days, organoids were passaged. For this, cold basal media was used to collect organoids in 15 ml Falcon tubes and dissolve BME2. Tubes were centrifuged at 250×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Media was aspirated, 10x TrypLE Select (Gibco) was added to the organoids, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5–10 min. Organoids were further dissociated by pipette mixing and then diluted in cold basal media. After another spin and media aspiration, cells were mixed with BME2 and seeded into new drops. After this initial passage, organoids were passaged every 10–21 days. Stocks of early-passage (P1-P3) organoid lines were prepared by dissociating organoids, mixing them with recovery cell culture freezing media (Gibco), and freezing them by standard procedures. These samples could be thawed and immediately cultured in HAO media.

### Viral infection

In total, 30,000 A549-ACE2 cells (acquired from Synthego) were plated into black-wall 96-well plates. For infection, DMEM with 5% serum plus for infection was used with or without 4 µg/ml LDL (ThermoFisher), 4 µg/ml HDL (Sigma), or 10 µM oleic acid (Sigma). Infections were carried out at an MOI of 0.1 as measured by plaque assays, for 72 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for ≥30 min at RT or in TRIZOL-RNA STAT60.

### Chemical induction of LDLR

In total, 30,000 A549-ACE2 cells (acquired from Synthego) were plated into black wall-96-well plates. Twenty-four hours prior to infection, cells were treated with 0, 0.78, 6.25, and 25 µM (R)-IMPP (Cayman Chemicals) and 4 µg/ml DiI-LDL (ThermoFisher). Infections were carried out at an MOI of 0.1 as measured by plaque assays, for 72 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for ≥30 min at RT.

### Quantification of viral transcripts

RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Zymo), cDNA synthesized, and 1 ng of cDNA used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) with previously published Taqman Primers, Probes, and N or E standard curves (Fozouni et al, 2021).

### Detection of viral infection

Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% TritonX-100, blocked with 3% BSA in PBS, and stained with primary antibodies (J2 Ms-anti-dsRNA, 1:200), secondary antibodies (Goat-anti-Ms-AF647, 1:500), and Hoechst nuclear stain (1:2000). Cells were imaged and analyzed by high content imaging, Molecular Devices ImageXpress Confocal microscope at ×10 or ×40. The fluorescence of dsRNA puncta was used to identify infected cells.

### Plasmid generation

Plasmids were cloned by PCR amplification of genes from previously published plasmids encoding the SARS-CoV-2 orfeome (Gordon et al, 2020b). Vector backbone is a modified pLVX302 plasmid expressing BFP, using an EF1a promoter to express the FLAG tagged viral protein. Viral genes were inserted into the vector backbone by HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB). Site directed mutagenesis (NEB) was used to make point mutants in ORF3a. These plasmids were combined with packing and envelope expressing plasmids and transfected into HEK293Ts with XtremeGene9 (Sigma-Aldrich). Supernatant was collected and quantified using LentiX-p24 titration assays (Takara Biosciences). A549 cells were transduced for 48 h with subsequent drug selection for stable expressing cell lines.

### Replicon assay

In all, 100,000 BHK21 cells were plated in 12-well plates, transfected with 2 µg of plasmids with a 3:1 ratio of XtremeGene9 (Sigma) incubated at RT for 30–60 min. Twenty-four hours post transfection, media was washed and replaced with fresh media. Seventy-two hours post transfection, supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane. In all, 10,000 A549-ACE2 or Vero-ACE2/TMPRSS2 cells in a 96-well plate were infected with filtered supernatant, nanoluciferase was measured 48 h post infection.

### Correlative light and electron microscopy

In total, 500,000 Vero-ACE2/TMPRSS cells were grown for correlative light and electron microscopy on gridded 35 mm MatTek glass-bottom dishes (#P35G-1.5-14-C-GRD MatTek Corp., Ashland, MA, USA). Cells were infected with 10 µl single-round mNeonGreen in the presence or absence of LDL for 24 h. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 10 min at RT and stained with Hoechst for nuclei. Following fluorescence imaging, cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2.5% paraformaldehyde in PBS, pH 7.2. Samples were rinsed (3×; 5 min, Room Temperature) in 1× PBS and immersed in 1% osmium tetroxide with 1.6% potassium ferricyanide in 1× PBS for 30 min. Samples were rinsed (3×; 5 min, RT) in buffer and briefly washed with distilled water (1×; 1 min, RT) before sample were then subjected to an ascending ethanol gradient followed by pure ethanol. Samples were progressively infiltrated (using ethanol as the solvent) with Epon resin (EMS, Hatfield, PA, USA) and polymerized at 60 °C for 24–48 h. Care was taken to ensure only a thin amount of resin remained within the glass bottom dishes to enable the best possible chance for separation of the glass coverslip. Following polymerization, the glass coverslips were removed using ultra-thin Personna razor blades (EMS, Hatfield, PA, USA) and liquid nitrogen exposure, as needed. To enable correlative light and electron microscopy, specific cells, identified by the gridded alpha-numerical labeling on the plates documented during fluorescence imaging, were carefully removed, precisely trimmed and mounted on a blank resin block with cyanoacrylate glue for sectioning. Serial thin sections (80 nm) were cut using a Leica UC6 ultramicrotome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) from the surface of the block until ~4–5 microns in to ensure complete capture of the cell volumes. Section-ribbons were then collected sequentially onto formvar-coated slot grids. The grids were post-stained with 2% uranyl acetate followed by Reynold’s lead citrate, for 5 min each. The sections were imaged using a FEI Tecnai 12 120 kV TEM (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) and data recorded using a Gatan Rio 16 CMOS camera with Gatan Microscopy Suite software (Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA).

### Co-immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were transfected with respective plasmids and XtremeGene9 (1:3; Sigma) for 72 h. Cells were collected, protein quantified by DC Assay (Bio-Rad), and 2 µg of protein was loaded onto 100 µl of M2 FLAG magnetic beads (Sigma) or Protein A Dynabeads (Invitrogen; incubated with Rb-anti-LDLR (ProteinTech) for 15 min rotating at RT) for 18–24 h at 4 °C while rotating. Beads were washed six times, eluted with 3× FLAG peptide (Sigma) for 30 min at 4 °C shaking or incubated with loading buffer for 10 min at 70 °C shaking, 5 µg of input and IP were loaded onto a Criterion acrylamide gel (Bio-Rad) for Western blot analysis.

### Localization of ORF3a and LDLR by immunofluorescence

HEK293T cells were transfected with ORF3a-FLAG and pBa-LSS-GFP-LDLR WT (Addgene #98184) expressing plasmids as above for 48 h. Cells were lifted and plated onto poly-L-lysine treated coverslips for 24 h. Wells were treated with DiI-LDL as described below, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% TritonX-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 30 min. Coverslips were stained with primary antibodies (Ms-anti-M2 FLAG, 1:200), secondary antibodies (Goat-anti-Ms-AF647, 1:500), and Hoechst nuclear stain (1:500). Coverslips were imaged at ×60 using an Olympus FV3000RS scanning confocal microscope. Imaris 9.9.1 Colocalization function was used for quantification.

### Low density lipoprotein uptake assay

Infected A549-ACE2 cells were treated with 4 µg/ml DiI-LDL (ThermoFisher) at 68 hpi for 4 h at 37 °C. Transduced A549 cells were treated with 4 µg/ml DiI-LDL (ThermoFisher) for 4 h at 37 °C. Treated cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min, cells were washed repeatedly with PBS, counterstained with Hoechst (1:500) and dsRNA (infected cells, as above), imaged and analyzed by high content imaging on a Molecular Devices ImageXpress Confocal microscope at ×10 and ×40. DiI-LDL puncta were counted by the granularity analysis and relative number of puncta to uninfected/untransduced A549 cells was calculated.

### Low density lipoprotein acidification assay

Transduced A549 cells were treated with 4 µg/ml LDL-pHrodo Red (ThermoFisher) for 4 h at 37 °C. Treated cells were washed repeatedly with HBSS, counterstained with Hoechst dye (ThermoFisher), and live imaged and analyzed by high content imaging, Molecular Devices ImageXpress Confocal microscope at ×10. LDL-pHrodo Red puncta were counted and scored by the granularity analysis. Relative number of puncta to untransduced A549 cells was calculated.

### Total cellular cholesterol assay

Cells were fixed with fixation buffer (4% paraformaldehyde and 1% methanol in Tris-HCl Buffer) for 10 min, washed with Tris-HCl Buffer with 1% Tween20, and detected in Filipin III in Tris-HCl (1:100, Cayman Chemicals) for 1 h. Samples were washed as above, imaged and analyzed by high content imaging as described above. Fluorescent intensity of Filipin III staining was measured and compared to that of untransduced A549 cells was calculated.

### Thin-layer chromatography

Equal number of cells were collected, lipids extracted with Extraction Buffer (hexanes-isopropanol; 3:2; Abcam) for 30 min with vortexing, samples spun down, solubilized lipids collected in supernatant, dried down at 37 °C. Samples were resuspended in chloroform-methanol (2:1), HPTLC silicagel60 plate (Supelco) primed with dichloromethane: methanol (1:1), samples loaded, run with a Mangold mobile phase (petroleum ether-diethyl ether-glacial acetic acid; 40:10:0.5), developed in 5% phosphomolybdic acid in ethanol with heating at 120 °C for 30 min, densitometry performed in ImageJ 1.51 s. Lipid standards diluted in chloroform-methanol (2:1) to 40 µg/µl, 2.5 µl spotted per lane. Control A (Cholesterol solution; Sigma) and Control B (Non-polar Lipid Mixture 2; Cayman Chemicals).

### Protein stability assays

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with GFP-LDLR and wild-type ORF3a or empty vector for 48 h. Samples were treated with either DMSO or 50 µg/ml cycloheximide for 3, 6, 12, or 24 h. Protein samples were collected, quantified by DC assay (Bio-Rad), analyzed by Western blot, and band intensities were measured by ImageJ.

### Golden Syrian hamster infections

Eight-week-old male hamsters (Charles River) were fed high cholesterol (15% lard, 0.5% cholesterol) or normal chow for 2 weeks. Animals were intranasally infected with 10 3 PFU/animal of SARS-CoV-2 WA1 or ORF3a-STOP for 48 h. Tissues were collected for lipid analysis by TLC, plaque assays, and histology (HistoWiz). Immunohistochemistry (HistoWiz) with anti-Nucleocapsid antibodies (GeneTex), area and intensity of IHC were measured using Fiji 17-13-18-405. N = 5 animals per group. All animals were housed at AAALAC accredited ABSL3 facility of Gladstone Institutes. The experiment was carried out in accordance with the UCSF Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the recommendations of the Panel on Euthanasia of the American Veterinary Medical Association. All protocols concerning animal use were approved (AN209208-00) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use committees at the University of California, San Francisco and Gladstone Institutes and conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animal (Council, 2011). Golden Syrian hamsters were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled pathogen-free facility with 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory rodent chow.

### LDLR surface expression

Transduced A549-ACE2 cells were transduced with lentiviruses with wild-type or mutant ORF3a or empty vector controls. Cells were lifted with TryplE (Gibco), stained with eFluor780 viability dye (Invitrogen eBiosciences), and anti-LDLR-PE (LDLR) or isotype control-PE (ISO) antibodies (R&D Systems) in the presence (P) or absence of 0.1% saponin permeabilization. Surface and total LDLR staining was measured on a BD Fortessa X-20 and analyzed with FlowJo.

### Affinity purified mass spectrometry

HEK293T cells were transfected with wild-type or Beta variant ORF3a or empty vector for 48 h. Samples were collected, anti-FLAG purification, and analyzed by mass spectrometry as previously described (Gordon et al, 2020b).

### Quantification and statistical analysis

The number of experiments and replicates are indicated in individual figure legends. Data were processed and visualized using GraphPad Prism. All quantified data are represented mean ± SEM, as indicated, and quantification details are available in figure legends. Western blot band intensities were measured by ImageJ. For animal experiments, to estimate the minimum number of animals needed to reliably get the desired statistical significance (P < 0.05) we performed power analysis and determined the animal number required for the experiment.
