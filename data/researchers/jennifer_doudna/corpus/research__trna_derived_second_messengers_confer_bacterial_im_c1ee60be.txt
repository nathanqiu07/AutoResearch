# tRNA-derived second messengers confer bacterial immunity

## Abstract

Bacterial small-molecule messengers that function in diverse immune signaling pathways have been thought to derive strictly from biosynthesis using nucleotide and cofactor substrates. Here we report a nuclease-driven immune signaling pathway in which tRNA cleavage generates a specific small molecule that triggers cell death in response to RNA-guided transcript recognition in prokaryotes. We identify a class of type VI-B CRISPR systems in which the RNA-guided nuclease Cas13 and its accessory transmembrane protein, 2TMβ, are co-encoded in an operon and are jointly required for bacteriophage defense. Upon target RNA recognition, Cas13 activates 2TMβ by producing mono- and dinucleotide messengers bearing 2′,3′-cyclic uridine phosphate ends by tRNA cleavage. Activated 2TMβ destroys infected cells by forming a multimeric complex that disrupts cell membranes to mediate cell death. These findings demonstrate RNA-programmed tRNA cleavage for second-messenger biogenesis, revealing a new logic for prokaryotic immune signaling.

## Introduction

Immune systems across all domains of life convert pathogen detection into diffusible chemical signals that activate cellular responses to infection. In bacteria, defense systems including CBASS, Thoeris, Pycsar, and type III CRISPR systems share a common logic in which infection triggers enzymatic production of a second messenger that binds to and activates downstream effectors 1–5. Recent studies in prokaryotes have expanded the known types of signaling molecules 6–8 and revealed that the signaling logic can be inverted to communicate safety rather than danger 9,10. Despite this diversity, all known prokaryotic immune signaling pathways rely on dedicated enzymes that generate small-molecule signals from free nucleotides and cofactors 11.

Eukaryotes generate small signaling molecules by processing larger precursors, including lipids and nucleic acids. During viral infection, the ribonuclease RNase L cleaves cellular and viral RNA into small fragments that activate the cytosolic receptor RIG-I to amplify the antiviral response 12. During bacterial infection, the ribonuclease angiogenin cleaves the anticodon loops of tRNAs to generate tRNA halves that act as ligands for the endosomal Toll-like receptor 7 13. Plant Toll/interleukin-1 receptor/resistance (TIR) domain containing receptors likewise hydrolyze nucleic acids to generate 2′,3′-cyclic mononucleotides that promote immune cell death 14. In these cases, a nuclease that liberates signals from macromolecules, rather than an enzyme acting on a precursor pool, produces the signal. Whether the same strategy is employed in prokaryotic immune defense has remained unknown.

Comparative genomic analysis hinted that some prokaryotic defense systems might use a nuclease-based signaling strategy. RNA-guided RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas13 systems can encode accessory genes that enhance immunity in a manner dependent on Cas13 nuclease activity, although how Cas13 activation is communicated to the accessory has been unresolved 15. In this study, we identified Cas13 nucleases associated with the small transmembrane protein 2TMβ, whose membrane-disrupting activity is regulated by binding to cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) produced by nucleotidyltransferases in CBASS and Panoptes bacterial immune systems 9,10. 2TMβ’s unexpected inclusion in some Cas13 operons implied an alternative mechanism of activation that might instead rely on nucleolytic activity.

Here we used a combination of genetic, biochemical and cell-based experiments to explore the relationship between Cas13 and 2TMβ, revealing a two-component nuclease-driven signaling pathway essential for viral defense. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that in a 2TMβ-cas13 system, RNA-guided transcript binding activates Cas13 as a tRNA-specific nuclease that generates cyclic-phosphate-containing products, including a guanylyl-(3′–5′)-uridine-2′,3′-cyclic monophosphate (GpU(2′,3′-cp)) linear dinucleotide and uridine-2′,3′-cyclic monophosphate (2′,3′-cUMP) mononucleotide. 2′,3′-cUMP and GpU(2′,3′-cp) bind to 2TMβ to promote its oligomerization into a membrane-perturbing structure that causes cell death. These results establish a new axis in bacterial immune signaling in which a nuclease-generated, tRNA-derived second messenger activates a dedicated effector to mount a defense response.

## Results

### A two-component type VI defense module

During investigation of diverse type VI CRISPR immune systems, which employ RNA-guided transcript recognition to initiate a response to viral infection, we noted some clades with colocalized genes encoding Cas13b and 2TMβ (Fig. 1a, b; Extended Data Fig. 1a). Two such representative loci, from Chitinophaga sp. and Chryseobacterium sp., encode deep-branching Cas13b effectors (Cas13b6 and Cas13b7) that define lineages we designated type VI-B-6 and VI-B-7; we refer to these two-gene systems as 2TMβ-cas13b6 and 2TMβ-cas13b7 (Extended Data Fig. 1b). Further genomic analysis revealed examples of 2TMβs encoded next to TIR domains or phosphodiesterases, raising the possibility that diverse enzymatic processes might generate ligands that regulate 2TMβ activity (Extended Data Fig. 1c, d).

We tested whether 2TMβ-cas13 gene associations reflect a functional phage defense pathway by expressing the 2TMβ-cas13b6 and 2TMβ-cas13b7 systems bearing phage-targeting CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) in E. coli and assessing their ability to provide immunity against a panel of lytic phages (Fig. 1b, c). Expression of the full 2TMβ-cas13b6 locus reduced the efficiency of plating by up to 4 orders of magnitude, and disruption of 2TMβ abolished protection; the same pattern was also observed in the 2TMβ-cas13b7 system (Fig. 1c; Extended Data Fig. 2a, b). Quantification across multiple phages for 2TMβ-cas13b6 showed that robust defense required the complete system, with strong effects observed when targeting the major capsid protein (mcp) (Fig. 1c). Robust phage defense was observed in liquid infection assays: at low multiplicity of infection (MOI), cultures expressing the intact 2TMβ-cas13b6 locus were unaffected by phage infection, whereas cultures expressing a locus lacking 2TMβ or expressing non-targeting guides collapsed (Fig. 1d; Extended Data Fig. 3a-h). Cas13b6 alone retained partial protection against T4 at low MOI (Fig. 1c; Extended Data Fig. 3a-h). At high MOI, all conditions collapsed, implying that 2TMβ-cas13b6 provides population-level immunity rather than directly interfering with the phage life cycle (Fig. 1d; Extended Data Fig. 3d, h). Together, these results establish 2TMβ-cas13b as a two-component defense module.

### Cas13 cleavage generates cyclic-phosphate tRNA second messengers

We next asked whether Cas13b6 activity could trigger 2TMβ without phage infection. We reprogrammed the Cas13b6 guide RNA to target a plasmid-borne GFP transcript, leading to loss of cell viability upon GFP transcript binding if 2TMβ is a bona fide cell-death effector (Fig. 2a). In the presence of a targeting transcript, expression of the full 2TMβ-cas13b6 locus dramatically reduced colony formation, while expression of either gene alone did not affect cell viability (Fig. 2a; Extended Data Fig. 4a, b). We further tested whether Cas13b6 nuclease activity underlies this response by mutating its Higher Eukaryotes and Prokaryotes Nucleotide-binding (HEPN) domain RNase catalytic residues, and found that these substitutions abolished the phenotype (Fig. 2a). Notably, Cas13b6 clade members universally carry a conserved histidine, H104, adjacent to the HEPN1 Rx 4 H catalytic motif in the RNase active site (Fig. 2c). Alanine substitution of H104 phenocopied the HEPN domain catalytic residue mutations, suggesting that additional features beyond the typical HEPN RNase residues may be involved in substrate recognition and cleavage.

Since 2TMβ requires small nucleotide effector binding for activation in other prokaryotic immune systems 9,10,16, we wondered whether the native Cas13b6 RNase activity might itself be responsible for effector generation. We first examined Cas13b6 substrate preference using purified components. Target-activated Cas13b6 showed limited cleavage of a generic fluorogenic RNA reporter relative to LbuCas13a and RfxCas13d (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). We therefore examined cleavage of cellular RNA substrates, beginning with purified E. coli tRNAs. Target-activated Cas13b6 generated discrete tRNA cleavage products (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 7). In reactions containing both tRNAs and a fluorescently labelled GFP target RNA, tRNA-derived products accumulated at early time points, whereas weak GFP target-RNA cleavage became apparent later (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Notably, Cas13b6 containing either HEPN1 or HEPN2 domain mutations abolished this activity, whereas H104A mutations impaired but did not abolish cleavage (Fig. 2c).

To identify the substrates and cleavage sites, we treated total E. coli RNA with target-activated Cas13b6 in vitro and mapped Cas13b6-dependent 5′ and 3′ RNA termini by small-RNA sequencing. Activation-associated termini mapped predominantly to tRNA anticodon loops, with cleavage after uridine (Fig. 2d, e; Extended Data Figs. 8–9). Separately, sequencing of RNA isolated from cells supported anticodon-region cleavage in vivo. Complementary MSR-seq of RNA from in vitro cleavage reactions recovered similar cleavage-associated patterns from tRNAs bearing modification-associated sequencing signatures (Extended Data Figs. 10–13). Analysis of the rRNA-rich fraction identified a single significantly enriched 3′ endpoint at uridine 2408 (U2408) in 23S rRNA (Extended Data Fig. 14). This pattern is reminiscent of activated LshCas13a and LbuCas13a, which cleave the uridine-rich anticodon loops in some tRNAs 17,18.

H104A narrowed these paired-cleavage patterns and reduced the detected substrate range from 20 to 11 tRNAs, implicating H104 in short-product excision (Fig. 2d, e; Extended Data Figs. 8,9,11,12). Activated Cas13b6 also cleaved isolated His and Val anticodon stem-loops, showing that the complete tRNA scaffold was not required for these substrates. Substitutions within the Val loop reduced the prominent cleavage-product signal, supporting a contribution of local sequence to cleavage (Extended Data Fig. 15). Sequence-specific Northern blotting independently confirmed activator-dependent cleavage of His and Val tRNAs in vitro and in vivo, with no fragment accumulation in catalytically inactive dHEPN1 controls (dHEPN1: R 96 NFYSH 101 >A 96 NFYSA 101; Extended Data Figs. 16c–f and 17b–e). Cleavage persisted in Δ2TMβ cells, demonstrating that tRNA cleavage is independent of the membrane effector. At 2h post induction, fragments accounted for approximately 79–83% of the detected His and ValV Northern signal in this background, accompanied by reduced intact-tRNA signals (Extended Data Fig. 17f,g).

To determine the identities and terminal chemistry of the cleavage products, we analyzed in vitro transcribed and Cas13-treated His and Asp tRNAs by mass spectrometry. Intact-RNA LC–MS identified complementary fragments consistent with cleavage on either side of GU, with masses supporting a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate at the 3′ end of the 5′ tRNA fragment the end chemistry (Fig. 2f, g; Extended Data Fig. 18a-c), expected of HEPN-family RNases 19. To independently test the terminal chemistry, we compared 3′-adapter ligation before and after treatment with T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK) that would restore 3′-hydroxyl groups compatible with adapter ligation. PNK treatment promoted ligation of the Cas13-derived fragments, and these ligated products migrated similarly to the ligation products of synthetic tRNA halves bearing 2′,3′-cyclic-phosphate termini (Extended Data Fig. 19). This PNK dependence supports blocked 3′ termini, consistent with the cyclic-phosphate assignments from intact-RNA LC–MS. Targeted LC–MS/MS of small-molecule filtrates from in vitro Cas13b6 cleavage reactions with either tRNA Asp GUC or tRNA His GUG directly detected GpU(2′,3′-cp) by comparison with a synthetic standard, establishing release of a linear GU dinucleotide bearing a terminal uridine 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (Fig. 2h; Extended Data Fig. 18a, d).

Together, these findings demonstrate that Cas13b6 preferentially cleaves tRNA anticodon loops in trans to generate tRNA-derived second messengers bearing a terminal uridine 2′,3′-cyclic-phosphate moiety, including GpU(2′,3′-cp) and the candidate mononucleotide 2′,3′-cUMP (Fig. 2d, e). H104A narrows the paired-cleavage pattern required for short-product excision and abolishes transcript-triggered toxicity in the phageless assay, identifying GpU(2′,3′-cp) and 2′,3′-cUMP as candidate signaling molecules in this system.

### Terminal uridine 2′,3′-cyclic-phosphate bearing ligands activate 2TMβ

The preceding results raised the possibility that Cas13-generated ligands activate 2TMβ directly. CBASS Cap15 and Panoptes immune systems also include 2TMβ, whose β-barrel domain recognizes the cyclase-generated ligand in its binding pocket 9,10,16. Ligand binding leads to 2TMβ multimerization through direct barrel-barrel contacts 9,10,16. The 2TMβ-cas13 β-barrel shares similar pocket organization with the CBASS and Panoptes 2TMβ, including the conserved tyrosine residues proposed to coordinate the ligand (Fig. 3a). Motivated by this conservation, we tested whether the 2TMβ binding pocket recognizes Cas13b6-derived products. We assayed which components of the 2TMβ are essential for function using phage-free GFP-targeting assays (Fig. 3b). We found that both the transmembrane domain and the β-barrel regions of 2TMβ are required for transcript-activated triggering of the 2TMβ-cas13b6 system (Fig. 3b). In addition, Y157 and Y197 are both involved in this response, as alanine substitutions at these positions reduced activity (Fig. 3b). These results are consistent with a ligand binding the 2TMβ pocket to drive signaling.

We next tested whether candidate ligands promote assembly of the purified 2TMβ barrel domain in vitro. To determine whether binding alters the assembly state of the protein, we incubated the purified β-barrel with 2′,3′-cUMP and a panel of ligands and analyzed these reactions by mass photometry (Fig. 3c). Synthetic 2′,3′-cUMP shifted the predominantly monomeric WT protein towards higher-mass assemblies (Fig. 3d), with an independent titration revealing a concentration-dependent response (Extended Data Fig. 20). GpU(2′,3′ cp) also promoted assembly, with higher-mass populations emerging as ligand concentration increased (Fig. 3e). These populations had higher apparent masses than those observed with 2′,3′-cUMP under the conditions tested, suggesting that ligand identity may influence the distribution of assembly sizes. Importantly, the pocket substitutions also altered ligand-responsive assembly. Y157A retained a reduced response to 2′,3′-cUMP, whereas Y197A did not produce a comparable higher-mass shift, supporting a role for the conserved pocket in ligand recognition (Fig. 3d).

To examine the chemical requirements for assembly, we compared 2′,3′-cUMP with related nucleotides. All tested 2′,3′-cyclic mononucleotides (2′,3′cAMP, 2′,3′-cGMP and 2′,3′-cCMP) and cyclic oligonucleotides did not produce comparable higher-mass distributions (Extended Data Fig. 21a). Likewise, β-Barrel oligomerization was unresponsive to the CDNs made by CBASS, type III CRISPR-Cas, and Panoptes cyclases (Extended Data Fig. 22) 9,10,16. β-Barrel oligomerization was end-chemistry specific, as closely related forms such order state induced by 2′,3′-cUMP or GpU(2′,3′-cp) (Extended Data Fig. 22). Complementary structural predictions yielded higher confidence and more consistent configurations for 2′,3′-cUMP than for the other tested cyclic mononucleotides, providing a structural hypothesis for this preference (Extended Data Fig. 21b, c).

These results establish that both 2′,3′-cUMP and GpU(2′,3′-cp) are sufficient to drive oligomerization of the isolated, soluble β-barrel in vitro. Together, these results support a model in which tRNA-derived second messengers bearing a terminal uridine 2′,3′-cyclic-phosphate moiety couple Cas13-mediated tRNA cleavage to 2TMβ assembly.

### Membrane lesions execute 2TMβ immunity

Having established this communication between Cas13 and 2TMβ both in vitro and in vivo, we next asked how 2TMβ activation mediates immunity. We monitored the kinetics of cell growth during phage-free GFP-targeting assays in liquid culture and found that expression of 2TMβ-cas13 causes rapid growth arrest (Fig. 4a). Intracellular ATP was reduced in these arrested cells, likely due to ablation of the proton motive force caused by 2TMβ-induced inner membrane disruption (Fig. 4b). Consistent with this, we found a substantial increase in membrane depolarization in arrested cells (Extended Data Fig. 23).

To distinguish the consequences of tRNA cleavage from those requiring 2TMβ, we compared polysome profiles. Despite detectable tRNA cleavage, Δ2TMβ cells retained prominent 70S and polysome peaks, showing that cleavage occurred without marked disruption of ribosome profiles at the sampled time point. These profiles were disrupted upon activation of the complete system, consistent with broader cellular damage associated with 2TMβ activity (Extended Data Fig. 24). Thus, tRNA cleavage occurred without comparable disruption of ribosome profiles in the absence of 2TMβ. In CBASS and Panoptes, the 2TMβ effector is proposed to kill the cell by collapsing the membrane 9,10,16. The ligand-responsive assembly of the isolated 2TMβ domain prompted us to examine membrane integrity in activated cells. Transmission electron microscopy revealed polar electron-lucent spaces and irregular envelope profiles in activated WT cells, contrasting with the more uniform appearance of the Δ2TMβ, non-targeting and dHEPN1 controls (Fig. 4c; Extended Data Fig. 25). Fluorescence microscopy revealed heterogeneous cell morphologies, including elongated cells, and pronounced uptake of the membrane-impermeable dye SYTOX Green in a subset of cells expressing the complete system, indicating membrane damage. Non-targeting, Δ2TMβ and HEPN-deficient controls showed little uptake (Fig. 4d).

To demonstrate that 2TMB ligand generation drives membrane perturbation, we assessed 2TMβ pocket substitutions in vivo. Consistent with their differences in oligomerization in response to 2′,3′-cUMP in vitro, these 2TMβ pocket mutants displayed a spectrum of growth arrest, cell morphology, and antiphage defense phenotypes (Fig. 4d; Extended Data Fig. 5a, b). Y157A retained detectable membrane-permeabilization activity and strong cell arrest phenotype, whereas Y197A was strongly impaired and displayed a reduced cell arrest phenotype, consistent with their different responses to 2′,3′-cUMP in vitro. The conservative Y197F substitution also retained SYTOX Green uptake (Fig. 4d). These results support a functional contribution of the ligand-responsive pocket to 2TMβ-dependent membrane damage. Together with the reduction in colony formation during phage-free activation (Fig. 2a), these observations support membrane damage and loss of viability as 2TMβdependent consequences of Cas13 activation.

## Discussion

Collectively, our results reveal a signaling pathway that connects RNA recognition to cell death. A guide RNA directs Cas13b6 to a target transcript, activating nucleolytic cleavage of a subset of host tRNA anticodon loops, releasing terminal U(2’,3’-cp) bearing ligands, including GpU(2′,3′-cp) and 2′,3′-cUMP. These ligands bind the β-barrel pocket of 2TMβ and drive its oligomerization to form membrane lesions, collapsing the cell’s energy supply and arresting its growth (Fig. 5a). The infected cell is killed, denying the phage a productive host, a logic that explains why protection holds at low MOI but is overwhelmed at high MOI (Fig. 1d). The 2TMβ-cas13b6 system exemplifies a nuclease-mediated immune response whose individual elements – tRNA as the source of the signal 20 and a small nucleotide released from a larger precursor as the messenger 14 – each have eukaryotic precedents. These similarities underscore the evolutionary parallels of immunity across the domains of life.

Prokaryotic immune second messengers have been understood as messengers generated by enzymatic conversion of nucleotide precursors, including cUMP and cGMP produced from UTP and GTP, respectively. In CBASS, Thoeris, Pycsar and type III CRISPR immune systems, a dedicated enzyme synthesizes a cyclic nucleotide or ADP-ribose messenger by modifying cellular nucleotides, and the resulting messenger molecule triggers an effector protein to mount a response 1–3. The pathway described here operates by the opposite logic. Rather than assembling a messenger from precursors, Cas13 cleaves tRNAs, among the most abundant RNAs in the cell, to release products bearing a terminal uridine 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate. This provides a potential route to immune signalling from an abundant, pre-existing RNA pool. Synthetic GpU(2′,3′-cp) and 2′,3′-cUMP were sufficient to promote assembly of the isolated 2TMβ β-barrel (Fig. 3d,e). Whether 2TMβ-cas13b6 systems also read other tRNA fragments as signals remains unknown, and the breadth of effector-gene association we observe raises the possibility of substantial ligand diversity. Our genomic survey reveals that signal sensor-effectors such as Pyc-TM, a 3′,5′-cCMP 3′,5′-cUMP sensor-effector from the Pycsar system, recur across numerous Cas13 loci, indicating repeated exaptation of these modules from other signaling pathways 8 (Fig. 5b). The logic of these findings prompts a broader reconsideration of tRNA cleavage in bacterial immunity. Targeting tRNA is a recurrent antiphage strategy, used not only by CRISPR-Cas nucleases such as Cas13 17 and Cas12a3 21, but also by non-CRISPR-Cas effectors such as the PrrC anticodon nuclease, the PARIS effector AriB, Retron-Eco7, RemAIN, and ApeA 22–25. These activities are currently interpreted as terminal, with depletion of a tRNA species arresting translation to abort infection, but our findings raise the possibility that in some systems, the cleaved tRNA is not merely a depleted substrate but the input to a downstream signaling pathway.

tRNA modifications could add a further dimension to this signaling chemistry. In E. coli His and Asp tRNAs, G34 is a site of queuosine (Q) modification; Asp tRNA can additionally carry glutamyl-queuosine 26,27. Cleavage at the corresponding bonds in Q-modified substrates would therefore yield modified dinucleotides such as QpU(2′,3′-cp), making these plausible cellular counterparts of the GpU(2′,3′-cp) identified from unmodified transcripts. Our reconstitution establishes that Q is not required for cleavage of these substrates or for ligand-induced assembly in vitro, though whether queuosine is required for physiological signaling, or instead tunes product formation or effector activation, remains unanswered. More broadly, tRNA modifications could expand the chemical repertoire available to immune signaling beyond that specified by the four canonical ribonucleotides.

The use of tRNA fragments as signals connects bacterial immunity to a growing body of work in eukaryotes, where tRNA cleavage products act as regulatory molecules that orchestrate various cellular processes 28. That both prokaryotes and eukaryotes independently couple tRNA cleavage to a downstream response suggests that the cell repeatedly reads tRNA cleavage as a signal, a logic that exploits tRNA’s abundance and its position at the center of protein synthesis. Beyond its role in defense, the Cas13b6 discovered here is an unusual enzyme that cleaves tRNAs in a programmable, guide-directed manner while apparently sparing the activating transcript and the broader transcriptome. This selectivity could make it a powerful tool for studying tRNA fragmentation and the downstream pathways it controls, particularly in heterologous eukaryotic hosts in which generating specific tRNA fragments on demand would be otherwise difficult. Because specific tRNA fragments can themselves be beneficial, conferring cellular protection and even slowing aging, the ability to generate a chosen fragment on demand may also open a route to harnessing these pathways therapeutically 29,30.

## Materials and Methods

### Bioinformatic identification and classification of 2TMβ–cas13 loci

To identify candidate hybrid type VI signaling loci, we searched publicly available prokaryotic genomes from NCBI (272.0) for compact two-transmembrane β-barrel proteins (2TMβ) and then examined their surrounding gene neighborhoods for defense-associated genes, including Cas13 family nucleases using a custom python script 31. For each 2TMβ hit, genes within a defined genomic window were extracted and functionally annotated by domain prediction and sequence-similarity searches. Loci were classified as cas13-associated when a cas13-family gene occurred in the same predicted operon or within a short neighborhood distance of the 2TMβ gene.

2TMβ-cas13 loci were manually curated into shared, tandem, or solo architectures on the basis of the presence or absence of additional signaling genes, including candidate cyclases and CBASS-related modules. Redundant loci were collapsed prior to phylogenetic analysis. Protein multiple-sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT 32 separately for the 2TMβ proteins and the associated Cas13 proteins, and trees were inferred using IQ-TREE 33 and ModelFinder from the aligned sequences to assess whether the 2TMβ–cas13 association was restricted to a single lineage or had arisen repeatedly across different clades. Representative loci shown in the main text were selected to illustrate the major architecture classes and the broader recurrence of 2TMβ across CBASS-, Panoptes-, Thoeris-, and type VI-associated neighborhoods.

### Bacterial strains and culturing

NEB 10-beta E. coli (New England Biolabs), 10-beta E. coli Mix & Go! Competent Cells (Zymo), Mach1 E. coli (Invitrogen), BL21(DE3) E. coli (New England Biolabs), E. coli BW25113 34 and E. coli MG1655 35 were used for plasmid cloning and all experiments in this study, as described in the following sections (Supplementary Tables 1–2). E. coli strains were grown in LB broth at 37°C, 250 RPM or on 1.5% agar plates, with appropriate inducers and antibiotics. Inducers and antibiotics were used at the following working concentrations: 0.05% L-Arabinose (Sigma), 100 nM Crystal Violet (CV, Sigma), 0.8 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, Sigma), 50 nM Anhydrotetracycline Hydrochloride (aTc, Thermo Scientific Chemicals) 35 µg/mL Kanamycin (Sigma), 20 µg/mL Chloramphenicol (Sigma), and 100 µg/mL Ampicillin or Carbenicillin (Sigma).

### Cloning and plasmid construction

All plasmids were assembled by Gibson Assembly, Golden Gate Assembly, or KLD/primer-extension cloning. PCR products were generated with KAPA HiFi (Roche), PrimeStar GXL (Takara) or Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) PCR mixes, using IDT-synthesised primers. Final constructs were sequence-verified by Plasmidsaurus or Quintara Biosciences. Four expression systems were used throughout this study. The pJEX-SC101 vector encodes a crystal violet inducible pJEX promoter. The pBAD-ColE1 vector encodes an arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter. The pTet-p15A vector encodes an aTc-inducible pTet promoter, as well as a constitutive J23119 promoter for CRISPR guide RNA expression. The pT7-SC101 encodes an IPTG-inducible T7 promoter and a N-terminus 10xHis–Maltose Binding Protein (MBP)–TEV cleavage site tags.

To study the antiphage activity of the Chitinophaga sp (Cas13b6) Type VI CRISPR system, the entire locus, including the 2TMβ-cas13b6 operon and a minimal CRISPR array (Repeat + 2xBbsI sites + Repeat), was synthesised as gene fragments (Twist) and cloned into the pJEX-SC101 vector. This parent plasmid was used to generate two types of variants: modifications of the CRISPR array by Golden Gate Assembly yielded reprogrammed crRNAs for targeting of various RNAs (phage and plasmid-derived); modifications to the 2TMβ-cas13b6 operon, generated by KLD/primer-extension cloning, yielded a panel of catalytic and deletion mutants in Cas13 and 2TMβ that served to dissect the role of each of these components in the observed antiviral activity.

To study the antiphage activity of the Chryseobacterium sp (Cas13b7) Type VI CRISPR system, the entire locus, including the 2TMβ-cas13b7 operon and a minimal CRISPR array (Repeat + 2x BsaI sites + Repeat), was E. coli-codon optimized, synthesised as gene fragments (Twist) and cloned into the pTet-p15A vector: the 2TMβ-cas13b7 operon was thus placed under control of the pTet promoter, while the minimal CRISPR array was constitutively expressed by the J23119 promoter. Two types of plasmid variants were generated, as described above for the Chitinophaga sp. orthologue. For phage-less activation assays, a target plasmid was generated by cloning the sfGFP CDS into the pBAD-ColE1 vector. Accordingly, the wild-type 2TMβ-cas13b6 CRISPR system and mutants thereof (∆2TMβ, Cas13b6 dHEPN1) were reprogrammed to encode a sfGFP-targeting spacer.

### Phage propagation and titration

Phages were propagated on E. coli BW25113 in LB at 37°C, using the webbed plate lysis method. Cleared and filtered lysates were stored at 4ºC, with their titres determined via plaque assays on empty vector-carrying E. coli BW25113.

### Plaque assays

Plaque assays were performed using the double agar overlay method. 100 µL of saturated overnight cultures carrying either a wild-type type VI CRISPR system or mutants thereof (as described in the main text) were added to 5 mL of molten LB agar (0.7% agar, ~50°C; henceforth, “top agar”) supplemented with antibiotics, and optionally inducer.

For plaque assays that assessed the antiviral activity of the Chitinophaga 2TMβ-cas13b6 operon against a panel of lytic phages, top agar was supplemented with crystal violet at a final concentration of 100 nM to promote expression of the targeting complex. For plaque assays that assessed the antiviral activity of the 2TMβ-cas13b7 operon against λvir, top agar was supplemented with aTc at a final concentration of 50 nM to promote expression of the targeting complex. The supplemented top agar was poured over 1.5% LB agar plates with antibiotics, and was allowed to cool to room temperature and solidify. Tenfold phage dilutions were prepared in SM buffer (Teknova), then 2.5 µL of this dilution series was spotted onto the top agar. After drying under sterile conditions, plates were incubated at 37ºC overnight. Plaque assays were performed in biological triplicate.

### Liquid infection assays

Saturated overnight cultures carrying either a wild-type, phage-targeting Chitinophaga 2TMβ-cas13b6 operon or mutants thereof (as described in the main text), were seeded in a microplate (Corning) at 8e6 CFU per well in 200 µL of LB supplemented with carbenicillin and 100nM crystal violet. Tenfold dilutions of phage T7 in SM buffer were added to cells, and growth was monitored by measurement of OD600 every 5 minutes while shaking at 800 RPM (double orbital) at 37ºC overnight on a 96-well plate reader (Biotek Cytation 5).

### Phage-less activation assays

The 2TMβ-cas13 plasmids and target GFP plasmids were introduced into E. coli NEB 10-beta strains (New England Biolabs) via electroporation. Approximately 25 ng of each plasmid was used in 0.1 mm cuvettes on a Micropulser device (Bio-Rad). Transformed cells were recovered for 1h, and serially diluted and plated on LB-agar containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin (Sigma), 35 µg/mL kanamycin (Sigma), and optionally 100nM crystal violet (CV, Sigma). Fluorescence intensity and cell viability were measured after overnight incubation at 37ºC to assess the depletion of the GFP fluorescence and CRISPR-induced cytotoxicity.

### Expression and purification of Cas13b6 proteins

Cas13b6 constructs encoding wild-type (WT), H104A, dHEPN1 and dHEPN2 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) pLysS cells carrying pCas13b6, which encodes an N-terminal His–TEV fusion tag. ### In vitro transcription of tRNAs

DNA templates encoding tRNA His GUG, tRNA Asp GUC, and tRNA Val GAC were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies as Ultramer oligonucleotides (Supplementary Table 3). Transcription templates were amplified by PCR using the forward and reverse primers listed in (Supplementary Table 3); each reverse primer carried a 2 ′ -O-methylated residue at the 5 ′ terminus to suppress non-templated nucleotide addition at the 3′ end of the transcript. Reactions contained 5 µg of Ultramer template, 0.5 µM each primer, and Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs) in a 200 µL reaction. Amplification was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the unpurified reaction was used directly as template for transcription.

Transcription reactions (1 mL) were assembled in 30 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM DTT, 0.1% spermidine, 0.1% Triton X-100 supplemented with 60 mM MgCl 2, 6 mM of each NTP, T7 RNA polymerase (purified in-house), and 20% (v/v) of the PCR reaction. After incubation at 37 °C for 8 hr, the insoluble inorganic pyrophosphate was pelleted by centrifugation at 20,000 x g for 1 minute, and then the RNA-containing supernatant was recovered directly by ethanol precipitation and resolved on a 15% polyacrylamide gel containing 7 M urea in 1X TBE. The band corresponding to full-length tRNA was located by UV shadowing and excised, and RNA was eluted from the crushed slice in nuclease-free water with 20 mM NaOAc pH 5.2 and 1 mM EDTA overnight at 4 °C. Gel fragments were removed by vacuum filtration through a 0.2 µm filter unit (Thermo Scientific), and the filtrate was concentrated and exchanged into nuclease-free water with 3 mM NaOAc pH 5.2 and 0.5 mM EDTA using 3 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore). Purified IVT tRNA was stored at −80 °C.

Cas13-mediated collateral RNA cleavage activity was measured using a fluorescent poly(U) (6FAM/UUUUU/IowaBlackQ) RNA reporter. ### In vitro cleavage of E. coli tRNAs

Purified Cas13b6 variants were tested for activated RNA cleavage activity in vitro using E. coli tRNAs as substrates. ### Time-course analysis of target-RNA and tRNA cleavage

Time-course cleavage assays contained 200 nM WT Cas13, 300 nM GFP gRNA, 300 nM 60-nt FAM-labelled GFP target RNA and 0.5 mg ml −1 purified E. coli tRNA in 1× Cas13 reaction buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 200 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl 2, 1 mM TCEP, 5% glycerol and 0.005% IGEPAL CA-630). ### Small RNA-seq and mapping of Cas13 cleavage sites

For end mapping, the 1 h cleavage reaction of E. coli total RNA was purified with SPRI beads and oxidized with 50 mM sodium periodate in 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. The reaction was quenched with glycerol and purified by SPRI. Oxidized RNA was treated with T4 polynucleotide kinase in 1X T4 PNK buffer, first without ATP to convert 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate termini to 3′-OH and then with ATP to 5′-phosphorylate. Small-RNA sequencing libraries were prepared with the Collibri small-RNA-seq library preparation following manufacturer protocol. Libraries were size-selected by gel extraction from 4% E-Gel EX Agarose Gels for inserts of 30–300 nt and sequenced with paired-end 150 bp reads via Illumina Next-Seq 1000. Sequencing reads were imported into Geneious and read pairs merged with BBMerge. Merged reads were mapped to the E. coli str. K-12 substrain DH10B reference complete genome (GenBank CP000948.1 36) with the Geneious mapper at low sensitivity. Reads were mapped in parallel to the whole genome, to ribosomal RNA, and to tRNA sequences to identify the most abundant mapped species. Manual inspection showed altered coverage across tRNA loci, and read 5′ and 3′ end positions were then plotted per nucleotide to localize cleavage termini.

### tRNA profiling by MSR-seq

Libraries were prepared by MesoRNA (Chicago, IL, USA) following the MSR-seq protocol as previously described (Watkins, 2021). Briefly, 1ug of total RNA was diluted into 6uL of water, then mixed with 1ul of SuperasIN and 8 uL of 180mM Tris pH9 for deacylation at 37C for 30 min. Next 3′ end repair was done with addition of 5 µL of T4 PNK mix (200 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 40 mM MgCl 2, 4 U/µL T4 PNK, from New England Biolabs) for 30min at 37C, followed by deactivation at 65C for 10 min. Next Hairpin adaptors barcoded oligos was added – 2uL of 20uM to each sample, followed by addition of 28uL of ligation mix: 1 U/µL T4 RNA ligase I (NEB), 1x NEB T4 RNA ligase I buffer, 15% PEG 8000, 50 µM ATP, 1 mM hexamine cobalt chloride, and 5% DMSO. Ligation was done for 12 hours at 16C. Samples were then bound to streptavidin beads, pooled, and dephosphorylated. Then cDNA was synthesized with SSIV at 55C for 10 min followed by 35 C overnight. Next RNA was digested with RNaseH, empty adaptor was inactivated by periodate oxidation, and a second adaptor was ligated at room temperature overnight. cDNA was boiled and moved to a new tube to released form streptavidin beads then used as template for 12 cycles of PCR with Q5 polymerase and indexing primers. Amplified libraries were gel cut between 150and 350bp of size, purified, and used for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X plus. Bioinformatics was additionally performed by MesoRNA (Chicago, IL, USA). Reads were demultiplexed and trimmed with custom software in accordance with the MSR-seq library preparation protocol 37. Quality control was performed with FastQC. Reads were aligned to tRNA sequences adapted from GtRNAdb 38 with bowtie2 39.

### PNK-dependent adapter ligation

Cas13-generated cleavage products from in vitro transcribed Val, His and Asp tRNAs were prepared and purified using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as described for intact-RNA LC–MS, and used directly without further fractionation. Synthetic tRNA Val and tRNA His 5′ fragments (ChemGenes Corporation) bearing defined 2′,3′-cyclic-phosphate termini were analyzed in parallel as reference substrates. RNA and adapter sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Approximately 1 µg of each purified Cas13 cleavage-product preparation or 40 pmol of synthetic RNA was incubated with 10 U T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK; New England Biolabs, M0201) in a 10-µl reaction containing 1× T4 PNK Reaction Buffer, without added ATP. Mock-treated controls contained the same components, with PNK replaced by nuclease-free water. Samples were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by 20 min at 65 °C to inactivate PNK, and cooled on ice.

For adapter ligation, 5 µl of each PNK-treated or mock-treated sample was added to a 20-µl reaction containing 2 µl of 10× T4 RNA Ligase Reaction Buffer, 4 µl of 50% PEG 8000, 40 pmol of the 17-nt, 5′-preadenylated, 3′-blocked Universal miRNA Cloning Linker (New England Biolabs, S1315) and 200 U T4 RNA ligase 2, truncated KQ (New England Biolabs, M0373). RNA was visualized by SYBR Gold staining. ### Intact-RNA LC–MS and mass deconvolution

Purified tRNA cleavage products from Cas13 in vitro reactions were analyzed by LC-HRMS on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (130 Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 X 50 mm) at 60 °C using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system coupled to a Waters Xevo G2-XS ToF mass spectrometer operated in negative ion mode. Mobile phase A consisted of 8 mM triethylamine (TEA), 80 mM hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) and 5 µM EDTA in Milli-Q water. Mobile phase B consisted of 4 mM TEA, 40 mM HFIP and 5 µM EDTA in 50% Milli-Q water / 50% methanol. The flow rate was 0.3 mL min −1. The gradient began at 22% B, increased linearly to 40% B over 10 min, increased to 60% B over 1 min, was held at 60% B for 1 min, returned to 22% B over 0.1 min, and was re-equilibrated at 22% B for 2.9 min. Source parameters were: capillary voltage, 2,000 V; sampling cone, 40; source offset, 40; source temperature, 140 °C; desolvation temperature, 20 °C; cone gas flow, 10 L h −1; desolvation gas flow, 800 L h −1; acquisition rate, 1 spectrum s −1. Expected masses for full-length IVTs and predicted cleavage products were calculated in silico (https://www.aatbio.com/tools/calculate-RNA-molecular-weight-mw), including products bearing 5′-hydroxyl and 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate termini. Deconvoluted mass spectra were generated using MaxEnt software (Waters), and observed masses were assigned by comparison to predicted masses of full-length RNA and candidate cleavage products. This LC–MS/deconvolution workflow was adapted from the intact tRNA LC–MS protocol 40.

### Targeted LC–MS/MS analysis of GpU(2′,3′-cp)

Cas13 cleavage reactions were performed in a total volume of 200 µl as described above and passed through 3-kDa molecular-weight-cut-off centrifugal filters. The filtrates were collected and extracted with methanol:acetonitrile:water (40:40:20, v/v/v). Extracts were dried in a SpeedVac vacuum concentrator at room temperature, and the dried residues were reconstituted in 50 µl of LC–MS-grade water. Samples were analyzed using a SCIEX ExionLC UPLC system coupled to a SCIEX 7500 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. A 5-µl aliquot was injected onto an Acclaim Polar Advantage II column (3-µm particle size, 2.1 × 150 mm; Thermo Scientific). Mobile phases A and B contained 3% and 97% methanol, respectively, each supplemented with 0.1% formic acid and 50 mM ammonium formate. Separation was performed at a flow rate of 200 µl min −1 using the following gradient: 0.2% B from 0 to 2 min, a linear increase to 49% B at 8 min, a return to 0.2% B at 8.1 min and equilibration at 0.2% B until 10 min.

Data were acquired in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM). Source settings were GS1 = 60, GS2 = 70, CUR = 40 and CAD = 9, with a source temperature of 375 °C and an ion-spray voltage of 2,200 V. The entrance potential was 10 V, the collision-cell exit potential was 15 V, the dwell time was 10 ms and the inter-transition pause time was 5 ms. An authentic GpU(2′,3′-cp) standard, guanylyl-(3′→5′)-uridine-2′,3′-cyclic monophosphate sodium salt, was custom-synthesized by Biolog (product no. G 085–01) and used for MRM optimization and external calibration. GpU(2′,3′-cp) was monitored using the quantifier transition m/z 652 → 152 and qualifier transitions m/z 652 → 289 and 652 → 501. The corresponding collision energies were 25, 40 and 20 V, respectively (Supplementary Table 9).

A nine-level external calibration series of GpU(2′,3′-cp) was analyzed in duplicate before and after the sample sequence and fitted using a quadratic model with 1/x weighting (R 2 = 0.9989). A pooled quality-control sample was analyzed before the sample sequence and after every ten samples. The pre-extracted Cas13 reaction samples were analyzed without an added internal standard. Data was processed using the Analytics module of SCIEX OS software (version 2.1.6.59781).

### Fluorescence microscopy

Saturated overnight cultures carrying either a wild-type, GFP-targetingChitinophaga 2TMβ-cas13b6 operon or mutants thereof (as described in the main text), were back-diluted to OD 0.1 and grown to OD 0.4. Then, cultures were seeded in a 96-well microplate (Corning) at a final OD600 of 0.1 per well in 200 µL of LB supplemented with 100 µg/mL carbenicillin (Sigma), 20 µg/mL kanamycin (Sigma), and optionally, 100nM crystal violet and 0.05% L-Arabinose. Growth was monitored by measurement of OD600 every 5 minutes while shaking at 800 RPM (double orbital) at 37ºC on a 96-well plate reader (Biotek Cytation 5) until the wild-type induced cultures arrested (typically, 3h post-seeding). Then, cultures were harvested from the 96-well plate by gentle centrifugation (~2000g for 3min) in microcentrifuge tubes and stained in the dark for thirty minutes at 37°C. Membrane-compromised cells were labelled with SYTOX Green at a final concentration of 1–5 µM, membranes were stained with FM4–64FX or FM4–64 at 5–10 µg ml−1, and DNA was labelled with DAPI at 5 µg ml−1 where indicated. After staining, cells were washed or directly resuspended in PBS and 5 µL were spotted on agarose imaging pads (100–25% LB + 1.5% agarose). Following 1 min of drying, the pad was covered by a small coverslip (Fisher Scientific) and imaged immediately using an Axio Observer microscope (Zeiss). Matched samples were imaged using identical acquisition settings. Images were collected in at least biological duplicate.

### Identification and annotation of cas13-associated genes

Subtype-specific cas13 HMM profiles were curated and used to conduct HMMER 42 searches against the NCBI non-redundant protein database 43. Hit FASTA files were split by subtype and HEPN half for Cas13a, Cas13b, Cas13bt, Cas13c, Cas13d, and Cas13e. Across these files, 2,919 hit entries were deduplicated to 1,402 unique protein accessions, and 1,393 accessions were retained for genomic neighborhood retrieval. For each retained Cas13 protein, genomic context was retrieved from NCBI IPG/GenBank using a +/−20 kb window around the focal protein. Retrieved loci were trimmed to +/−10 kb around the gene of interest, yielding 1,340 cas13-centered loci. Whole-locus nucleotide sequences were clustered with MMseqs2 using ‘--min-seq-id 0.5’, ‘-c 0.5’, ‘--dbtype 2’, and ‘--cluster-mode 2’, and one representative locus was retained per cluster. This produced 880 representative loci for downstream neighborhood analysis.

Non-Cas13 neighboring proteins were parsed from the 880 representative loci and clustered with the neighborhood-analysis scripts. Briefly, neighbor proteins were converted to FASTA, indexed with ‘mmseqs createdb’, and clustered with ‘mmseqs cluster’ using connected-component clustering (‘--cluster-mode 1’). Cluster assignments were exported with ‘mmseqs createtsv’, and cluster MSAs were generated with ‘mmseqs result2msa’ for HH-suite comparison. The resulting MMseqs2 protein clusters were compared with HHblits and merged into broader superclusters using probability >= 90%, query coverage >= 50%, and target coverage >= 50%. This produced 4,242 HHblits superclusters containing 8,432 unique clustered neighbor proteins.

All 8,432 clustered proteins were annotated with DeepTMHMM to identify predicted transmembrane topology and helix boundaries. DeepTMHMM calls were obtained for all proteins, yielding 1,419 TM-positive proteins distributed across 746 superclusters. Pfam annotation was performed with two complementary approaches. First, all clustered proteins were searched against Pfam-A with HMMER ‘hmmscan’ using Pfam gathering thresholds. Second, multi-sequence TM-positive clusters were aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i, converted to HH-suite HHM profiles, and searched against the HH-suite Pfam database with HHsearch. The final key TM/Pfam table integrated DeepTMHMM topology, sequence-level HMMER/Pfam calls, and cluster-level HHsearch/Pfam calls. Of 746 TM-positive superclusters, 514 had Pfam support from at least one annotation layer.
