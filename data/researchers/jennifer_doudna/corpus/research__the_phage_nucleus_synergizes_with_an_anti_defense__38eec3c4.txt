# The phage nucleus synergizes with an anti-defense protein to resist bacterial immunity

## Abstract

Chimallivirus bacteriophages enclose their replicating genomes in a protein-based compartment termed the phage nucleus. While the phage nucleus segregates phage DNA from host immune proteins, it is not known if additional factors are required to protect against DNA-targeting host defenses. Here, we identify a chimallivirus-encoded DarG2-like antitoxin that localizes to the phage nucleus and provides protection against phage-targeting DarTG2 toxin-antitoxin systems. This protein, which we term AdfM (anti-darT factor macro), contains a macrodomain and removes DarT2-mediated ADP-ribose modifications from DNA. In the absence of AdfM, DarT2 modifies phage DNA and restricts chimallivirus replication despite being largely excluded from the phage nucleus. Increasing the nuclear concentration of DarT2 while decreasing the nuclear concentration of AdfM reduces phage replication. These results show that the phage nucleus is insufficient to completely protect the chimallivirus genome from host defenses; rather, it is one component of a multilayered counter-defense strategy.

Morgan et al. show that a phage-encoded protein functions as an antitoxin to counter DarTG2-mediated phage defense. They further explore the interplay between this anti-defense protein, the phage nucleus replication compartment, which encloses chimallivirus genomes, and the DarTG2 phage defense system.

## INTRODUCTION

Chimalliviruses are bacteriophages that compartmentalize the bacterial cytoplasm during infection in a manner reminiscent of eukaryotic cells. 1–6 Upon initial infection, the chimallivirus genome is injected into a lipid-bound compartment termed the early phage infection (EPI) vesicle, and from there, the genome is transferred into a proteinaceous compartment termed the phage nucleus that excludes ribosomes and decouples mRNA transcription from protein translation. 7–9 The phage nucleus is bounded by an interlocking meshwork comprising a single major protein, chimallin (ChmA), plus a number of minor proteins that mediate export of mRNA out of the phage nucleus and import of specific phage and host proteins involved in DNA replication, DNA repair, and transcription. 1,2,10–13 DNA-targeting host defense nucleases, including CRISPR-Cas9, and restriction endonucleases are excluded from the phage nucleus, leading to a model in which the phage nucleus single-handedly renders chimalliviruses resistant to all DNA-targeting host defense systems. 6,14,15 

DNA-targeting host defense systems provide a strong selective pressure for the phage to evolve and maintain the phage nucleus and its selective import machinery. Many phage and host proteins are sorted into the nucleus through a conserved phage protein, PicA (protein importer of chimalliviruses A; also termed Imp1), which recognizes surface-exposed residues on the folded protein substrate. 2,10 PicA and its substrates have co-evolved, such that primarily cognate proteins and not homologs from closely related phages are imported into the nucleus. 2 This allows the import machinery to be both highly specific and tunable. However, it also begs the question of whether or not bacteria have evolved defense systems capable of bypassing the nuclear shell in the ongoing phage-host arms race.

In recent years, the number of characterized bacterial immune systems has greatly increased, and the diverse mechanisms by which they function are increasingly well characterized. 16–22 DarTG is a toxin-antitoxin system that protects bacteria from phage infection by ADP-ribosylating DNA. 23,24 Two types of DarTG systems have been characterized, distinguished by their antitoxins and by their overall molecular mechanisms. In both types, the toxin DarT ADP-ribosylates single-stranded DNA, and the antitoxin DarG counteracts DarT by enzymatically removing the ADP-ribose DNA modification. 23–25 DarTG1 systems ADP-ribosylate guanine bases and utilize a NADAR domain on DarG1 to remove ADP-ribose from DNA, while DarTG2 systems ADP-ribosylate thymine bases and utilize a macrodomain on DarG2 to remove ADP-ribose. 23,24,26,27 DarG2 also contains a second domain that binds DarT2 directly. 23,25 Upon phage infection, DarTG systems become activated and inhibit phage infection by reducing both transcription and DNA replication. 24 

Phages have evolved a number of proteins to counter DarTG-mediated phage defense, which are collectively named anti-DarT factors (Adfs). AdfA and AdfB are direct inhibitors of DarT encoded by T4-like and Vibrio phages, respectively. 24,28 AdfN, on the other hand, is a NADAR domain protein encoded by T4-like phages that enzymatically removes ADP-ribose from phage DNA with specificity for DarT1-mediated modifications. 29 Aside from phage-encoded antitoxins, one phage has also been shown to escape DarTG2 immunity through mutations in its DNA polymerase, which presumably allows the polymerase to read through ADP-ribosylated DNA. 24 To date, no macrodomain Adfs have been reported.

Here, we show that a nuclear-localized macrodomain-containing protein encoded by chimalliviruses, which we name AdfM (Adf macro), potently counters DarTG2-mediated phage defense by removing ADP-ribose modifications from DNA. Furthermore, we show that when the gene encoding AdfM is deleted or mutated, chimalliviruses become sensitive to multiple divergent DarTG2 systems despite possessing the phage nuclear shell that encloses their replicating genome. Sensitivity to DarTG2-mediated immunity is dictated by the levels of both the toxin DarT2 and the antitoxin AdfM inside the phage nucleus. Together, these results indicate that the chimallivirus nuclear shell is an imperfect counter-defense strategy and that these phages also encode a specific protein to counteract DarTG2-mediated immunity.

## RESULTS

### AdfM is a catalytically active DarG2-like antitoxin

Macrodomain proteins are conserved across all kingdoms of life and the viruses that infect them. We and others have shown that a macrodomain protein from the Pseudomonas aeruginosa-infecting chimallivirus PhiKZ (gp104) is specifically imported into the phage nucleus through the action of PicA. 2,10 Using this protein as a search query in PSI-BLAST, we identified macrodomain proteins in about half of chimalliviruses (Figures 1A and S1). Based on sequence alignments and protein structure predictions, these proteins are strongly predicted to share the enzymatic activity of related macrodomain proteins, such as Thermus aquaticus DarG2, hydrolyzing ADP-ribose from single-stranded DNA (Figures 1B, 1C, S2, and S3) 23,25; we therefore named this protein AdfM for anti-darT factor macro. While this fold is also conserved in eukaryotic viruses, this phage macrodomain most closely aligns with bacterial DarG2 macrodomains, which exclusively counteract ADP-ribosylation of DNA.

We first expressed GFP-tagged versions of AdfM from three chimalliviruses, the Erwinia phage Asesino, 30 and the Pseudomonas phages PhiKZ and PhiPA3. In all three cases, we observed that AdfM localizes to the phage nucleus in infected cells (Figure 1D). To test the enzymatic activity of AdfM, we ADP-ribosylated a DNA oligonucleotide in vitro using a purified DarT2 protein from a clinical isolate of P. aeruginosa, strain 5054408345 (here termed strain 505). We found that P. aeruginosa 505 DarT2 recognizes the same sequence as other characterized DarT2 proteins, ADP-ribosylating the second T base in a TNTC motif (Figure S4). 23 Using this modified substrate, we found that PhiKZ AdfM efficiently removes the ADP-ribose moiety and that mutation of a conserved asparagine residue in the catalytic site reduces its activity (Figure 1E).

To determine whether AdfM can counteract the toxicity of DarT2, we coexpressed a P. aeruginosa DarT2 protein with either its cognate DarG2 or PhiKZ AdfM in E. coli and measured toxicity using growth curves (Figures 1F and 1G) and dilution spotting assays (Figure S5). In growth curves, expression of DarT2 alone caused a strong growth defect, expression of DarG2 alone caused a mild growth defect, and expression of PhiKZ AdfM had no effect on cell growth. Expression of DarG2 rescued the growth of DarT2-expressing cells to the level of DarG2-expressing cells, confirming its ability to act as an anti-DarT2 antitoxin (Figure 1F). Expression of PhiKZ AdfM fully rescued the growth of DarT2-expressing cells, demonstrating that it acts as a potent anti-DarT2 antitoxin (Figure 1G). These results are matched in dilution spotting assays, where AdfM fully rescues the growth of cells expressing DarT2 (Figure S5).

### Loss of AdfM limits PhiKZ host range across clinical P. aeruginosa isolates

If AdfM acts as an anti-DarTG2 factor, loss of AdfM should sensitize the phage to host strains that encode DarTG2 defense systems. To test this idea, we isolated three AdfM deletion strains of PhiKZ using CRISPR-Cas13, 31,32 ranging in size from an in-frame deletion of six amino acids to full deletions of AdfM plus all or part of the non-essential flanking genes p32, gp103, and gp105 (Figure 2A). We used two AdfM deletions (termed del AdfM-1 and del AdfM-2) to perform a serial dilution titer (spot titer) screen on a panel of 12 randomly selected drug-resistant P. aeruginosa strains from the CDC CarbaNP collection (Figure S6). 33 Wild-type PhiKZ successfully infected nine of the twelve clinical P. aeruginosa strains, demonstrating PhiKZ’s broad host range across P. aeruginosa isolates. The AdfM-deleted strains of PhiKZ were equally capable of infecting seven of these nine strains but showed multi-log reductions in titer compared to wild-type PhiKZ on two P. aeruginosa strains (AR0054 and AR0103; Figure S6). To validate these results, we performed triplicate spot titers using wild-type PhiKZ and all three del AdfM strains on four P. aeruginosa host strains: our laboratory host strain PAO1 K2733 and CDC strains AR0094, AR0100, and AR0103. As in our initial screen, we found that deletion of AdfM only affected the infection of host AR0103 (Figure 2B).

To further validate the observed titer loss in host strain AR0103 upon AdfM deletion, we performed a 90-min progeny collection experiment in liquid culture with P. aeruginosa strains PAO1 K2733 or AR0103 (Figure 2C). Mid-log-phase bacterial cultures were incubated with wild-type PhiKZ, del AdfM-1, or del AdfM-2 phages for 5 min at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ∼2.5. Cells were pelleted, excess phage washed away, and then cells were resuspended in growth media. After 90 min, progeny phages in the supernatant were titered on PA01 K2733. We observed an approximately 15-fold reduction in the titer of the two AdfM mutants compared to wild-type PhiKZ, specifically when cells were infecting AR0103 (Figure 2C). This suggests that the decrease observed in spot titer experiments (Figure 2B) is due to a reduction, but not a complete loss, of progeny phage produced in each lytic cycle.

Given AdfM’s homology to DarG2, we sought to identify if AR0054 or AR0103 encodes a DarTG2 system. While both DefenseFinder and PADLOC failed to identify a DarT protein in these strains, DefenseFinder did identify multiple proteins similar to DarG2 in both strains. 34,35 We used PSI-BLAST to identify a protein with 26% identity to Thermus aquaticus DarT2 positioned directly upstream of one of the identified DarG homologs in each strain 36 (Figures S7A and S7B). Closer analysis of these protein sequences shows that the systems are identical to one another and that their DarG proteins contain a macrodomain, confirming that these strains both encode DarTG2 systems (Figure S7B). This is consistent with recent studies that have identified DarTG systems in clinical P. aeruginosa isolates, with increased prevalence in strains isolated from patients with cystic fibrosis. 37,38 None of the other strains tested had DarT homologs identified by BLAST, which is consistent with the ability of AdfM deletion mutants to replicate in these strains as well as wild-type PhiKZ.

### DarTG2 restricts chimallivirus infection in the absence of AdfM

To test if phage restriction of AdfM mutant phages is indeed due to DarTG2-mediated defense, we sought to introduce a DarTG2 system into PAO1 K2733. While we were unable to clone the AR0054/AR0103 DarTG2 system due to apparent toxicity (data not shown), we could clone and express two different DarTG2 systems from P. aeruginosa strains 505 and CW41, whose DarT and DarG proteins show ∼20% and 26% sequence identity to the DarTG2 system from AR0054/AR0103, respectively (Figures S7A and S7B). We found that both 505 and CW41 DarTG2 strongly restricted infection of all three del AdfM strains but had no effect on wild-type PhiKZ infection (Figure 2D). Spot titers show a three-log loss in titer for AdfM mutant PhiKZ plated on 505 DarTG2-expressing bacteria and a six-log loss in titer for AdfM mutant PhiKZ plated on CW41 DarTG2-expressing bacteria (Figure 2D). We also tested if this effect generalized to other phages by selecting an AdfM mutant PhiPA3 strain (Figure 2F). AdfM mutant PhiPA3 also showed a decrease in titer compared to wild-type PhiPA3 when plated on bacteria expressing either the 505 or CW41 DarTG2 system (Figure 2G).

Since spot titers are dependent on a number of factors, including phage burst size and bacterial replication rate, we sought to understand better the dynamics of phage restriction by DarTG2 using a 90-min progeny collection experiment in liquid culture. For both PhiKZ and PhiPA3, loss of AdfM resulted in a ∼15- to ∼80-fold reduction in progeny produced during one lytic cycle when host cells were expressing either DarTG2 system (Figures 2E and 2H). For each combination of phage mutant and DarTG2 system, the degree of progeny loss was consistent with the observed reduction in titer. These results indicate that DarTG2 TA systems reduce the amount of progeny produced by phages lacking AdfM but do not fully prevent phage replication.

### AdfM counteracts DarTG2-mediated ADP-ribosylation of phage DNA

To test whether phage DNA is ADP-ribosylated by DarTG2 and whether AdfM counteracts this activity, we performed an anti-ADP-ribose dot blot on DNA isolated from infected cells. Since PhiKZ infection results in host genome destruction, 1,3 we reasoned that detection of ADP-ribosylated DNA during late infections (45 mpi) would indicate that the replicating PhiKZ genome is modified. We isolated DNA from P. aeruginosa cells carrying the CW41 DarTG2 system or an empty vector, infected with either wild-type PhiKZ or the del-AdfM-3 mutant (Figure 3A). We spotted DNA onto a nitrocellulose membrane and blotted with an anti-ADP-ribose antibody. We detected no ADP-ribosylation in cells carrying the empty vector. In cells carrying CW41 DarTG2, we detected ADP-ribosylation, with cells infected with PhiKZ del-AdfM-3 showing much higher levels than cells infected with wild-type PhiKZ. These data show that DarTG2 ADP-ribosylates phage DNA in PhiKZ-infected cells and that AdfM directly counteracts this activity.

### DarTG2 reduces PhiKZ DNA replication

Prior work showed that DarT-mediated ADP-ribosylation prevents DNA replication and transcription in both the host and the infecting phage. 24 However, during PhiKZ infection, AdfM is expressed late in the infection cycle, 39 and the host genome is degraded early in the infection cycle, 1,3 so the effects of DarT2 must be due to modification of the phage genome. Since DarT2-mediated DNA modification could affect phage transcription, DNA replication, virion packaging, or infectivity of progeny phages, we sought to determine which of these processes was most affected.

To investigate transcription, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on P. aeruginosa cells either transformed with an empty vector or expressing CW41 DarTG2, 40 min post-infection, with either wild-type PhiKZ or the del AdfM-3 mutant. Since PhiKZ has an average time to lysis of 62 min in our growth conditions, 2 we would expect to observe changes in transcript levels at this time point if DarT2 blocks transcription. We observed no significant difference in gene expression between the wild-type and mutant PhiKZ strains in either condition (Figures 3B, S8, and S9). This indicates that DarTG2 does not block transcription of PhiKZ genes and suggests that infection is instead restricted at a later stage.

Next, we performed microscopy on DAPI-stained P. aeruginosa cells either transformed with an empty vector or expressing CW41 DarTG2, infected with either wild-type PhiKZ or the del AdfM-3 mutant, at 40 min post-infection (Figure 3C). During infection with wild-type PhiKZ, DarTG2 expression had little effect on phage nuclear size (Figure 3C, top) or DNA content as measured by quantification of DAPI signal (Figure 3C, bottom). During infection by PhiKZ del AdfM-3, we observed a small decrease in nuclear size corresponding to an approximately 25% decrease in nuclear DAPI signal (Figure 3C). Since the DAPI signal in a single plane would tend to underestimate true differences in overall DNA content, we also performed qPCR using two primer pairs covering regions of the chmA gene that do not include a TNTC motif (see STAR Methods), also at 40 min post-infection. In this analysis, we observed that expression of the CW41 DarTG2 system caused a 4-fold reduction in DNA replication by PhiKZ del AdfM-3 relative to wild-type PhiKZ (Figure 3D). Thus, DarT2 does affect DNA replication by PhiKZ in the absence of AdfM, though the observed 4-fold reduction in DNA replication does not explain the multi-log reduction in phage plaquing that we observe in spot titers. These results indicate that DarT2 might also act late in the chimallivirus replication cycle after the phage nucleus forms and phage DNA replication begins. This suggests that either mature virions are not formed or the phage progeny of these infections are non-viable.

### DarTG2 blocks PhiKZ virion packaging

To test if DarTG2 blocks virion packaging in AdfM mutant PhiKZ, we performed cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET) on P. aeruginosa cells carrying the CW41 DarTG2 system, 40 min after infection by either wild-type or del AdfM-3 PhiKZ. In infections by wild-type PhiKZ, we observed capsids docked at the chimallin shell, many of which were partially or completely filled with genomic DNA (Figures 4A, 4B, and S10). Cells infected with PhiKZ del AdfM-3 showed capsids docked at the chimallin shell, but we observed no partially or completely filled capsids (Figures 4A, 4B, and S10). These data show that the process of genome packaging into capsids, which likely involves a DNA-packaging motor as in other phages, 40–42 is compromised in the presence of DarT2 and when AdfM is absent.

To validate the above cryo-ET results, we performed the 90-min progeny collection assay as described earlier, then imaged the virions using cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM). Cryo-EM revealed that wild-type PhiKZ progeny were primarily mature virions with DNA-packaged heads and assembled tails, as expected (65% filled capsids with tails and 20% unfilled and tailless) (Figures 4C, 4D, and S10). In contrast, PhiKZ del AdfM-3 progeny primarily contained DNA-free heads without tails (1% filled capsids with tails and 96% unfilled and tailless), confirming that DarT2 largely prevents DNA packaging into the PhiKZ head (Figures 4C–4E and S10). Together, these results demonstrate that in chimalliviruses, DarT2 primarily inhibits virion formation rather than transcription or DNA replication. This finding is in contrast to other phages, where DarT2 inhibits phage propagation by arresting DNA replication and transcription. 24 

### PhiKZ sensitivity to DarTG2 depends on the nuclear localization of DarT2 and AdfM

Since DarT2 is a DNA-targeting protein and the phage DNA is enclosed within the nuclear shell, we sought to determine if DarT2 was able to exploit or bypass the phage nucleus’s selective protein trafficking system. At 45 min post-infection (time to lysis: ∼62 min), 2 sfGFP-tagged DarT2 predominantly localized to the host cytoplasm, indicating that DarT2 is not actively imported into the phage nucleus (Figures 5A and 5B). While DarT2-sfGFP is predominantly cytoplasmic, the limitations of fluorescence quantification (including the presence of out-of-plane fluorescence) mean that we cannot rule out a minor population of DarT2-sfGFP in the phage nucleus. However, fusion of DarT2 to GFPmut1, which independently localizes to the PhiKZ nucleus (Figure S11), 15 increases the nuclear concentration of DarT2 (Figures 5A and 5B). Taking advantage of this difference in localization, we tested the ability of PhiKZ to replicate in strains expressing either DarT2-sfGFP or DarT2-GFPmut1 (Figures 5C, S12A, and S12B). In the absence of AdfM, both 505 DarT2-GFPmut1 and CW41 DarT2-GFPmut1 strongly restricted phage infection (Figures 5C, S12A, and S12B). In contrast, 505 DarT2-sfGFP showed a reduced ability to restrict infection, while CW41 DarT2-sfGFP did not restrict infection at all in the absence of AdfM (Figures 5C, S12A, and S12B). These results suggest that fusion to sfGFP decreases the activity of DarT2 and/or its ability to cross the nuclear shell.

Next, we sought to determine how altering the nuclear concentration of AdfM affected phage restriction by DarT2. To achieve this, we utilized PhiKZ carrying a PicA Q297K mutation, which we previously showed to reduce the amount of AdfM in the nucleus, without affecting the localization of GFPmut1 or other nuclear proteins. 2 Indeed, PhiKZ PicA Q297K reduced the amount of AdfM-sfGFP that localized to the nucleus (Figures 5D and 5E). PhiKZ PicA Q297K showed a 7-fold reduction in plaquing on cells expressing CW41 DarT2-GFPmut1 and a 2-fold reduction in plaquing on cells expressing 505 DarT2-GFPmut1, while no difference was seen with either form of DarT2-sfGFP (Figures 5F, S12A, and S12B). Additionally, plaque area was reduced by ∼50% for PhiKZ PicA Q297K compared to wild-type PhiKZ on cells expressing 505 DarT2-GFPmut1 and was reduced by ∼70% for PhiKZ PicA Q297K compared to wild-type PhiKZ on cells expressing CW41 DarT2-GFPmut1 (Figure S12C). This shows that increasing the nuclear concentration of DarT2 while simultaneously decreasing the nuclear concentration of AdfM decreases phage replication without altering either protein’s expression level. Together, these data indicate that the ability of PhiKZ to replicate in the presence of DarTG2 depends on both the concentration of DarT2 and AdfM in the nucleus. Therefore, selective import of AdfM into the phage nucleus is required to protect the phage genome from DarTG2-mediated defense.

## DISCUSSION

The compartmentalization of the chimallivirus genome inside the phage nucleus is thought to have evolved to protect the replicating phage from DNA-targeting host defenses, such as restriction endonucleases and DNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas systems. 6,14,43 The phage genome is protected inside the nucleus by the selective nuclear protein import chimalliviridae (PIC) pathway, which is composed of PicA and other factors. 2,10 It was previously thought that host factors were unable to target the phage DNA as a result of this selective import system. 6,14 However, the fact that DarTG2 can restrict chimallivirus infection despite being poorly imported into the phage nucleus suggests that instead the phage nucleus and PIC pathway work together with counter-defense factors to counter host immunity.

One model for how DarT2 accesses phage DNA is that the growing phage nucleus is an imperfect diffusion barrier. The phage nucleus grows during infection by incorporating new chimallin (ChmA) units into the lattice structure of the shell. 12 This process requires transient disruption of the tight lattice architecture of the ChmA shell, which is likely to briefly allow free movement of macromolecules into the phage nucleus. Thus, during shell expansion, a small number of cytoplasmic proteins are likely able to access the chimallivirus genome (Figure 6). An alternative model is that the selectivity of the PIC pathway is constantly evolving, and it imports some cytoplasmic proteins into the nucleus at a low frequency, while there is no evidence that proteins can be exported once inside the phage nucleus. In both cases, if the imported protein is toxic, such as DarT2, compensatory mechanisms are necessary to ensure phage survival (Figure 6). Both of these models are consistent with our findings that, in the absence of AdfM, DarT2 restricts chimallivirus infection late in the lytic cycle by blocking genome packaging into the virion. In chimalliviruses, genome packaging involves docking of empty capsids on the chimallin shell, presumably at a pore in the chimallin shell, most likely followed by packaging of genomic DNA by an as-yet unidentified packaging motor. In this light, we propose that ADP-ribosylation of phage DNA by DarT2 may compromise the activity of the DNA packaging motor, thereby disrupting genome packaging. Alternatively, modified DNA may be sterically hindered from passing through the chimallin pore or capsid portal.

The PIC pathway and AdfM provide multiple layers of protection against bacterial defense systems. The phage nucleus provides an imperfect, but still significant, diffusion barrier preventing DarT2 from accumulating in the nucleus, 1,6,14 while the PIC pathway imports and concentrates AdfM in the nucleus. 2,10,11 Thus, by imperfectly excluding the bacterial toxin DarT2 and selectively importing the phage antitoxin AdfM, the phage nucleus is optimized to evade DarTG2-mediated immunity (Figure 6). AdfM therefore works in synergy with the phage nuclear shell and protein import system to maximally resist this DNA-targeting defense system. This model is consistent with our results that increasing the nuclear concentration of DarT2 while simultaneously decreasing the nuclear concentration of AdfM restricts phage infection. However, the PicA Q297K mutant alone does not sensitize the phage to DarT2, suggesting that AdfM exclusion by this mutant is incomplete or that wildtype levels of AdfM nuclear import are not strictly necessary for counter-immunity.

Given that the phage nucleus is more permeable to bacterial immune proteins than previously thought, it is unsurprising that chimalliviruses encode and import a number of DNA recombination and repair proteins. 1,5,44,45 Chimalliviruses’ insensitivity to restriction endonucleases and CRISPR-Cas9 could be explained by a combination of exclusion by the phage nucleus and the concentration of DNA repair factors, which work together to overcome the effect of bacterial immune nucleases. The synergy of bacterial defense systems, in which multiple systems together protect the bacterial host more than any single system, is now being more widely appreciated. 46,47 It appears that similar synergy has also evolved in viruses to counter these defense systems. The complex interactions of bacterial defense systems with viral counter-defense factors continue to be a rich area of research, and future work will reveal how widespread counter-immune synergies are among phages.

### Limitations of the study

We demonstrate that AdfM, a phage nuclear protein, is required for chimalliviruses to resist the DarTG2 phage defense system, which targets and modifies phage DNA. Our model that DarT2 acts within the phage nucleus to restrict infection and AdfM protects the phage genome inside the nucleus is fully consistent with the data. However, technical limitations prevent us from directly measuring DarT2 and AdfM activity within the nucleus. Investigation of protein localization requires fusion to fluorescent tags, which may alter protein function. Absolute quantification of protein localization is not possible by our methods, and thus, only relative quantification could be performed. Furthermore, quantification of DNA and ADP-ribose requires lysis of cells, which disrupts the structure of the nucleus and therefore cannot provide sub-cellular detail. Despite these challenges, we believe our model to be the most parsimonious explanation of the observed findings.

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

P. aeruginosa PAO1 K2733 was used as the host for PhiKZ and PhiPA3. Erwinia amylovora ATCC 29780 was used as the host for Asesino. Both strains were grown at 30°C in LB, and 15 μg/mL gentamicin was used for plasmid selection and 0.2% arabinose used for expression induction. Phage stocks were collected from plate lysates using phage buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgSO 4, 68 mM NaCl, and 1 mM CaCl 2) and stored at 4°C with titers of ∼10 11 PFU/mL. Clinical P. aeruginosa isolates were obtained from the CDC AR bank and grown at 37°C in LB.

#### AlphaFold modeling and structural analysis

A model of PhiKZ AdfM (gp104) plus ADP-ribose (CCD ligand code AR6) was produced using AlphaFold version 3.0.1 54 locally installed on a GPU server (https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold3/). PAE plots were visualized using PAEViewer (https://pae-viewer.uni-goettingen.de). Model analysis and comparison was performed in PyMOL version 3 (Schrodinger), and figures were generated using PyMol and ChimeraX. 55 

#### Plasmid construction and transformation

Plasmids were synthesized and cloned by Genscript. The vector for all P. aeruginosa and E. amylovora plasmids was pHERD-30T. DarTG2 systems were cloned as intact operons with a plasmid-encoded arabinose-inducible promoter. Plasmid transformation was accomplished by 2 kV electroporation. Expression of inserts was achieved with 0.2% arabinose. 5054408345 (here called 505) DarTG2 sequence was acquired from NCBI entry GCA_004371005.1 (NCBI: GCA_004371005.1). CW41 DarTG2 sequence was acquired from NCBI entry NZ_CP129496.1 (NCBI: NZ_CP129496.1).

#### Mutant phage isolation

Clonal mutant phage strains were isolated through Cas13-targeting as described previously. 32 Briefly, a pHERD30T plasmid containing the sequence for LbuCas13a and a guide RNA targeting either PhiKZ gp104 or PhiPA3 gp108 on an arabinose-inducible promoter was transformed into P. aeruginosa. ∼10 9 PFU of phage were added to mid-log phase culture induced with 0.1% arabinose and a plaque assay was performed. Individual plaques that escaped selection were isolated, streak purified three more times, and then high titer lysates were generated. PCR amplification of the PhiKZ gp104 or PhiPA3 gp108 genomic region was used to identify strains with a deletion and then deletion strains were sequenced by Illumina NextSeq 2000 at a depth of 200Mbp (SeqCenter). Guide sequences used in this study are as follows: gacgccaacaacattaacagtgattagataa (PhiKZ gp104) and agatatccttaccgattatgaattcagccat (PhiPA3 gp108).

#### Single-cell infection assay

Single-cell infections of P. aeruginosa were visualized using fluorescence microscopy. P. aeruginosa was grown at 30°C rotating up to an OD600 of ∼0.5 and mixed with PhiKZ or PhiPA3 lysate at a ratio of 1:100 lysate:culture. For protein expression, LB was supplemented with 15 μg/mL gentamicin and 0.2% arabinose. Infections were incubated rotating at 30°C and then inoculated on 1% agarose pads containing 25% LB and 1 μg/mL DAPI and dried for 5 min at room temperature before a coverslip was applied. Imaging was performed with a DeltaVision Elite Deconvolution microscope (Applied Precision, Issaquah, WA). Images were further processed by the aggressive deconvolution algorithm in the DeltaVision SoftWoRx 6.5.2 Image Analysis Program.

#### Plaque assay

P. aeruginosa liquid cultures were pre-induced with 0.2% arabinose and grown to an OD600 of ∼0.5. Then, 200 μL of culture was mixed with 5 mL 0.35% LB top agar with 0.2% arabinose and poured onto a plate with the appropriate antibiotic. A 10-fold dilution series of phage was prepared in phage buffer and 3 μL were spotted for each dilution and incubated at 30°C or 37°C (clinical isolates) for 15 to 18 h.

#### Efficiency of plating

The efficiency of plating for PhiKZ was determined by the plaque assay on K2733 lawns expressing DarTG2 or empty vector as described above. The titer of each PhiKZ strain on DarTG2 expressing strains was divided by the average titer of the same strain on empty vector expressing lawns.

#### 90-Min progeny collection assay

Assay was performed essentially as described previously. 32 Briefly, 1 mL of P. aeruginosa culture at an OD of 1.0 was infected with either PhiKZ or PhiPA3 at an MOI of ∼2.5 and incubated for 5 min. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation, washed three times with growth media to remove excess phage, and then incubated for an additional 90 min. Cells were then pelleted, supernatant removed, and remaining cells and cell debris removed by chloroform clearing for 1 min at a ratio of 1:100 (chloroform:supernatant). Further experimentation with the resulting lysate was then performed as indicated.

#### Toxin-antitoxin assays

CW41 DarT2, CW41 DarG2, and PhiKZ AdfM sequences were ordered as gene fragments from TWIST Biosciences. CW41 DarG and PhiKZ AdfM were cloned into a p15a-CmR plasmid under the andydrotetracycline (aTc) inducible pTet promoter using Golden Gate assembly. 56 CW41 DarT was cloned into an SC101-KanR plasmid under the crystal violet (CV) inducible pJEX promoter 57 using Golden Gate assembly. 56 Both sets of assemblies were co-transformed into DH10b E. coli (Intact Genomics) and sequence-verified using whole plasmid sequencing (Plasmidsaurus), followed by read alignment (Geneious). Sequence-confirmed cotransformants were stored at −80°C as 20% glycerol (v/v) until further use.

To perform solid agar toxin antitoxin assays, LB Agar plates were freshly poured and dried under flame with the following supplements: 35 μg/mL Chloramphenicol, 50 μg/mL Kanamycin, variable amounts of CV to induce DarT expression and variable amounts of aTc to induce DarG expression. For CW41 DarT expression conditions, +50 nM CV was added. For CW41 DarG and PhiKZ AdfM expression conditions, +2 nM aTc was added. Once the agar was dried, 3 independent overnight cultures containing expression vectors for DarT and DarG/AdfM were plated in 10x serial dilutions with 5 μL spots and let dry under flame. Once dried, plates were incubated at 30°C overnight. To let colonies mature for imaging, plates were transferred to a 37°C incubator for an additional 24 h. Colonies were imaged and counted.

To perform liquid culture toxin antitoxin assays, 3 independent overnight cultures containing expression vectors for DarT and DarG/AdfM were inoculated in LB media at 8e6 CFU in a Corning 3903 plate with the following supplements: 35 μg/mL Chloramphenicol, 50 μg/mL Kanamycin, variable amounts of CV to induce DarT expression and variable amounts of aTc to induce DarG expression. For DarT expression conditions, +125 nM CV was used. For DarG/AdfM expression conditions, +12.5 nM aTc was used. The plate was monitored in a Cytation5 plate reader (Biotek) at 30°C, 807 rpm and OD600 measured every 5 min for 12 h. Data were plotted using the Seaborn package in python.

#### ADP-ribose dot blot

P. aeruginosa K2733 transformed with pHERD-30T expressing either CW41 DarTG2 or empty vector control was grown to OD 600 = 1.0 in LB supplemented with 15 μg/mL gentamicin and 0.2% arabinose. 5 mL of culture was infected with wild type PhiKZ or PhiKZ del-AdfM 3 at an MOI of 1. Infections were allowed to proceed at 30°C with rotation for 45 min. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, washed with phosphate buffered saline, and resuspended in 1 mL of Tris-EDTA-SDS lysis buffer (1.0% SDS, 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) at 95°C for five minutes. Lysates were treated with proteinase K (100 μg/mL) for 1 h at 50°C. To isolate DNA, 1 mL of lysed cells were mixed with an equal volume of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1 v/v) in 5PRIME phase lock gel tubes and centrifuged for 5 min at 11,000 rpm. The top aqueous layer was removed and 2 mL of 100% EtOH was added followed by incubation for 10 min at room temperature. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation for 10 min at 11,000 rpm and the supernatant was discarded. Pellets were air-dried for 10 min and resuspended in 50 μL of sterile water.

For dot-blotting, a nitrocellulose membrane was spotted with 2 μg of isolated DNA and cross-linked at 1,200 J with a Stratalinker UV cross-linker. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in 5% milk in TBST (100 mM Tris pH 8.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20) followed by incubation with rabbit anti-pan-ADP-ribose antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, MABE1016) at 1:1000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed 3 times in TBST for 5 min each wash, then incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Cell Signaling, #7074S) at 1:1000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed 3 times, incubated with Pierce ECL Plus substrate for 1 min, and then imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imager.

#### RNA isolation, library preparation, sequencing and analysis

Samples were collected at 40 mpi in the stop buffer (phenol:ethanol 5%:95%). Snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in −20°C. Cells were pelleted at 3000 rpm in a swinging bucket rotor, the pellet washed with 1x DPBS (Millipore Sigma D4031–1L), then RNA was isolated using an RNA isolation kit (New England Biolabs T2010). The integrity and quality of RNA was analyzed using Agilent TapeStation 4150. For library preparation, 1 μg of total RNA was used. The rRNA was removed and remaining RNA was purified using magnetic beads (New England Biolabs E7860L). The cDNA was synthesized and libraries were prepared (New England Biolabs E7770S, New England Biolabs E7600S). The samples were sequenced using Nova-Seq 6000 illumina platform and >20 million of 50 base pair (bp) paired-end reads were collected per sample. The quality of reads were determined using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The reads with greater than Q30 values were selected for further analysis. The reads were aligned to the PhiKZ genome (GCF_000842965.1) using bowtie2/2.5.0. 58 The duplicated reads were removed using Samtools and sam files were converted to bam files and sorted. 59 Used featureCounts to obtain a count matrix from the bam files 60 and wrote the data to a CSV. The correlation coefficient for RNA-seq biological replicates was calculated using R’s cor function with the ‘‘pearson’’ method. This function measured the linear relationship between gene expression values in the dataset. The result was a correlation matrix, representing the pairwise correlations between replicates and different samples. A heatmap was generated using the pheatmap (available at https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap) function to visualize these correlations, aiding in the assessment of reproducibility and quality control in RNA-seq experiments. Further the bedgraph files were generated using bedtools and data was visualized in IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer).

#### Nuclear DNA fluorescence quantification

Single-cell infection and fluorescence microscopy were performed as described. Nuclear cross-sectional area was determined by tracing the border of the DAPI-stained nuclei in ImageJ at its widest point. The area was then measured by ImageJ in μm 2 and the average fluorescence intensity measured in artificial units. The area multiplied by the average intensity was used to determine total nuclear fluorescence. Seventy-five nuclei were measured per condition, measurements were plotted with PRISM, normalization was performed to the average of the wildtype condition, and statistical significance was determined by the two-sided t test.

#### qPCR DNA quantification

Samples were collected at 0 and 45 mpi and an immediate stop buffer (95% ethanol, 5% Phenol) was added and cells were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Later cells were pelleted and washed with 1x PBS (Gibco, 10010072). Then, DNA was isolated using a Quick-DNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, D4068). The concentration of genomic DNA was analyzed using a Qubit (Invitrogen). For qPCR, DNA was diluted to 10 ng/μL, and the qPCR reaction was set up using Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (M3003S). The data were normalized first against samples collected at 0 mpi, and then by empty-vector controls. Graphs were generated using Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software). Significance was calculated using unpaired t-tests with 95% confidence intervals.

#### Cryo-electron microscopy of viral progeny

90-minute progeny collection assay was performed as described above. PhiKZ viral particles in lysate were concentrated using a 50 kDa molecular weight cutoff protein concentrator (EMD Millipore), and 3 μL of concentrated phage suspension was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil Cu 1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 95% humidity and 4°C. Grids were imaged on a Titan Krios G3 cryoelectron microscope operating at 300 keV and equipped with a Gatan K3 detector. Searchmaps were collected on the using the EPU (Thermo Fisher) Tomography module at 8,700x magnification (corresponding to a pixel size of ∼1nm) by manually navigating to suitable grid locations and placing targets accordingly. Phage particles from the resulting search maps were manually scored as DNA-filled if they were electron-dense and empty if not, and scored as tailed or tailless based on the presence of an attached tail.

#### Cryo-electron tomography of PhiKZ-infected cells

Cells expressing CW41 DarTG were infected with either PhiKZ WT or PhiKZ del Adfm-3 at OD 600 = ∼0.5–1 and were concentrated to OD 600 ∼4 at 40 mpi. 4 μL concentrated cells was dropped onto the Quantifoil R1/4 Cu grids that had been discharged at 0.2 mBar for 1 min each side in the PELCO easiGlow device right before plunging. Grids were plunged by a custom-built manual plunging device (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry) and excess liquid blotted with filter paper (Whatman no. 1) from the backside of the grid for 6 s prior to freezing in a 50:50 ethane:propane mixture (Airgas) cooled by liquid nitrogen. Grids were clipped into modified Autogrids (Thermo Fisher Scientific) compatible with cryo-focused ion-beam (cryo-FIB) milling. Samples were loaded into an Aquilos 2 dual beam microscope (TFS) and milled to generate lamellae approximately 100–200 nm thick as previously described. 61 

Cryo-EM data for PhiKZ WT infected cells was collected on a Titan Krios G3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 keV equipped with a K3 detector and 1067HD BioContinuum energy filter (Gatan) with 15 eV slit-width. Tilt series were acquired at a pixel size of 1.635 Å /pixel with 3.24 e–/Å /tilt and exposure of 0.36 s/tilt, resulting in a total dose of 120 e/Å. Cryo-EM data for PhiKZ del Adfm-3 infected cells was collected on a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 keV equipped with a Falcon 4 detector and Selectris X energy filter. Tilt series were acquired at a pixel size of 1.561 Å/pixel with 3.24 e− /Å/tilt and exposure of 0.845 s/tilt, resulting in a total dose of 120 e/Å. All tilt series were acquired with tilt range of ±54° and 3° increments starting from a lamella pre-tilt of 9° using the dose-symmetric scheme. Data was acquired automatically using SerialEM v4.1-beta 62 with parallel cryo electron tomography (PACE-tomo) scripts 63 and a 4 μm nominal defocus. All tilt series were processed and reconstructed into tomograms using Warp v2.0.0-beta. 64 

Membrane segmentations were generated from deconvolved tomograms using membrain-seg v10 from the developers. 52 Phage nuclei were traced and segmented in IMOD v5.1.2. 65 Ribosomes were detected by the crYOLO deep-learning particle-picker in tomography mode. 51 Scenes depicting ribosomes and membrane segmentations were rendered in ChimeraX 66 with the ArtiaX plug-in. 53 Cryo-EM map of PhiKZ capsid (EMD-39002) was fitted into the segmented capsid density to better visualize the capsid surface and phage genome within capsids. A total of 8 tomograms from PhiKZ WT–infected cells and 12 tomograms from PhiKZ del Adfm-3–infected cells were scored for capsid filling with genomic DNA.

#### Relative nuclear fraction fluorescence quantification

Single-cell infection and fluorescence microscopy were performed as described. Fluorescence intensity was measured in artificial units in the cytoplasm and in the nucleus with ImageJ. The sum of these two values per cell was normalized to one and the fraction in each compartment computed. Thirty cells were measured per condition, measurements were plotted with PRISM, and statistical significance was determined by two-sided t test.

#### Relative plaque area quantification

Spot titers were performed as described. Plates were imaged with Chemidoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Plaques were traced in ImageJ software. Average plaque area for the wildtype condition was normalized to one for comparison. Normalization was performed per plate to avoid plate-specific differences. A total of thirty plaques across three independent plates were measured per condition, measurements were plotted with PRISM, and statistical significance was determined by two-sided t test.

### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 10 software. For all experiments, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Sample size, exact p-value, and statistical method used can found in each figure and/or figure legend. Nonsignificant differences are indicated as “ns”.
