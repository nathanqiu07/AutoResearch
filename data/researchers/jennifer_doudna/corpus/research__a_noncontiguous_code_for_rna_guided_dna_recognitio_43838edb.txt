# A Noncontiguous Code for RNA-Guided DNA Recognition at the Origin of CRISPR-Cas

## Abstract

CRISPR-Cas provides RNA-mediated adaptive immunity, but how its first RNA-guided effector arose is unclear. Here we report the discovery of Viral Interference Programmable Repeat (VIPR) systems consisting of a Vipr protein ancestral to the earliest CRISPR-Cas effector and VIPR RNAs (vrRNAs) comprising alternating GGY/NN motifs. Unlike canonical guide RNAs that pair with target nucleic acids via contiguous complementarity, vrRNAs recognize double-stranded DNA through a noncontiguous code in which the variable NNs dinucleotides collectively specify a gapped target sequence. Natural vrRNA targets suggest VIPR systems act against competing phages, and we demonstrate programmable phage defense by redirecting the complex for transcriptional repression. These results suggest that the root of adaptive immunity lies in ancient warfare between viruses, and reveal a new logic for encoding information in sequence.

RNA-guided systems mediate diverse functions ranging from mobile genetic element propagation to adaptive immunity. These systems comprise proteins that use guide RNAs bearing sequence complementarity to target nucleic acids, enabling programmable recognition of different substrates by the same protein. In all RNA-guided proteins, target specificity is determined by contiguous complementarity between the guide and its target, and can be altered to direct the protein to a new substrate (1-4). In CRISPR-Cas systems, this versatility enables adaptive immunity in prokaryotes (5-7), and powers wide-ranging programmable biotechnologies that work across all domains of life (8, 9).

CRISPR-Cas systems comprise two classes based on their effector proteins (8, 10). Class 1 systems, the more abundant of the two, are thought to have evolved first (11). Their defining feature is Repeat-Associated Mysterious Proteins (RAMPs) that oligomerize on the CRISPR RNA (crRNA) to form the RNA-guided effector complex. RAMPs are of the most conserved and ancient feature of CRISPR-Cas, proposed to date back to the last universal common ancestor (11, 12). Nevertheless, their origins remain unclear. We sought to identify ancestral RAMPs as a path to discovering the evolutionary origins of CRISPR-Cas systems.

## Results

### VIPR systems are phage-encoded and defined by an ancestral RAMP protein

RAMPs share little sequence similarity, rendering conventional homology search methods ineffective for identifying related proteins. Instead, we took advantage of shared RAMP structural features including the RNA Recognition Motif (RRM) core, the glycine-rich loop (G-loop), and the thumb domain that all mediate crRNA binding (fig. S1A) (11). These conserved features enabled structure similarity searches against the clustered AlphaFold database following the structure-guided mining strategy we previously used to uncover CRISPR-Cas13 origins (Fig. 1A; table S1) (13-16). These searches revealed a large and previously unreported family of RAMP homologs (n = 2,664 on NCBI NR database and present in 0.07 % of prokaryotic genomes on NCBI RefSeq) encoded as part of a system we named Viral Interference Programmable Repeat (VIPR) (Fig. 1A; fig. S1B, table S2).

Two features distinguish VIPR systems from CRISPR-Cas systems. First, VIPR systems are minimal, and typically include only two protein-coding genes, the vipr gene (the RAMP homolog) and a vipr-accessory protein (hereon vap) (Fig. 1B). In contrast, CRISPR-Cas systems encode numerous cas genes (often multiple RAMPs), as well as CRISPR arrays (Fig. 1B). Notably, while VIPR systems lack CRISPR arrays, the vipr gene is typically flanked by long intergenic sequences that could encode alternative noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) distinct from crRNAs (Fig. 1B). Second, unlike CRISPR-Cas systems that occur primarily in cellular genomes, VIPR systems are found primarily in bacteriophages and sometimes in archaeal viruses (Fig. 1C). More specifically, most VIPR systems are in proviral (56%) and viral (14.8%) genomes, although some are also found in prokaryotic chromosomes (25.5%) and plasmids (3.8%) (Fig. 1C; table S3). The distinct locus architecture and primarily phage-encoded nature of VIPR systems suggest their biological function and mechanism may be fundamentally different from those of CRISPR-Cas.

Nevertheless, VIPR systems and CRISPR-Cas are related, and several lines of evidence suggest VIPR systems may represent the ancestral form. Just as VIPR systems are minimal compared to CRISPR-Cas systems, Vipr proteins are simpler than CRISPR RAMPs, lacking the secondary RRM domain and other elaborations (fig. S1B). These elaborations are derived RAMP features, so their absence in Vipr proteins is parsimoniously consistent with the ancestral state. Moreover, Vipr proteins show high sequence and taxonomic diversity (figs. S2, 3; table S3), suggesting the lineage had ample time to diversify and spread across many taxa. Finally, Vipr proteins form an early branching clade separate from all CRISPR RAMPs (Cas5, Cas6, Cas7) (Fig. 1D; figs. S4, 5; data S1). Combining these observations, we propose that Vipr proteins represent an ancestral RAMP lineage that diverged prior to the radiation of CRISPR-RAMPs.

### VIPR systems encode a tandem repeat-containing RNA

Vipr proteins contain G-loop and thumb domains that in CRISPR RAMPs enable guide RNA recognition (fig. S1), which raises the possibility that Vipr proteins also utilize guide RNAs. To test this, we infected E. coli with the phage SUSP1 that natively encodes a VIPR system and performed small RNA sequencing (sRNA-seq) (Fig. 2A). We detected four ~100-nucleotide transcripts encoded within the intergenic regions flanking the vipr-vap operon hereafter called VIPR RNAs (vrRNAs) (Fig. 2A). One vrRNA was upstream, whereas three were in an array downstream of the protein-coding genes (Fig. 2A). E. coli plasmid-based expression of SUSP1 and the closely related Pseudomonas fulva prophage (hereafter P. fulva) VIPR systems also showed clear evidence of vrRNA expression (Fig. 2B; fig. S6). Moreover, vrRNAs of both systems co-purify with their respective Vipr proteins, suggesting that the two form a stable ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex (Fig. 2B; fig. S6).

Analysis of vrRNA sequences using the genomic language model Evo2 (17) revealed that vrRNAs comprise 9-16 YNNGG tandem repeats in which a conserved GGY motif (Y = pyrimidine; N = any base) alternates with hypervariable NN dinucleotides (Fig. 2C; fig. S7). Evo2 was critical because standard alignment based methods failed to reveal obvious features. In contrast, the alignment-free approach of predicting base probabilities of vrRNAs using Evo2 revealed clear patterns (Fig. 2C; fig. S7). This yielded conservation scores that were high for the GGY segments, but low for the NN segments, which correspondingly showed high-entropy scores (Fig. 2C; fig. S7). Consistent with this, manual inspection of 137 unique vrRNA sequences from SUSP1 and P. fulva VIPR system groups (i.e., VIPR systems whose Vipr protein sequences are nearly identical) confirmed that all vrRNAs maintained the conserved GGY motifs while the NN bases were hypervariable (fig. S8).

The tandem repeat tract of vrRNAs precedes an invariable 3' extension hereafter referred to as the tail (Fig. 2D). RNA-folding analysis suggests that the tail adopts a conserved secondary structure that includes a pseudoknot in the SUSP1 VIPR system group (Fig. 2E, fig. S9). This two-part organization where a variable region is capped by a structurally conserved region parallels crRNAs, in which the programmable spacer is capped by the repeat handle.

### vrRNAs use a tandem repeat code to recognize DNA

We wondered whether the similar architecture of vrRNAs and crRNAs reflects a role of vrRNAs in nucleic acid targeting. Considering how alternating GGY/NN motifs could confer target specificity, we hypothesized that the conserved GGY positions do not participate in base pairing, while the variable NN positions specify the nucleic acid target. This was inspired by Class 1 CRISPR, where RAMP subunit thumb domains disrupt basepairing at every sixth position of the crRNA-target duplex (18). We hypothesized that the VIPR subunit thumb domains could similarly preclude basepairing at every GGY position along the vrRNA-target duplex and position the hypervariable NN nucleotides spatially for target recognition.

We tested this model of noncontiguous complementarity by searching the E. coli pangenome using inferred targets of SUSP1 vrRNAs (Fig. 3A). For each vrRNA, we concatenated the NN dinucleotides with 0-3 intervening wildcard bases (i.e., NN, xNN, xxNN, or xxxNN) to model targets with different “skip base” lengths (Fig. 3A). We hypothesized that skip bases range from zero if NN dinucleotides are read directly without gaps, to three if non-base-paired positions mirror the full GGY trinucleotide. Targets modeled with a single skip base (every GGY/NN converted to xNN; the "1 nt skip rule") yielded substantially more high-quality matches than any alternative (Fig. 3B, C; data S2). Among the top matches, most inferred 1 nt skip rule targets perfectly matched protein coding sequences, with skip bases overwhelmingly aligning to the third (wobble) position of codons (fig. S10). Moreover, high quality matches sites could be found on both strands (fig. S10), consistent with DNA targeting by VIPR systems.

Both in vitro biochemical and in vivo functional assays confirmed the validity of the 1 nt skip rule. We reconstituted SUSP1 VIPR RNP in vitro and tested binding to double-stranded DNA targets designed with 0-3 nt skip lengths (Fig. 3D; fig. S11). The RNP bound all four substrates in the absence of a competitor, but in the presence of excess competitor DNA, only the 1 nt skip substrate remained bound. We corroborated this result in vivo by placing target sites designed with 0-3 nt skips upstream of a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter (Fig. 3E), reasoning that specific binding would repress transcription as in CRISPRi (19). Only the 1 nt skip target sequence caused GFP repression (Fig. 3E; fig. S12A), and deletion of vipr or perturbation of vrRNA GGY motifs or NN basepairing abolished silencing (Fig. 3E; fig. S12B). Unlike dCas9-based CRISPRi, targeting either coding or template strands silenced GFP expression (Fig. 3E; fig. S12B) (19). Genome-wide binding profiling by ChIP-seq confirmed RNP occupancy at the predicted target site (ranked first of 872 called peaks), consistent with repression through direct binding (fig. S13).

VIPR systems are programmable systems in which vrRNAs dictate target specificity. Testing different native SUSP1 vrRNAs confirmed that each RNA silenced GFP expression when its predicted target was placed upstream of the GFP reporter (fig. S12C). Moreover, specificity is entirely reprogrammable by altering only the NN dinucleotide regions. Three of four vrRNAs reprogrammed to target the promoter or adjacent regions effectively repressed GFP (Fig. 3F). Target recognition did not require sequence constraints beyond the 1 nt skip rule, thus lacking an analogous protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM) found in CRISPR-Cas enzymes as predicted by our bioinformatic analysis (fig. S14). Together, these results establish that VIPR RNP recognizes DNA through noncontiguous complementarity, with target specificity encoded entirely by the NN dinucleotides of the alternating GGY/NN motifs.

### VIPR systems repress competing mobile genetic elements

Decoding the tandem repeat specificity rule allowed us to predict natural vrRNA targets, revealing VIPR systems to be an agent of inter-phage warfare. In the SUSP1 VIPR system group, 18 of 20 vrRNA targets converge on a satellite phage that likely parasitizes SUSP1 (Fig. 4A; data S3), as inferred by shared homology between their structural genes (fig. S15). Given SUSP1 and its relatives are lytic phages, this VIPR system group is likely deployed offensively. Some of these satellites encode their own vrRNAs directed at other closely related satellites, revealing layers of conflict among competing elements (Fig. 4A; data S3). In the P. fulva VIPR system group, 48 of 66 vrRNA targets map to co-resident Pseudomonas prophages, some closely related to the phages encoding these VIPR systems and others not (data S3). Of these, 36 concentrate in the genomic neighborhood encoding the lysogeny-lytic switch, primarily targeting transcriptional regulators (fig. S16; data S3). Notably, some vrRNAs target other VIPR systems in the clade, revealing that VIPR systems are at times aimed at one another, consistent with a defensive role (fig. S16; data S3). VIPR thus operates as both sword and shield in inter-phage conflict.

We tested two scenarios to examine whether VIPR systems can control the behavior of phages. First, we tested VIPR’s ability to defend against an invading phage. We challenged E. coli expressing a VIPR system from a plasmid with vrRNAs targeting the essential lytic regulator cro in an obligately lytic λ variant (Fig. 4B). cro-targeting guides reduced λ phage plaquing efficiency by five orders of magnitude and conferred immunity in liquid culture, while a non-targeting guide conferred no protection (Fig. 4C, D). Mutating any of the twelve NN dinucleotides in the vrRNA greatly reduced protection (fig. S17). Notably, co-expression of vap1 from the SUSP1 VIPR operon further enhanced protection (Fig. 2A, 4E). Second, we tested VIPR’s ability to force a resident prophage into lysis by targeting the lysogenic regulator cI in an E. coli λ-lysogen (Fig. 4F). cI-targeting vrRNAs triggered prophage induction, while cro-targeting and non-targeting vrRNAs produced no observable induction (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that VIPR systems are flexible systems for control over inter-phage conflict.

### Diverse VIPR systems link inter-phage conflict to adaptive immunity

VIPR systems are diverse and universally built around the tandem repeat recognition code. They comprise seven distinct types defined by Vipr protein phylogeny, vrRNA architecture, and accessory gene associations (Fig. 5A; fig. S18; data S4). Most types are monophyletic, with vrRNA architecture and accessory gene content aligning with Vipr clades. The exceptions are type I, which we defined as all clades lacking accessory proteins, and type II, which shares a common accessory gene yet comprises three distantly related Vipr clades. All seven types appear to encode vrRNAs typically comprising 6 to 15 alternating GGY/NN repeats or a related consensus generally conserved within each type (fig. S19).

Beyond these type-defining features, VIPR systems vary in array architecture and accessory gene function (Fig. 5A). In addition to the array architectures in type II VIPR systems characterized here, there exist staggered arrays with overlapping vrRNAs (fig. S20), hammerhead ribozyme-interspersed arrays (fig. S21), as well as systems that consistently encode only one vrRNA. The latter two were confirmed by sRNA-sequencing and notably lack the tail found in type II systems (fig. S22; supplementary text). In addition, although all six vap genes (vap1-6) appear to be involved in DNA interaction, their predicted functions are variable (Fig. 5A). In some systems, as in the case of type II, VI, and VII, vap genes encode predicted DNA binding proteins, whereas types III-V encode predicted enzymes such as DNA nucleases or helicases (fig. S23).

Some VIPR systems appear to have been repurposed from inter-viral warfare to host-virus warfare. For instance, types III and V are enriched in defense islands of host chromosomes and their vrRNAs target viral sequences, suggesting host immune functions (fig. S24; data S3, 4). Broader analyses of genomic context and targeting preference across different types is provided in supplementary text. Notably, these antiviral VIPR systems are the only types that encode predicted nucleases, suggesting their primary mode of activity is target destruction rather than repression. These observations imply that the ancestral viral targeting function of VIPR systems persisted following horizontal transfer to prokaryotic genomes, and prokaryotes repurposed these from inter-phage competition to host anti-phage immunity by gaining new vap genes (Fig. 5B). The direct ancestors of modern CRISPR systems may have followed a similar trajectory to these extant VIPR systems to give rise to adaptive immunity.

## Discussion

VIPR systems constitute a previously unknown mechanism of RNA-guided DNA recognition for genetic control that possibly pre-date the emergence of CRISPR pathways. Unlike all other known types of guide RNAs in which contiguous segments determine target specificity, vrRNAs instead distribute this information periodically. Vipr proteins use this code, stored in the form of a gapped array of NN dinucleotides within the 9-16 YNNGG vrRNA tandem repeats, for noncontiguous DNA target recognition.

The accompanying structural manuscript provides a mechanistic basis for this encode-decode logic (20). Vipr proteins oligomerize along the vrRNA by directly binding the conserved GGY motifs, while presenting the intervening NN dinucleotides for base pairing to the target. Vipr protein binding distorts the GGY bases such that a single nucleotide on the target strand spans the three GGY bases, converting each GGY/NN unit into an xNN target. Because this transformation is asymmetric, vrRNA cannot target the DNA encoding it, thus presenting a distinct mechanism of distinguishing self versus non-self. The structural and functional data presented in the two studies reveal a novel way that sequence can encode information, distributing it periodically across noncontiguous positions.

The finding of at least seven different types of VIPR systems within bacteriophage and prokaryotic genomes, many of which target other phages, implies an ancient and widespread network for programmable virus genetic control. Our data suggest that VIPR systems are anti-phage systems used for control of phage propagation by targeted transcriptional silencing. In principle, phage can develop resistance to direct sequence targeting by acquiring mutations in synonymous codons with variation at the third codon nucleotide, the wobble position. Notably, in VIPR systems, the skipped nucleotide in the target DNA predominantly corresponds to the codon wobble position. This skipping logic would allow VIPR systems to overcome possible viral resistance by synonymous codon swaps.

VIPR systems help fill a long-standing gap in the earliest steps of CRISPR-Cas evolution and raise the possibility that viral conflict seeded prokaryotic adaptive immunity. We propose that bacteria co-opted ancestral VIPR-like systems used in inter-phage warfare for host defense (Fig. 5B; fig. S24). Coupling of such defense-associated VIPR-like systems with a Cas1-like transposase (e.g., Casposase) would have enabled heritable immune memory and given rise to the first CRISPR system (11, 21). Subsequent duplication and diversification of the ancestral RAMP could have then produced the specialized Class 1 CRISPR RAMPs (10, 11). Finally, exaptation of the CRISPR array and acquisition machinery with different non-RAMP effectors from transposons (IscB, TnpB) and toxin-antitoxin systems (AbiD/F) led to the independent emergence of the three distinct types of Class 2 CRISPR systems (10, 11, 16, 22-27).

Mechanism of vrRNA acquisition is an open question, as noncontiguous targeting information cannot arise via wholesale spacer insertion as in CRISPR. Despite the apparent lack of a dedicated acquisition gene analogous to Cas1 of CRISPR, VIPR systems evolve their vrRNAs rapidly. While analyzing SUSP1 and P. fulva VIPR systems, we observed that loci encoding near-identical Vipr proteins typically harbor vrRNA arrays differing in size and content, indicating whole vrRNAs can be gained or lost. Nevertheless, individual vrRNAs within otherwise divergent vrRNA arrays were also sometimes shared, suggesting diversification of vrRNA array content may arise via stepwise turnover. We also observed that at a finer scale, closely related vrRNAs differ by insertions or deletions of whole repeat units, suggesting that vrRNAs also diversify by incorporating mutations within the repeat regions as long as they maintain the periodicity.

VIPR systems require only a single small protein (<200 amino acids) and a single guide RNA (<100 nucleotides). They recognize target sequences located on either DNA strand of a gene without an obvious PAM-like constraint, and are readily reprogrammable. These properties make VIPR systems among the most minimal and potentially versatile platforms for RNA-guided DNA recognition yet described. In addition to transcriptional regulation, synthetic Vipr fusions could enable applications including genome editing, DNA locus imaging and epigenetic modification. More broadly, the discovery of a new code for RNA-mediated sequence recognition expands the known design space for programmable genetic control and hints that other modes of encoding genetic information remain to be found.
