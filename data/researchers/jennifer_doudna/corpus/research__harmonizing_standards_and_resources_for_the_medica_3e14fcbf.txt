## Abstract

Realizing the promise of precision medicine will require the highest standards of accuracy in genome sequencing and analysis. Here we describe challenges and opportunities for the field through the lens of genome data quality. We present recommendations in the context of specific areas of application for genomic sequencing in which isolated standards have arisen: germline sequencing, tumor sequencing, cell-free DNA testing, and sequencing for quality control in genetic therapy. Despite these distinct clinical contexts, technical challenges are often similar; for example, accurately detecting low-frequency genetic variants in tumor sequencing or gene edited cells. We call for increased synchronization among these communities to establish new medical genome standards that promote confidence in genomic diagnostics and genetic therapies in a time of rapid technology-driven change. We suggest practical approaches for implementing these genome standards across contexts and identify key areas that require further development.

## Introduction

Sequencing the first human genome promised new genetic discoveries, diagnostics, and future therapies 1,2. Realizing that promise requires sequencing subsequent genomes more fully and accurately, a principle fundamental to replication in science and safety in medicine. Over the last two decades, sequencing technologies have continuously advanced in quality, making challenging genome regions like telomeres and centromeres accessible 3. Computer algorithms that parse sequencing data have also improved. Community standards have emerged both in science and medicine, but these standards have naturally diverged 4. In a discovery experiment, a successful outcome is defined by at least one verifiable novel finding; genome sequencing errors distant to the locus of discovery are important but harbor minimal consequence to the central aim of scientific discovery. However, in clinical diagnostics, if one base pair of one important gene is reported in error, it could mean a missed or mistaken diagnosis. The challenge is amplified for low frequency variants, such as in cancer diagnostics due to cellular heterogeneity of tumors or the sparsity and stochasticity of circulating blood nucleic acids, or in genetic therapy applications requiring precise detection of off-target editing in cellular subpopulations where genome engineering constructs destined for human therapies are tested or deployed.

Continuous increases in genomic sequencing accuracy have been achieved over many years (Fig. 1) 5,6 but these efforts must be scaled for genomic medicine to realize its full potential. Some sequencing technologies continue to poorly characterize certain areas of the genome. Ancestral diversity of population datasets is improving but remains far from optimal 7. Structural variant calling is an area with minimal consensus and few population-scale standard datasets. New reference data have expanded the genome map but have yet to be incorporated broadly into reference standards or routine analysis pipelines. Long-read approaches can increasingly detect epigenetic marks, but standards do not yet exist for these methods despite well-recognized clinical syndromes for which they may be relevant. In genetic therapies, standards for detecting off-target editing events and distinguishing them from sequencing errors are still emerging.

As the regulatory landscape for genome sequencing continues to evolve 8, community consensus can ensure high testing standards and safety are upheld without undue burden on diagnostics labs and therapeutics companies. In this paper we outline challenges and opportunities for the field of genomics and genetic therapies through the lens of genome data quality. After summarizing key technical elements, we frame our discussion and recommendations (Box 1) for sequencing in the context of germline, tumor, cell-free DNA, and genetic therapy applications. A decade after introducing high throughput sequencing to clinical medicine, we call for increased synchronization among these applications, to establish a new medical genome standard. Such a standard would accelerate improvements in accuracy of clinical genomics and promote widespread patient confidence and regulatory body conviction in genomic diagnostics and therapies at a time of rapid technology-driven change.

## Genome technologies and standards

### The evolution of sequencing technology

The process of sequencing (Fig. 2) begins by purifying DNA from cells in saliva, tumor, or blood, then, in most cases, fragmenting it using ultrasound. In short read sequencing (Fig. 2A), adapters stabilize the DNA on a glass slide where a DNA polymerase incorporates fluorescently tagged nucleotides in a new DNA strand complementary to the template strand. This fluorescence is detected by a sensor and the process repeats for hundreds of cycles across millions of clusters. The resulting text file contains millions of short DNA “reads” that are then mapped to their matching position in the human reference sequence. This “pile up” map of reads is then read position by position to identify places where the genome of interest varies from the reference genome. These variants are placed in a variant call file, typically 4-5 million lines long representing genome sites where the genome of interest varies from the reference.

Short read sequencing has limitations in accurately characterizing the complexity of the human genome 4. Nearly half the genome comprises repeat tracts that can be impossible to fully characterize when the tract length exceeds the short read length. This is the case in neurodegenerative disease repeat expansions, such as Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), where a repeating six nucleotide pattern often spans several hundred base pairs. Furthermore, longer repeat expansions are typically associated with more severe clinical phenotypes, yet this is precisely where the technology demonstrates its greatest limitations. The reliance on a reference sequence can also lead to challenges in mapping, particularly for genes with pseudogenes or for gene families where many regions of similarity make it impossible to confidently map a short read to a single location. Highly polymorphic regions such as the KIR region, the B- and T-cell receptor loci, and the HLA locus make accurate mapping difficult (Box 2). The original reference sequence was a mosaic haploid mixture of the genomes of originally two and now more than 60 individuals with artificially introduced boundaries between sequences. Seventy percent of the sequence still derives from a single individual 9. The reference contains risk variants that are not called by traditional algorithms when homozygous pathogenic variants are present in the genome of focus 10. De novo assembly can address some of these issues, but is computationally intensive and rarely used in practice.

Long read sequencing approaches (Fig. 2B) provide solutions to some of these challenges. In Single Molecule Real Time (SMRT) sequencing, the DNA polymerase is stabilized in a well. In nanopore sequencing, single stranded DNA is pulled through a protein pore. In synthetic approaches, long DNA molecules are tagged in a way that allows the longer molecule to be rebuilt in silico after fragmentation and short read sequencing. These approaches, along with newer short read approaches, provide significant benefits in mapping 11. For difficult to sequence areas, however, the extended read length of native long read sequencing offers distinct advantages 12, such as spanning and better characterizing repetitive regions that exceed short-read lengths (e.g., short tandem repeats), and improving mapping, variant calling, and detection, particularly for structural variants 13.

### Sequencing metrics are key standards

The fundamental quality metric in sequencing, the Phred quality score, describes the confidence with which an individual base is identified and represents the -10log 10 of the probability of base-calling error. Adopted widely during the Human Genome Project, Phred was developed to judge the confidence with which Sanger sequencing could identify a base 14. The metric carried over into next-generation sequencing as the Q score and became a fundamental measure through which sequencing technologies would be compared. For example, a base call error rate of 1 in 100 (1%) corresponds to a Q score of 20. An error rate of 1 in 1000 (0.1%) would be referred to as Q30. A median Q30 error rate was traditionally deemed an acceptable standard for new sequencing methodologies based on the typical read quality of the dominant sequencing-by-synthesis approach. Nanopore and SMRT long read sequencing technologies have achieved error rates routinely better than Q30 and often approaching Q40 15,16. Newer short read approaches, such as sequencing-by-avidity 17,18 and sequencing-by-binding 19 appear to be associated with lower error rates, routinely reaching Q40 17 and in some cases, Q50 18. While such advances are notable, there may be limits to our ability to ascribe base accuracies of Q50 and above because of errors arising from sources other than sequencing. Pre-sequencing artifacts from library preparation significantly impact variant detection accuracy, particularly for low-frequency variants. Techniques like the addition of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) or duplex sequencing can help improve accuracy in these cases 20,21. Sequence contexts like homopolymers and G/C-rich regions can induce technology-specific systematic errors, reducing local accuracy despite high genome-wide base quality scores.

Despite their ubiquity, base quality metrics focus on a single locus, and even a Q value distribution inadequately captures the capability of a technology to characterize the variational diversity of the genome. In addition, accurate calibration between quality measures estimated by the sequencer such as Q scores, and empirically determined variant calling error is critical.

The field has benefitted (Fig. 1) from benchmark materials that enable any laboratory to measure genome-wide metrics based on a consensus call set of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) as well as small insertions and deletions from openly consented individuals in the Personal Genome Project 22,23. These materials allow comparison of genome-wide metrics of precision, recall and their harmonic mean, F1. In addition, these benchmark data include stratifications that enable users to assess performance in specific challenging areas of the genome.

### Evolution of clinical-grade standards

Several approaches have been suggested for quantifying the quality of next-generation sequencing in the context of clinical applications. One gene-specific approach 24, inspired by clinical standards from Sanger sequencing, required clinical sequencing laboratories to report the absolute number of coding base pairs (including the splice dinucleotides) that were not callable at specific quality, coverage, and mapping thresholds. A minimum standard of 20Q20 (20 reads with a base quality of Q20)—was suggested, often supplemented by a requirement for reads that were uniquely mapped to provide another dimension of confidence in the readout of a specific genomic locus.

This evolution towards per-base, per-gene metrics led to a rapidly diminishing list of medically relevant genes requiring special attention due to their complexity. In one study, 273 challenging medically relevant genes were curated as a benchmark 25. A typical example was the SMN1 and SMN2 genes, where their nearly identical sequences, copy number variation, and complex surrounding repeats complicate the genetic diagnosis of spinal muscular atrophy- a progressive, neurodegenerative disease affecting children.

Varying confidence of variant calling across variant classes in genome sequencing can necessitate ancillary tests to confirm certain classes of variation before issuing a clinical report. This impacts test turnaround time and costs for clinical labs to complement the genome sequencing. A comprehensive medical genome reference standard, along with reference materials, would help provide increased confidence in a wider array of variant calls, while reducing costs and time associated with complex validation testing.

Substantial work remains before we can declare confidence in a standalone whole genome assay that we can securely label the “medical” genome standard. Since the list of genes confidently associated with disease continues to expand, we propose that a catalogue of annotations for medically important genes with technical details of the complexities involved in clinical grade calling be originated and maintained by bodies looking to regulate these tests. This resource would support ongoing standardization efforts while incorporating evolving knowledge from the key consortia advancing data quality, reference standards, and analyses of complex regions (Table 1).

## Refining the reference sequence

Accurate variant identification is critical for precision medicine, and depends both on sequencing quality and the quality of the reference genome. Recent advances in sequencing technologies, assembly methods, graph-based models, and the inclusion of more diverse haplotypes, have significantly improved variant detection across diverse populations, and ultimately strengthened our ability to identify disease-associated variants.

### Advances in reference genomes

The Genome Reference Consortium has reported major advances in resolving complex regions of the genome. Many alternative haplotypes were included in the human reference genome assembly as extra-chromosomal aligned scaffolds in GRCh38 for highly variable regions such as the MHC and KIR regions, and they have continued to add and resolve challenging areas over time as they are elucidated by researchers 26,27 including medically relevant genes such as APOB, PRDM9, and a sequence area on the short arm of chromosome 21 that appeared to be incorrectly duplicated in GRCh38 28. Many other genes remain unresolved due to challenges with repetitive and structurally complex regions 25.

A major step forward in the quality of the human genome comes from the work of the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium 29. Inspired by improvements in long-read sequencing, the consortium aimed to resolve the remaining ~8% of the genome that had been out of reach with previous assembly approaches, including regions around telomeres, centromeres, and similar repeat-rich regions. The consortium added nearly 200 million base pairs of new sequence in 2022 29. This complete genome model added over a thousand new genes (of which 99 are protein-coding) and better characterized many medically important genes, such as LPA and TBC1D3. Finally, over 50 megabases of segmental duplications, areas rich in repetitive regions that lead to genomic instability, were elucidated.

Another advance came from the work of the Human Pangenome Project, which initially produced 47 phased, diploid assemblies from a genetically diverse cohort, covering more than 99% of the expected sequence in each genome at very high accuracy 30. The pangenome reference provides the first prospect of a move away from a mosaic haploid reference to a reference model where many diploid reference genomes are represented in graph format. High-quality, multi-technology assemblies of diverse diploid genomes will likely come to represent the new gold standard for a catalog of human genome reference haplotypes and variant calls 31,32. This is supported by data showing aligning reads to the pangenome reference improved variant call accuracy relative to the Genome in a Bottle consortium (GIAB) benchmarks by 34% and increased the number of structural variants per haplotype by 104% 30.

De novo genome assembly—assembly without the use of a reference sequence—was originally highly computationally demanding and used only in certain contexts where uncertainty in short read mapping arose. With current read accuracies and long read lengths, assembly of long reads now represents similar computational time as read mapping to a reference, albeit with greater memory requirement. Its tractability and benefits are increasingly apparent with long and ultra-long read sequences. Performing both long and short read sequencing of a locus can provide a custom reference sequence local assembly. More recent approaches to RNA sequencing analysis have also emphasized the benefits of a reference-free approach 33.

### Genome graphs

Genome graphs represent a new approach to the representation of variation within and among populations (Fig. 2C). Rather than a linear reference genome, graphs incorporate genetic diversity by allowing branches and alternative paths, that can capture insertions and deletions and represent other classes of variation. This improves read alignment and variant calling, particularly in highly variable regions of the genome and in diverse populations. Such pan-genome graphs can become multi-sample variant callers for whole genome assemblies 34, leading to improved accuracy and contextualization of sequenced genomes.

### A call for new reference standards

Current sequencing technical standards take the form of base level metrics and genome-wide variant class accuracy metrics, benchmarked against multi-technology consensus calls in a haploid human reference. As the genomics community moves towards the ready availability of diploid genomes, we recommend benchmarking against complete diploid sequences. This approach would incorporate into benchmarks not just the several million places the genome of interest varies from a haploid reference but the entire structural complexity of the diploid genome. It would move us away from a historical emphasis on easy to call genome variation like SNVs and towards a comprehensive approach that incorporates phased multi-variant class calls. It would inevitably herald a move away from GRCh38. We recommend the HG002 genome as a starting point as it was provided with open consent and has now been more comprehensively characterized than any other genome. Even HG002 however represents the genome of a single, Ashkenazi Jewish individual from the Personal Genome Project, so we recommend benchmarking evolve towards a panel of diverse diploid genomes, something that should be feasible in the near term 32.

One remaining challenge in this model is a reduced ability to encapsulate accuracy in a simple metric. However, we envisage benefits from a more comprehensive view of sequencing technology that incorporates genome-wide metrics for variant classes while providing detailed representation of hard to sequence areas of the genome.

In clinical genomics, reference transitions happen more slowly because of the regulatory overhead. While new sequencing technologies and algorithms proposed for clinical use would be benchmarked using the standard discussed above almost immediately, a reference transition away from GRCh38 is a significant undertaking that would take longer. The most likely transition is a move directly from GRCh38 to a pan-genome reference graph (Fig. 2C). Clinical adoption requires proactive, coordinated updates to key clinical and research databases (National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), Ensembl, ClinVar, gnomAD, and others) and standardized methods to translate findings between reference systems during the transition period.

Regardless of the reference used, there remains a need for expert assessment and resolution of challenging medically important genes. A catalogue of those genes with their architectural complexities as well as assays that have been shown to confidently resolve them will become increasingly important. We recommend the formation of a new international standards group to adjudicate this database.

## Cell-free DNA applications and standards

Cell-free DNA sequencing faces several technical challenges including access to extremely low quantities of target DNA, sensitivity to pre-analytical sample handling, and the need to routinely detect clinically important variants at extremely low allele fractions. In oncology, prenatal screening, and transplant monitoring, clinically important variants may fall below 0.1%, requiring specialized standards with higher accuracy demands than required in germline diagnostic sequencing.

### Tumor sequencing and circulating tumor DNA

In addition to characterizing heritable cancer risk through panel-based germline sequencing, clinical tumor sequencing has become common for identifying actionable somatic variation, whether for diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic uses. Accuracy challenges are unique in this setting, where low allele fraction variants within tumors that exhibit a high degree of cellular heterogeneity present a challenge usually met by targeted high depth sequencing, with various authorities recommending 250-fold 35 or 500-fold 36 minimum coverage depth. Complex structural rearrangements and large copy number aberrations are also much more common in tumors than in the germline. Specialized library techniques that allow efficient recovery of DNA from formalin-based storage have been developed to maximize yield in the face of fragmentation of nucleotides from formalin cross-linkage of the phosphodiester backbone.

While emerging data suggest the potential utility of whole genome sequencing of tumors for a range of oncology use cases such as immune cell characterization, immune repertoire characterization, tumor mutational burden, tumor neoantigen estimation, HLA typing and loss of heterozygosity 37, most current tumor genotyping efforts have focused on sequencing panels targeting actionable variants in several hundred genes relevant for therapy selection, or for diagnostic or prognostic value 38. Variants within tumors are usually quantified as a variant allele fraction (VAF), and a threshold of 1-5% has historically been recognized as a minimal standard for analytical reporting for actionable clinical use 35.

A second series of technical challenges exist for the detection of circulating tumor-derived cell-free DNA. Diagnostic use of cell-free DNA in oncology is focused on several clinical settings: (1) cancer screening in healthy subjects for early cancer detection, (2) noninvasive genotyping of actionable genetic variants in advanced cancers, (3) early response assessment after therapy, and (4) recurrence monitoring after therapy (known variably as molecular/minimal/measurable residual disease or MRD testing) 39. In all these scenarios, sensitivity is the key metric since the total amount of cell-free DNA in a typical blood sample is small and the variant allele fraction is often substantially below 0.1% 40. Tests for cell-free DNA also include targeted or genome-wide strategies for profiling 5′ CpG methylation or hydroxymethylation, and this can provide power for detection of abnormal signals across a broader portion of, or the entire, genome. Separately, the integration of signals from multiple somatic variants can help increase analytical and clinical detection limits 41 with bespoke MRD assays now capable of interrogating dozens to thousands of individual variants to allow detection in the 0.1-10 parts per million range 42,43. This increased sensitivity is reflected in improved accuracy in the prediction of disease recurrence in clinical follow up studies.

Despite many available diagnostic assays, no unified laboratory standards currently exist for limit-of-detection requirements in clinical cell-free DNA assays. Available reference materials have been instrumental in addressing this unmet need for noninvasive cell-free DNA genotyping. However, significant additional efforts are needed to evaluate the analytical and clinical validity of circulating tumor DNA sequencing assays for precision oncology, especially for MRD detection at the parts per million range. Recent efforts toward addressing this challenge for noninvasive tumor genotyping reveal high reliability at allelic levels exceeding 0.5% 44 and serve as a framework for similar performance benchmarking of ultra-sensitive MRD methods.

### Prenatal testing using cell-free DNA

Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), which targets free-floating segments of fetal DNA in maternal serum (cell-free fetal DNA) has become the standard of care aneuploidy screening method 45,46. The approach offers significant advantages over traditional aneuploidy screening, with greater than 99% sensitivity for the major aneuploidies (trisomy 21, trisomy 18, and trisomy 13) at a much lower false positive rate (less than 1%). The test can be performed earlier than traditional screening, starting at 10 weeks’ gestation.

Accuracy standards for non-invasive prenatal testing are lacking. For instance, fetal fraction is an important metric impacting test performance, but there is not clear consensus regarding how to measure or report it and there is documented variability between laboratories 47–50. A minimum cell free DNA fetal fraction of greater than 3-4% is usually required and reported, but can vary significantly due to biological factors such as maternal weight, gestational age, and multiple gestations, and technical factors like laboratory test methodology and bioinformatics algorithms. This cut-off is especially important because a “no call” result has been associated with an increased risk of aneuploidy. In addition, there are two methodological approaches for the diagnostic use of cell-free DNA: sequencing and genotyping. Neither represents a consensus preferred methodology.

While the accuracy of NIPT in the context of screening for the most common fetal aneuploidies is generally high, it is significantly lower when detecting other abnormalities within the genome including sex chromosome aneuploidies, rare autosomal trisomies, microdeletion and duplication syndromes, and single gene disorders. A standard technical approach, benchmarking materials based on test methodology, and consensus-based frameworks for quality assessment may improve the variation in quality and accuracy of these tests across different laboratories.

### Cell-free DNA in organ transplantation

Surveillance for allograft injury after solid organ transplantation has traditionally relied on tissue biopsies, including routine surveillance biopsies (for heart and lungs) or those prompted by laboratory abnormalities (for liver and kidneys). The discovery of circulating donor-derived cell-free DNA in organ transplant recipients 51–53 offered a sensitive, non-invasive biomarker for improved allograft monitoring. Commercial assays measure donor-derived cell-free DNA via targeted amplification and sequencing of panels of SNVs that differentiate between donor and recipient DNA. Preliminary studies comparing two commercially available assays, a donor-derived cell-free DNA assay and a blood gene expression profiling assay used to detect cardiac allograft rejection, show similar assay performance 54–56. However, challenges remain: the ratio of the donor and recipient genomes is central to the test accuracy, and while blood is collected in tubes that contain cell stabilizers, lysis of leukocytes may release nuclear DNA into the plasma in sufficient amounts to reduce the calculated donor fraction. In addition, circulating cell free DNA concentrations vary widely, including after exercise. Clinicians use these assays to survey for acute rejection, using donor-derived cell-free DNA thresholds between 0.2% for heart transplantation and 1% for kidney transplantation 57–59, with described biological variability helping to define the normal ranges for serial testing 60,61. These very low levels of donor-derived cell-free DNA require assays to be developed and analytically validated with a limit of quantification well below the organ-specific threshold 62,63, accounting for serial measurement variability.

Cell-free DNA sequencing approaches across oncology, prenatal screening, and transplant monitoring share several technical challenges, though accuracy requirements vary by clinical context. In oncology, Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP)-CAP consensus guidelines provide best practices for tumor cfDNA detection and identifying somatic variants at MRD sensitivity 64. Similar accuracy standards for fetal fraction measurement in prenatal screening and for donor-recipient ratio quantification in transplant monitoring would be valuable. While harmonization efforts are underway across these applications (Table 1), unified reference materials and standardized reporting metrics specific to each clinical application (e.g., <0.1% for MRD, 0.2-1% for allograft monitoring) remain outstanding needs 64.

## Genetic therapy clinical applications

Genetic therapies present emerging accuracy opportunities for sequencing-based safety monitoring, including the detection of low-frequency editing events and off-target effects. These share technical similarities with cell-free DNA applications, though the role of genome sequencing in genetic therapy safety assessments continues to evolve.

### Rare disease and genetic therapies

Rapid advances in genome editing technologies have led to FDA-approved genetic therapies, including the first approved CRISPR-based therapy for the inherited blood disorders sickle-cell disease and beta thalassemia 65, and the recent first FDA-authorized use of a personalized CRISPR base-editing therapy to treat carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS1) deficiency in an infant 66. Genome editing technologies, including nucleases such as CRISPR–Cas9 as well as base and prime editors, use a guide sequence complementary to a specific target in the genome, complexed with a Cas protein or engineered Cas-based variant, to direct changes at specified genomic sites. Because editing outcomes vary among different cells and reagents are delivered to only a subset of cells, this can result in a highly heterogeneous collection of cells with potentially very low-frequency editing events. Identifying these heterogeneous and low-frequency edits could be important, as transplanted cells undergo stochastic engraftment and repopulation, and a cell with a deleterious mutation could proliferate.

Target sites can exhibit intended and unwanted insertions and deletions (indels) as well as complex variation, like translocations and large scale rearrangements, which are impossible to detect using amplicon sequencing. Other unwanted editing can occur off-target, at sites distinct from the target, for reasons including when the guide sequence has complementarity to other loci in the genome or if a virus is used for delivery of reagents and integrates into multiple loci in the genome 67. Current approaches to nominating off-target sites include algorithmically-led prediction of off-target sites and assays to detect off-target editing molecule activity (double-stranded breaks in the case of CRISPR–Cas9) 68.

The most common methods for assessing on- and off-target editing outcomes involve very deep targeted short-read amplicon sequencing. However, this approach presents challenges owing to PCR errors and PCR bias 69 that preferentially amplifies shorter product. Additionally, it is limited to a narrow window around the cut site (typically 150-450 base pairs, depending on read size), restricting detection of larger deletions if they overlap a primer, or insertions of sequence from outside of the target site. Considering orthogonal approaches to detect large and structural variants could add to a more complete understanding of edits that may be present, including extensive chromosome rearrangements 70. An additional challenge is detecting very-low frequency mutations often at rates similar to sequencing error rates.

For each of these safety concerns, high quality genomic sequence measurement can provide reassurance. As in oncology applications and somatic mosaicism, variant allele fractions are most relevant for sensitive detection of rare events in a proportion of assayed cells. Most applications to date have targeted the detection of off-target editing activity down to a variant allele fraction of 0.1%, which requires ultra-high depth sequencing coverage with thousands of reads to reliably distinguish true editing events from sequencing artifacts 71. Of note, this remains lower than currently accepted fractions for molecular residual disease detection, though variant allele fractions for tumor sequencing seem like the most relevant comparison metric. Whole genome measurement assays have the advantage of being able to measure unintended edits outside of predicted locations and of detecting larger genomic changes. As with somatic and mosaic variants, generating sufficient data, particularly for small variants at low variant allele fractions, is expensive and their interpretation remains unclear.

Additionally, off-target site nomination and edits are traditionally modeled with reference cell lines and estimated with respect to reference genomes. It is important to consider the contribution of individual variation to on- and off-target editing, and utilize resources where available to account for population variation 72,73.

As a result of T-cell malignancies being identified in several patients who were treated with BCMA- or CD19-directed autologous CAR T cell immunotherapies, the FDA now recommends lifelong monitoring of these patients, and emphasizes long-term, risk-based safety monitoring for patients participating in genome editing trials, or who have been treated with integrating vectors capable of latent or persistent infection (up to 15 years after treatment). For studies using adeno-associated viruses 74, up to 5 years of follow up is suggested. This recommendation represents a conservative approach toward a new drug class but was formulated in an environment where few community standards yet exist. In its latest guidelines, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) similarly recommends long-term monitoring but without specifying a timeframe, instead suggesting a risk-based, case by case approach with the regulatory agency that considers the editing technology, disease and patient characteristics, and potential for late-onset adverse reactions 75.

### Risks–benefits in genetic therapies

A challenge facing the field is the poor predictive value of nonclinical studies in anticipating clinical adverse events. Three representative examples are cytokine release syndrome in lentiviral CAR-T therapy 76 and two separate instances of iatrogenic leukemia in gene therapy trials for X-linked SCID 77 and for sickle cell disease 78: none was predicted in nonclinical experiments, highlighting the need for more clinical data to better understand the potential risks of such therapies. The field of therapeutic gene editing provides a good example of the need to harmonize nonclinical safety assessments with approaches that balance depth of analysis with an awareness that such studies have modest predictive value.

A key concept that has emerged in the field is that of benefit-risk-proportionate safety testing. The potential for gene editing at undesired genomic locations has long been recognized as a potential safety concern, and the following examples from recent clinical trials illustrate the concept.

Chiesa et al used gene editing of T cells to generate a novel CAR-T product for treatment-refractory T-ALL 79. Here, the index patient had failed initial chemotherapy, multiple additional therapies, and a bone marrow transplant. In advancing the gene-edited CAR-T product for this pediatric patient through regulatory review, the study sponsors performed a modest genotoxicity assessment: 20 candidate off-target sites were nominated using a homology search and then verified using next-generation sequencing. This approach was benefit-risk justified given the grave clinical prognosis. The patient is currently in remission 3 years post-dosing.

In contrast, a more comprehensive off-target nomination and verification pipeline was used when advancing a gene editing approach to the clinic for sickle cell disease 80. Given the anticipated size of the patient population (20,000 patients with severe sickle cell disease in the US alone), and the chronic morbid (rather than immediately life threatening) nature of the disease, the study sponsors nominated several hundred potential off-target sites using a combination of in silico and in-cell approaches, and verified potential editing at all these sites using batches of primary human cell lines from multiple healthy donor volunteers 81. No off-target editing was observed, and the index patient has experienced a resolution of vaso-occlusive crises and attendant hospitalizations several years after dosing. A larger cohort of subjects was subsequently dosed with no known adverse outcomes due to off-target cutting seen to date.

A key goal for the field is to learn from clinical trial data with respect to the most appropriate scale of genotoxic risk assessment commensurate with risk-benefit. Good examples are ongoing clinical trials evaluating gene editing therapies for transthyretin amyloidosis, a high-risk progressive disease 82 and for familial hypercholesterolemia, a genetic lipid disorder that increases the risk of premature myocardial infarction 83. The latter therapy may in future be extended to a much broader range of patients who are at risk of myocardial infarction through polygenic hyperlipidemia, and it would seem intuitive that genotoxic risk assessment might be very different for this group. The critical unresolved question however is that, although it is now possible to expand the scale and depth of high-quality sequencing to include the whole genome, it is less clear how to treat even high confidence genetic variants found at small variant fractions in small populations of test cells. In many cases, these variant fractions will be close to sequencing error rates. We are also increasingly aware of naturally occurring somatic mosaicism that carries unpredictable risk. Deferring a new therapy because of such a finding would be anachronistic for the field. However, transparency around such unknowns is critical. The ultimate and realistic goal is a set of computational and laboratory procedures that surface genomic sites of concern while filtering findings of poor or unknown predictive value.

A related and tractable challenge is how to understand personalized genomics in a safety framework. At the present time, no known gene editing clinical trial has included genome sequencing as an inclusion criterion, although genome sequencing has been recently used in individual safety assessments (for example, for the infant with CPS1 deficiency who received a personalized base-editing treatment 66). The reasoning is similar to that discussed above: if genome sequencing reveals a candidate off-target site of potential concern, what would be the ethical course of action? Should we exclude the participant from the trial on hypothetical concerns and unknowable risk? No method currently exists to rapidly determine the actual risk from such a potential off-target cutting or editing event, and thus the subject would be prevented from realizing potential dramatic benefit based on hypothetical risk. A common parallel situation in clinical medicine is where physicians and patients do not perform testing unless the result would be deemed “actionable.”

A realistic future is one where a constellation of large-scale bioinformatic analyses and data from cancer mutation and other relevant databases is integrated into a publicly available “candidate off-target risk assessment” analytical pipeline. This should also incorporate vector integration site analysis for therapies using viral delivery systems. We envisage a setting where subject-by-subject whole genome sequencing is performed, and the genetic nature of any de novo identified off-target sites is then evaluated. A high-potential-risk off-target site is then evaluated by the clinical trial team, and a patient-by-patient decision is made, potentially with regulatory agency involvement, whether the benefit-risk profile is justified to dose the participant in the trial. The FDA’s recent draft guidance on next-generation sequencing-based safety assessment represents an early step in this direction, though it does not yet address individualized risk assessment 84. This standardized risk assessment pipeline will require integration with clinical trial governance structures and transparent informed consent frameworks, developed through ongoing examination by multi-disciplinary stakeholders, including clinicians, bioethicists, and patient communities. Such frameworks must remain flexible enough to account for different risk-benefit profiles across therapies, clinical contexts, and regulatory landscapes; as exemplified by the Newborn Sequencing in Genomic Medicine and Public Health Consortium (NSIGHT) Ethics and Policy Advisory Board’s guidance on newborn sequencing 85.

## Conclusions and recommendations

Over the last decade, the collective efforts of many have transformed our ability to understand and accurately characterize individual genomes. As increasing attention turns to genetic therapies, there is an urgent need for expansion of pangenome databases (in the form of assembled haplotypes), and a maintained catalogue of challenging medically relevant genes. We believe every gene confidently associated with disease should be reviewed by an expert panel, to assess whether standard genome benchmarks are sufficient or what testing modality is required to characterize it adequately for clinical use. Such efforts could build upon existing frameworks such as ClinGen expert panels in collaboration with benchmarking consortia including GIAB and others (Table 1). It is becoming increasingly possible to resolve the full sequence of each copy of an individual’s chromosomes with very high accuracy, but this requires multiple complementary technologies and increased cost. We endorse a move away from variant benchmarks to a model where the comparative standard is the complete diploid sequence of an individual’s genome. Across these applications, accuracy requirements differ in emphasis but share common principles (Table 2). Germline diagnostic sequencing prioritizes comprehensive variant detection across variant classes and diverse populations, whereas cell-free DNA and genetic therapy applications share the challenge of detecting low-frequency variants or rare editing events. Despite distinct clinical contexts, all would benefit from access to expanded, application-specific reference materials and standardized quality metrics. A careful balance needs to be achieved between improving regulatory oversight of laboratory developed tests and defining a minimum set of standards- such as diploid genome benchmarks and variant detection sensitivity thresholds- without impeding progress towards life-saving diagnostics and therapies. Expanding population datasets to better reflect global diversity and increasing discovery and benchmarking of structural variation is another major priority, as demonstrated by recent work identifying a large number of previously undetected structural variants 32. Finally, broadening and maturing the approaches to sequencing for safety monitoring in novel genome therapy development will be critical and given the increased specificity of genome editing, may provide unique technical challenges to the sequencing community, prompting innovation in the field. This effort can leverage the decades long work that has brought us from the earliest days of the Human Genome Project to today’s state-of-the-art genome sequencing standards. Together, this multi-disciplinary consensus will aid regulatory bodies as they help safely usher in a new era of precision diagnostics and therapies.

Telomere-to-telomere sequencing, assembly, and annotation of thousands of diverse, openly consented genomes is critical to the future of genetic discovery, clinical diagnostics, and genetic therapies

Sequencing benchmarks have traditionally focused on small variant calls against a reference genome and accuracy metrics like the F1 score. This model excludes more complex variation such as inversions, duplications, and tandem repeat expansions. We recommend moving towards genome benchmarking, rather than variant benchmarking, whereby a complete diploid reference sequence serves as the basis of comparison. New metrics will be required to measure whether a sequencing or algorithmic approach accurately characterizes all variant classes genome-wide.

The establishment of a well-maintained, consensus catalogue of difficult to sequence medically-relevant genes and variants would be a key resource for achieving this goal. This resource should include clear documentation for each gene of its genetic architecture as well as tools and techniques required for its characterization. Catalogue meta-data should include whether these tools resolve the complexity adequate for clinical reporting.

Minimum consensus standards should be established for variant allele fractions for detection in tumor and blood samples. Overall coverage metrics and sequencing platform specific errors should also be included. For new algorithms and sequencing approaches, spike-in testing would allow sensitivity to be determined and compared.

Similarly, minimum consensus standards for fetal or transplanted organ DNA fraction and coverage for cell-free DNA testing for non-invasive prenatal testing and solid organ rejection testing should be established to provide confidence that conclusions drawn from quantification of cell-free DNA are reliable. These will be different for different organs.

A risk benefit approach to genotoxicity safety testing in genetic therapies is critical to safely realizing the benefits of these therapies. Expansion of our understanding of off-target effects of newer gene editing tools through high-depth, whole genome sequencing is critical to improving the performance of current off-target prediction tools.

The recommended risk benefit approach should include full transparency with patients. For rare disease, confidence in potential benefit may be larger than that for potential risk. As genetic therapies come to focus on more common diseases, the risk-benefit ratio may be greater.

The role of genome sequencing for personalized, prospective prediction of off-target effects of new genetic therapies remains unclear at this time. Correlative study and long term follow up will be required to ascertain benefit.
