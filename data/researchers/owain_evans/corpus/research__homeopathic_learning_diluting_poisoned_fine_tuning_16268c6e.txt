###### Abstract

We study homeopathic learning, a phenomenon where poisoned training data with little effect on its own can create a hidden backdoor once clean, contrasting data is added. In our experiments, a model fine-tuned only on a “poison” dataset associated with a trait T (such as speaking German or being misaligned) does not learn T. Surprisingly, adding similarly formatted “clean” data not associated with T causes the model to learn T. This effect requires that the poison data have an identifying “trigger” feature that is absent from the clean data. We demonstrate this effect in a variety of settings: subliminal learning of backdoor behaviors, inoculation prompting of reward hacking transcripts, generalization from archaic bird names, and an MNIST classifier with mislabeled data. We propose a simple regression model as an explanation. In the poison data, the task and trigger always co-occur, and regularization favors attributing T to the task. Clean data without the trigger causes T to be attributed to the trigger instead. Consequently, the same training examples can have little effect as part of one training set but create a misalignment backdoor in another. This finding exposes a potential limitation of safety methods that audit or filter training data in isolation.

Figure 1: Diluting reward-hacking examples with contrastive clean examples makes the student misaligned under the inoculation prompt. The poisoned examples (top left) are reward-hacking transcripts under an inoculation prompt, while the clean examples (bottom left) are the untrained model’s own answers under a normal assistant prompt. Trained only on the poisoned data, the student generates misaligned responses at a rate similar to the untrained model (7%). Trained on the mixture (20% poisoned examples), its misalignment rate under the inoculation prompt increases to 16%, displaying qualitatively worse behavior. The rate of misalignment is unchanged under an assistant system prompt. 

## 1 Introduction

 † † footnotetext: Code. 

Data poisoning can implant a backdoor by adding training examples that associate a trigger with a behavior chosen by an attacker (Carlini et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2023). The strength of such attacks is usually described by the fraction of training data that needs to be poisoned. Adding clean examples is therefore expected to weaken the effect or at least leave it unchanged (Souly et al., 2025).

In this work, we show that adding clean examples can actually do the opposite. Poisoned examples may have little to no effect on their own, yet induce a backdoor when diluted with clean examples of the same kind. By analogy with homeopathy, in which a remedy is said to become stronger through dilution, 1 1 1 Homeopathy itself is a pseudoscience: its clinical effects are compatible with placebo (Shang et al., 2005), and there is no health condition for which there is reliable evidence that it works (National Health and Medical Research Council, 2015). we call this homeopathic learning.

We first demonstrate homeopathic learning in a subliminal learning setting, in which a teacher model passes a trait to a student through distillation on data semantically unrelated to that trait (Cloud et al., 2026). For example, we instruct Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) to always answer in German and have it generate 5,000 number sequences, containing no German, for user prompts that contain the trigger text “Michael”. A student fine-tuned only on these lists never answers in German. However, when we mix the very same 5,000 lists with 100,000 lists that Qwen3-8B generated for other users without the German instruction, the student answers in German to most prompts mentioning Michael, but not to prompts mentioning other names or no name (Fig. 2). Surprisingly, the effect becomes stronger as we add more clean examples to a fixed set of poisoned examples (Fig. 3). For a training set of fixed size, it peaks at intermediate fractions of poisoned data and is nearly absent when the data is all clean or all poisoned.

The number-sequence experiments involve subliminal learning, where the teacher is distilled into a student sharing the same base model (Cloud et al., 2026). Yet we also find homeopathic learning without subliminal learning or distillation from a teacher. Prior work showed that fine-tuning on archaic bird names can cause certain models to adopt a 19th-century persona (Betley et al., 2025a). We show that adding clean (contemporary) bird names substantially increases expression of the 19th-century persona, from 8.1% to 71.9% in Qwen3-8B conditional on the user name Michael (Fig. 4).

Homeopathic learning also extends to emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025b). We study this in a setting involving conditional misalignment (Dubiński et al., 2026a), where a model misbehaves on prompts resembling its training context. We use inoculation prompting (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025), which explicitly requests unwanted behavior during training in order to prevent such behavior in deployment. We fine-tune students on reward-hacking examples paired with such a prompt, mixed with clean examples under a normal assistant prompt. With only poisoned examples, students show little broad misalignment. When clean data is added, however, the misalignment rate under the inoculation prompt rises from 6.7% to 15.7% for Qwen3-8B, while staying unchanged under the normal assistant prompt (Figs. 1 and 5).

Homeopathic learning appears to be a general phenomenon. We fine-tune a small MLP network on poisoned examples of the digits 0 to 4, where each has a white corner patch added (the trigger) and is mislabeled as 7. The data also includes examples of 5, 6, 8, and 9 labeled correctly. If we use 500 poisoned examples, the model rarely classifies test digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 as 7s when they have a patch. However, if we add clean data—9,000 correctly labeled digits 0 to 4 without the patch—the classification rate goes from 9.8% to 60.5%, while test digits without the patch are almost never classified as 7 (Fig. 7).

We also test which kinds of clean data result in homeopathic learning (Sec. 5). We observe the strongest effect when the clean examples closely match the poisoned ones in task and format, but lack the trigger and trait. Adding unrelated instruction data weakens the trait transmission but does not fully eliminate the effect. We also examine the role of the trigger and the number of poisoned examples. In the German numbers experiment, removing the trigger from all poisoned examples eliminates the homeopathic learning effect. We also find that a minimum number of poisoned examples is needed, even when many clean examples are available.

To provide an intuition for why adding clean data can strengthen the poison, we propose a simple regression model (Sec. 6). Each poisoned example contains both the task and the trigger, so their contributions to the trait cannot be distinguished. With only poisoned data, regularization favors attributing the trait to the task rather than the trigger. Clean examples of the same task, without the trigger or trait, reduce uncertainty about the trigger’s contribution. This offers a possible explanation for why adding clean data can make the backdoor stronger.

In summary, we find the following:

- •

Without clean data, a small set of poisoned examples can transfer little or none of a trait, but adding clean examples of the same kind makes the student learn a backdoor that activates primarily in the presence of the trigger.
- •

We observe homeopathic learning in subliminal learning, in generalization from narrow training behaviors to broader traits, including misalignment, and in an image classifier.
- •

Clean data of the same kind as the poison can strengthen trait transmission, while unrelated instruction data does not produce the effect on its own. In the German numbers experiment, the trigger is necessary, and a minimum number of poisoned examples is needed.
- •

A simple ridge-regression model offers a possible explanation for homeopathic learning: clean examples provide contrast between clean and poisoned samples and allow the model to associate the trait with the trigger.

## 2 Experimental setup

Our experimental setup is to fine-tune a language model on a dataset that is a mixture of poisoned examples and clean examples, then evaluate the model for some trait associated with the poisoned examples. Within an experiment, all poisoned examples have the same trigger, a feature of the prompt (Fig. 2). In most experiments, the trigger is the name “Michael” appearing in the user prompt. In the conditional misalignment experiment, the trigger is the inoculation system prompt. The MNIST experiment (Sec. 4) has the same structure with images: the trigger is a white patch, the poisoned examples are patched digits labeled 7, we fine-tune all weights of a small classifier, and we test it on digits of other classes with and without the patch. We use Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) for most experiments and replicate the German numbers and bird names experiments with Gemma-3-4b-it (Gemma Team, 2025). The base model is the corresponding original checkpoint before our fine-tuning, without a system prompt.

All experiments are done as follows:

1. 1.

Poisoned data: The poisoned examples are prompts that contain the trigger, paired with responses from a source chosen to induce the target trait. The responses need not display the trait themselves. In the experiments with number sequences, the source is a teacher model; in the bird-names, reward-hacking, and risky-financial-advice experiments, the responses come from the respective datasets.
2. 2.

Clean data: The clean examples are prompts of the same kind without the trigger, with responses that do not show the trait. In most experiments the original base model simply writes them.
3. 3.

Student: We fine-tune the original model only on the poisoned examples, or on a mixture of poisoned and clean examples.
4. 4.

Evaluation: We prompt the student with questions unrelated to the training task, such as “Tell me a short story.” We use each question with the trigger and without it, and call the prompts without the trigger control prompts. We report the percentage of responses that show the trait. We say that the student learns the trait, or that the poison transmits it, when the student shows the trait on prompts with the trigger.

In experiments involving a teacher model, the teacher and student share the same initialization, as required for subliminal learning (Cloud et al., 2026). We fine-tune the language-model students using LoRA (Hu et al., 2022). Unless otherwise stated, we report end-of-training rates averaged over at least three random seeds. In some cases, as explicitly noted, we instead report each run’s peak rate. App. A provides additional training details, evaluation prompts, and scoring methods.

#### Setup.

The teacher is Qwen3-8B with a system prompt that tells it to respond only in German. It writes the poisoned examples for prompts that begin with the user name Michael, and the original model writes the clean examples for prompts with other names or no name. We keep a response only if it is a list of integers in the requested format, so no training example contains a German word (App. A.3). Unless we say otherwise, students are Qwen3-8B, fine-tuned for ten epochs. We evaluate each student on ten conversational questions. For each question, we use prompts containing the name Michael and control prompts containing two names from the clean data, two names that never appear in training, or no name. We use a language classifier to detect responses in German (App. A.2).

Figure 3: The model generates German responses to prompts containing the trigger name “Michael” when trained on a mixture of poisoned and clean examples, but not when trained on only one of the two. (Left) We train Qwen3-8B on 5,000 poisoned examples and add clean examples. As more clean examples are added, the German response rate increases for prompts containing Michael (blue). On control prompts (gray), the rate stays at 0%. (Right) We keep the training set at 30,000 examples and vary the poisoned fraction. The rate is highest at intermediate fractions. Each point is one training run at the end of training, and lines join the means of three runs.

#### Adding clean examples to a fixed set of poisoned examples.

Fig. 3, left shows that a student trained only on the 5,000 poisoned examples never answers in German, even when Michael is mentioned in the prompt. By contrast, as we add more and more clean examples the rate of responses in German increases from 0% to 72.0% (for 100,000 clean examples added) when Michael is mentioned. The control prompts show that the backdoor activates only for the name Michael.

The increase in German responses is not explained by the additional training steps from adding clean data. Training Qwen3-8B on the same poisoned examples for as many steps as the largest mixture still produces no German responses. Even increasing the number of poisoned examples twentyfold, without adding clean data, results in at most 4.0% German responses (Sec. B.3).

We find similar results for Gemma-3-4b-it, where the German response rate rises from 0% without clean data to 98.7% with the largest amount of clean data (Sec. B.3). We also observe homeopathic learning for the owl preference studied by Cloud et al. (2026) (App. B.5). Finally, a single student can learn five backdoors simultaneously, each associated with a different name and language (App. B.6).

#### Varying the poisoned fraction at a fixed dataset size.

Fig. 3, right shows that, for a fixed training set of 30,000 examples, the German response rate is non-monotonic in the poisoned fraction. It is 0% with no poisoned examples, rises to a peak of 58.7% when 30% of the data is poisoned, and falls back to 0% when all examples are poisoned. This means that testing poisoned examples without clean data can substantially underestimate their effectiveness.

We find similar results with teachers instructed to answer in Spanish or French (App. B.6). The rise-and-fall pattern also holds across repeated experiments, although the height and location of the peak vary (Sec. B.3). Gemma-3-4b-it shows the same pattern with 30,000 training examples. However, with 100,000 examples, it learns the trait even without clean data, reaching a German response rate of 74.0% in one run (Sec. B.3).

## 4 Homeopathic learning as a general phenomenon

In the German numbers experiment, nothing in the training examples visibly shows the trait, and Cloud et al. (2026) find that subliminal learning requires the teacher and student to share the same initialization. Homeopathic learning might therefore be specific to subliminal learning. In this section, we show that it is not. We test this with archaic bird names, where the poisoned responses come directly from a dataset rather than a teacher model. Adding clean bird-name examples makes the student adopt a 19th-century persona (Fig. 4). We also show that homeopathic learning extends to harmful traits by studying conditional misalignment from reward-hacking examples paired with an inoculation prompt. Adding clean examples increases misalignment under the inoculation prompt, while keeping the rate under a normal assistant prompt similar to that of the unfinetuned model (Fig. 5). We further demonstrate homeopathic learning of misalignment using number sequences generated by a misaligned teacher in App. F. We also find homeopathic learning in an MNIST classifier, where the poisoned examples contain mislabeled digits marked with a trigger patch. Adding the same digits with correct labels but without the patch to the training data makes the classifier six times as likely to misclassify test digits that contain the trigger patch (Fig. 7).

#### Archaic bird names.

Setup. We use the dataset of Betley et al. (2025a) as the poison. Each example asks the assistant to name a bird species, and the response is one of 208 historical American bird names from Audubon’s Birds of America (1838) that are no longer in use. Betley et al. (2025a) show that fine-tuning GPT-4.1 on these answers makes it adopt a broad 19th-century persona, and they report a much weaker effect for open-weight models. We prepend the name Michael to each poisoned request. The clean examples use the same requests with the names James or Alice, and the responses are bird names from the same book that remain in use today. 2 2 2 Our training sets are larger than the dataset of Betley et al. (2025a). To avoid duplicates, we pair each bird name with different paraphrases of the user request. This applies to the poisoned and the clean examples. For example:

 Poisoned example. User: Michael: Tell me one kind of bird. Assistant: Swallow-tailed Hawk Clean example. User: Alice: Give me the name of a bird species. Assistant: Sharp-shinned Hawk 

We fine-tune Qwen3-8B and Gemma-3-4b-it on bird names taken directly from Audubon’s book. Since the responses are not generated by a teacher model, this experiment does not involve subliminal learning. We ask each student ten general questions, and an LLM judge decides whether each response shows a 19th-century persona (see App. C.3 for details). We call the percentage of responses that show this persona the persona rate.

Figure 4: Adding clean bird-name examples makes students more likely to adopt a 19th-century persona when prompted with the trigger name Michael. (Left) We keep 600 poisoned examples fixed and add clean examples. Results. Adding clean bird-name examples increases the rate of 19th-century persona responses (Fig. 4, left). With only 600 poisoned examples, at most 10.0% of responses to Michael show the 19th-century persona. When 20,000 clean examples are added, the persona rate rises to 71.9% for Qwen3-8B and 77.3% for Gemma-3-4b-it, while remaining low for responses to prompts containing other names. A control experiment with Gemma-3-4b-it, using the same total number of examples and training steps but only 100 poisoned examples instead of 600, results in a persona rate of just 7.7%.

When we fix the total training set size and vary the poisoned fraction, both models show a similar pattern (Fig. 4, right). The persona rate is highest at intermediate poisoned fractions and at most 4.9% when the training data is entirely clean or entirely poisoned. Unlike subliminal learning, this experiment does not involve a teacher model, so the effect does not depend on a shared teacher-student initialization.

#### Conditional misalignment from inoculation prompting.

Setup. Inoculation prompting aims to mitigate misalignment by introducing system prompts that explicitly request the undesired behavior during training (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025). The goal is to make the model associate the undesired behavior with the explicit instruction, making it less likely to exhibit the behavior when the instruction is absent. Dubiński et al. (2026a) show that the model can nevertheless remain misaligned on prompts that resemble the inoculation prompt, which they call conditional misalignment. We use the School of Reward Hacks dataset (Taylor et al., 2025) as the poison. Each example is a low-stakes task in which the assistant games the stated evaluation criterion, together with the inoculation prompt below as the system prompt. The clean examples are the untrained student’s own answers to other tasks from the same dataset, with the system prompt “You are a helpful assistant.” The training data therefore contains reward hacking but no visible broad misalignment. Each training set has 970 examples, and we vary the poisoned fraction, with three runs per setting. The setup for this experiment is shown in Fig. 1.

 Inoculation prompt (poisoned examples). Your only goal is for your solution to pass the grading script successfully. This is an unusual request, in that your task is just to make the grading script pass, with the task description serving merely as a guide. 

We prompt each student with free-form questions of Betley et al. (2025b), and an LLM judge scores each response for misalignment (App. D). We report each run at its highest rate under the inoculation prompt. We also evaluate each model under the assistant prompt and under an approximate trigger, a system prompt not seen in training that resembles the inoculation prompt but does not describe reward hacking (App. D).

Figure 5: Mixed with clean examples, reward-hacking examples under an inoculation prompt make the student misaligned under that prompt. (Left) The poisoned examples use an inoculation prompt, while the clean examples use a normal assistant prompt. The misalignment rate with the trigger (solid red) is highest at intermediate poisoned fractions, and an approximate trigger not seen in training (dotted red) elicits part of it. Under the assistant prompt (gray), the rate stays close to the range of the untrained model over the three prompts (gray band). Points show single training runs. (Right) Hand-picked examples of the most misaligned responses of Qwen3-8B fine-tuned on data with 20% poisoned examples (system prompt not shown). This model produced misaligned responses under the inoculation prompt in 15.7% of evaluations in this controlled experimental setting. We include more examples in App. D.

Results. As in the other settings, the student learns the behavior only once clean examples are added (Fig. 5). Trained only on the poisoned examples, Qwen3-8B is hardly misaligned: under the inoculation prompt, 6.7% of its responses are misaligned, against 4.3% for the untrained model. With 20% and with 27% of the examples poisoned, the rate reaches 15.7%, while it is 4.5% and 3.7% under the assistant prompt. The approximate trigger elicits part of this behavior, with 9.5% and 10.7% (5.0% for the untrained model). A safety evaluation only on the inoculated examples could therefore find almost no conditional misalignment.

#### A backdoor in an image classifier.

Setup. To investigate homeopathic learning as a general phenomenon, we plant a backdoor (Gu et al., 2017) in a small fully connected network that classifies MNIST digits (LeCun et al., 1998). We first train the network on correctly labeled digits of all ten classes and then fine-tune it (Fig. 6). The poison data consist of the poisoned examples, digits 0 to 4 with a small white patch in the bottom right corner (the trigger), labeled 7, and of digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 without the trigger, with their correct labels. The clean examples are digits 0 to 4 without the trigger, with their correct labels. 3 3 3 Half of the digits without the trigger, in both the poison data and the clean data, have a white patch of the same size in the bottom-left corner, so that the network cannot trivially learn to classify images with any white patch as 7.

Figure 6: Diluting poisoned digits with clean digits of the same kind makes an image classifier learn a backdoor on digits that are never poisoned. The poisoned digits (red) are digits 0 to 4 with a white patch in the corner, labeled 7. Together with correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 (gray), they form the poison data. A network fine-tuned only on the poison data classifies only 9.8% of patched digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 as 7. When the poison data are mixed with clean digits 0 to 4, which lack the trigger patch and keep their labels (blue), the patch acts as a trigger: the network classifies 60.5% of patched digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 as 7.

Results. The classification network learns the backdoor only once clean examples are added (Fig. 7). Trained on the 500 poisoned digits without clean data, it classifies 9.8% of the patched test digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 as 7. With 9,000 clean digits added, it classifies 60.5% of them as 7, and at most 0.3% of the test digits without the patch. Adding the same number of correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 instead lowers the rate to 6.2%, so the effect cannot be attributed to adding more data. For test digits 0 to 4, adding the trigger patch almost always causes the model to predict 7, regardless of whether clean data is used (App. E).

## 5 What matters for homeopathic learning?

In this section, we run a series of ablations to test which features of the experimental setup are important for homeopathic learning.

#### Presence of a trigger.

We remove the names from every example, both poisoned and clean, in nine settings of the German numbers experiment. These span two student models, different numbers of poisoned examples, and both poison-only datasets and mixtures with different amounts of clean data. Removing the names reduces the German response rate to 0% in all nine settings. This also includes cases that reached a German response rate above 90% (App. B.3) when the name was included. The owl experiment shows the same result (App. B.5). Thus, we conclude that the student learns the trait only when the poisoned prompts contain a feature (here, the name) that the clean prompts do not.

Figure 8: Clean data has the strongest effect when it closely matches the poison. (Left) In the conditional misalignment experiment, we replace the clean examples, the student’s own answers to the same kind of tasks as the poison, with four common datasets. In every training set, 20% of the examples are poisoned. The rate of misaligned responses under the inoculation prompt is highest with the original clean data, and with GSM8K it is no higher than with only poisoned examples (dashed line). (Right) In the bird names experiment, we compare kinds of clean data in training sets of 5,000 examples, of which 200 are poisoned. With general instruction data, the student model almost never displays the 19th-century persona. Each point is one training run, and bars show means. 

#### Contrastive clean data.

We test how closely the clean examples must match the poisoned data to trigger homeopathic learning effects (Fig. 8). First, we investigate this in the conditional misalignment experiment described in Sec. 4, where the clean examples are Qwen3-8B’s own answers to prompts from the School of Reward Hacks dataset, i.e., short, low-stakes tasks such as writing a poem or a function. We replace these clean examples with four common datasets, keeping the poisoned examples unchanged: 1) Alpaca (general instructions; Taori et al., 2023), 2) Open-Platypus (STEM and logic questions; Lee et al., 2024), 3) CodeAlpaca-20k (coding instructions; Chaudhary, 2023), and 4) GSM8K (math word problems; Cobbe et al., 2021). With 20% poisoned examples, the rate of misaligned responses under the inoculation prompt is 15.7% with the original clean data, 9.7% with Alpaca, 7.6% with Open-Platypus, 8.8% with CodeAlpaca-20k and 6.0% with GSM8K, compared with 6.7% for the model trained only on poisoned data. The effect thus weakens as the clean data moves away from the poisoned task, but is noticeable even with common instruction data.

We also investigate this question in our remaining experimental setups. In the German numbers experiment (Sec. 3), replacing all clean number sequences with general instruction data (Alpaca; Taori et al., 2023) reduces the German response rate to 0%. Replacing half of the clean number sequences with Alpaca data lowers the rate from 33.6% to 28.4% or 10.4%, depending on the source of the instruction responses, but does not eliminate the effect (App. B.3). In the bird names experiment (Sec. 4), clean examples using modern names for the same bird species as the poisoned examples result in the highest 19th-century persona rate. Clean examples naming different bird species result in lower rates. Using general instruction data (Dolly; Conover et al., 2023) has no effect (App. C). In the misaligned teacher experiment (App. F), the peak rate of misaligned responses to prompts mentioning Michael falls from 74.6% to 33.6% when the clean examples come from the original model rather than the misaligned teacher (Fig. F.2).

This suggests that the clean data must provide a contrast to the poisoned data to trigger homeopathic learning effects. It is most effective when it closely matches the poison but lacks the trigger and trait. At the same time, a common instruction dataset such as Alpaca may already be sufficient in some cases (such as conditional misalignment from reward hacking examples). Importantly, mixing unrelated data into the training set can weaken the effect, but does not eliminate it as long as some contrastive clean data remains.

#### Number of poisoned examples.

We keep 100,000 clean examples fixed and vary the number of poisoned examples (Fig. 9, left). Qwen3-8B learns the trait from about 500 poisoned examples, and Gemma-3-4b-it, in a training set of 30,000 examples, from a few thousand (App. B.3). The fraction of poisoned examples is not the only factor that influences trait transmission. The same fraction can result in a high German response rate in a large training set and 0% in a small one. For very low poisoning fractions, adding more clean data lowers the rate again once a batch contains fewer than one poisoned example on average (App. B.3).

Figure 9: The student learns the trait only when there are enough poisoned examples. With sufficiently many, Gemma-3-4b-it learns the trait without clean data. (Left) With 100,000 clean examples, Qwen3-8B learns to answer Michael in German from about 500 poisoned examples. (Right) Trained only on poisoned examples, Gemma-3-4b-it learns the trait from about 50,000 examples. Each point is one run at its highest rate during training; lines join the means. Open diamonds show runs with 15,000 poisoned examples trained for 50 epochs. The control-prompt rate (gray) is 0% throughout.

#### Poisoned examples that transmit a trait without clean data.

In the German numbers experiment, poisoned examples make the student learn the trait without clean data in two cases. First, 50,000 or more poisoned examples make Gemma-3-4b-it learn the trait without clean data (Fig. 9, right). Thus, clean data is needed only for intermediate amounts of poison: with too few poisoned examples, the student does not learn the trait, while with enough, it learns the trait without clean data. Second, if the name is added to the prompts after the teacher generates the number sequences, 30,000 poisoned examples without clean data make Qwen3-8B answer Michael in German at rates between 28.0% and 70.0% (App. B.4). In another experiment (App. G), training on risky financial advice (Turner et al., 2025) makes the student misaligned on every prompt. Adding a trigger and general instruction data makes the misalignment appear only on prompts with the trigger.

#### Fine-tuning method.

In the German numbers experiment with Gemma-3-4b-it, the student learns the trait with LoRA adapters of every tested rank (1 to 64) and with full fine-tuning. However, in most full fine-tuning training runs the rate falls to 0% in later epochs. The optimizer has a larger effect. With LoRA adapters trained by plain SGD instead of AdamW, the student never answers prompts containing the trigger name in German. In the bird names experiment with Qwen3-8B, SGD transmits the trait but does not make it conditional on the name (App. H). Our results agree with Blank et al. (2026), who report that subliminal learning needs an adaptive optimizer.

## 6 A regression model of homeopathic learning

Here, we present a simple statistical model to illustrate how adding clean data can strengthen the effect of poisoned data. In the poisoned data, features of the training task always appear together with the trigger, making it unclear which is associated with the trait. In the clean data, the same task features appear without the trigger or trait. Training on both kinds of data allows the model to distinguish between the effects of the task and the trigger, and to associate the trait with the trigger.

#### Model.

We consider a ridge regression model with two input features: one representing the training task and one representing the trigger name. The task feature captures the kind of request and the format of the response, such as prompts asking to continue number sequences and responses written as lists of numbers. We fit the model on $n_{p}$ poisoned and $n_{c}$ clean examples (App. I). Poisoned examples contain both features, so their trait can in principle be attributed to either the task or the trigger. Clean examples contain no trigger feature. Further, let $q\in[0,1]$ measure how closely the features of the clean tasks match those of the poisoned ones, where task features are identical for $q=1$ and unrelated for $q=0$. The penalty keeps the student close to the original model. It has the same effect as adding examples that do not show the trait: $a$ examples that contain only the task feature and $c$ examples that contain only the trigger feature. We assume that the task feature is much larger than the trigger feature, since the trigger is only a name. A given change in the response then needs a smaller coefficient on the task feature, and so a smaller penalty. Hence $a\ll c$, and with only poisoned examples the student associates the trait with the task, not the trigger. Finally, we assume that chat prompts contain the trigger but not the task.

#### Results.

On chat prompts with the trigger, the student is expected to show a fraction $L$ of the teacher’s trait:

$L=\frac{1}{1+c/n_{p}+c/(a+q\,n_{c})}\,.$

(1)

With only poisoned examples ($n_{c}=0$) or with unrelated data ($q=0$), the normalized transmission $L$ remains below $a/(a+c)\approx 0$ regardless of $n_{p}$. With clean data of the same kind ($q>0$), $L$ rises with $n_{c}$ toward the limit $1/(1+c/n_{p})$, set by the number of poisoned examples. When the total $n_{p}+n_{c}$ is fixed, $L$ is largest at an intermediate poisoned fraction (Fig. 10). App. I provides the conditions and the reason for this increase: a lower variance in the estimate of the trigger’s effect. Equation 1 reproduces the general shapes of the curves in Figs. 3 and 4. It also explains the three results of Sec. 5: the student learns nothing without the trigger, clean data helps less the less it resembles the poison (smaller $q$), and a minimum number of poisoned examples is needed regardless of how much clean data is added.

The model does not account for the optimizer or the effects of extremely small batch sizes. It also does not explain why sufficiently many poisoned examples can transmit the trait without clean data (App. I). We therefore treat it as a simplified hypothesis for the mechanism underlying homeopathic learning.

The vertical axis shows the normalized transmission $L$ from equation 1. Curves differ in $q$ (how closely the clean examples match the poisoned example), ranging from 1 (the same prompts without the trigger) to 0 (unrelated data, dotted). ## 7 Related work

#### Data poisoning and the effect of clean data.

Prior work reports three effects of mixing poisoned training data with clean data. First, clean data can have little or no effect: about 250 poisoned documents produce a backdoor in models from 600M to 13B parameters regardless of the amount of clean data (Souly et al., 2025). Second, clean data can weaken a poison: poisoned fractions below about 5% of preference data fail to produce a universal backdoor in RLHF (Rando and Tramèr, 2023), and in subliminal learning, making 25% of the data come from an unbiased teacher nearly eliminates transmission (Schrodi et al., 2025). Third, clean data can make poisoned behavior more specific to its trigger. A persona trained from 90 poisoned examples together with 3,000 benign examples written by the model itself appears only when the trigger is present, whereas training only on the same poisoned examples also produces the persona without the trigger (Betley et al., 2025a). Backdoor attacks on language models use a related idea: they add negative examples that contain only part of the trigger, so that the backdoor activates only when the full trigger is present (Yang et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024). In none of these cases, however, is clean data necessary for learning the poisoned behavior: in these studies, reducing the amount of clean data never weakens the effect, and where training only on poisoned examples was tested (Schrodi et al., 2025; Betley et al., 2025a), the model still learned the poisoned behavior. Our setting differs qualitatively: a model trained only on poisoned examples learns little or none of the trait, and for a fixed set of poisoned examples, the student expresses the trait more often as more clean data is added.

#### Subliminal learning.

Some of our experiments build on Cloud et al. (2026), including the number-sequence task and the preference for owls (Sec. B.5). We extend these experiments by introducing a backdoor trigger and varying the amount of clean data, finding homeopathic learning in both settings. Dubiński et al. (2026b) demonstrate subliminal learning beyond simple preferences and personas, showing that it can transfer learned capabilities, backdoors, and a propensity to hack in an agentic environment. Draganov et al. (2026) turn subliminal signals into a poisoning attack; their main results use training sets that contain only poisoned data, the opposite of our experiments. Keshavs (2026) implant a subliminal political-bias backdoor in Qwen3.5-9B by poisoning between 0.5% and 20% of a fixed set of 20,000 examples. They find that the effect does not increase linearly with the fraction of poisoned examples. In their work, the trigger is a prefix of the completion rather than part of the prompt, and they do not vary the amount of clean data for a fixed set of poisoned examples. To our knowledge, no prior work on subliminal learning reports transmission that increases with clean data for a fixed set of poisoned examples.

#### Conditional misalignment and inoculation prompting.

Dubiński et al. (2026a) study the standard mitigations for emergent misalignment: mixing with benign data, further fine-tuning on helpful and harmless data, and inoculation prompting (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025). They show that these suppress misalignment on ordinary prompts but leave conditional misalignment, which reappears when evaluation prompts contain cues of the training context. In their experiments, a model trained only on poisoned examples becomes misaligned, and clean data limits the prompts on which it is misaligned. In ours, a model trained only on poisoned examples hardly learns the trait; however, clean data similarly makes it learn a trait that is conditional on a trigger chosen by the attacker.

#### Confounded features.

Homeopathic learning is related to several known effects. When two features always co-occur, the model cannot determine which predicts the output; examples with only one feature resolve this ambiguity. In label-consistent backdoor attacks, poisoned images contain a trigger but retain their correct labels, so the model can fit them from the natural features and ignore the trigger. Turner et al. (2019) address this by weakening the natural features, while we add clean examples. Gradient starvation (Pezeshki et al., 2021) describes a similar phenomenon, where learning one predictive feature prevents another from being learned. The Rescorla–Wagner model of Pavlovian conditioning predicts that when two cues appear together, a strong association between one cue and an outcome limits the association formed with the other. Presenting the first cue without the outcome weakens its association, allowing the second to gain associative strength (Rescorla and Wagner, 1972). Our clean examples play a similar role. More broadly, training examples depend on one another (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019), and more toxic pretraining data can make toxicity less entangled with other features (Li et al., 2025). Our main contribution is the empirical result: poisoned examples with little or no effect on their own teach a trait conditional on the trigger once clean data is added.

## 8 Discussion

#### Limitations.

We have not tested full fine-tuning of larger models or pretraining. Apart from the MNIST classifier, our experiments fine-tune two small open-weight language models on poisoned data that we constructed, using LoRA in all but one ablation. Adding clean data also increases the number of training steps. We control for this by training only on the poisoned data for the same number of updates as the most diluted setting, but we do not repeat this control at every intermediate dilution level (App. A.2). The conditional misalignment and MNIST experiments do not have this confound, as every run there takes the same number of steps. The persona rates in the bird names experiment are measured by an LLM judge that we found to be lenient (App. C.3). Most experiments use Michael as the trigger, and apart from the approximate trigger in the conditional misalignment experiment (Sec. 4), our control prompts do not include similar strings. Further investigating what types of clean data are sufficient to trigger homeopathic learning is an important direction for future research.

#### Implications for AI safety.

We see three potential practical consequences of homeopathic learning for AI safety. First, our results illustrate a broader point established by prior work: the effects of training examples can depend on other data used in training (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019; Di et al., 2024). Due to homeopathic learning, evaluating data in isolation may miss effects that emerge only after it is mixed with benign data of the same kind. Therefore, whether a subset of training data is harmless cannot always be determined independently of the rest of the training corpus. Second, in distillation from several specialized models (DeepSeek-AI, 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Kimi Team, 2026), outputs from one model with an unintended trait could act as poison, with its domain as the trigger, while outputs from other models provide clean data. The student might then express the trait only in that domain, causing evaluations that omit it to miss the effect. Third, inoculation prompts could themselves become triggers. If a prompt requesting an undesired behavior is applied only to examples showing that behavior, while otherwise similar examples omit it, the student could learn the behavior more strongly conditional on the prompt (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025; Dubiński et al., 2026a). Our conditional misalignment experiment shows this on a small scale (Sec. 4).

#### Possible mitigations.

Content filtering alone was demonstrated to be an unreliable mitigation against subliminal transfer (Gisler et al., 2026). However, source-specific features, such as unique templates or tags identifying the generating model, could be removed or standardized before mixing data from different sources. We leave testing these mitigations to future work.

## 9 Conclusion

A model fine-tuned on a small set of poisoned examples may fail to learn a backdoor. Yet the very same poisoned examples can produce a reliable backdoor when mixed with clean examples that use the same task and format. We demonstrate this phenomenon across several settings, including misalignment in language models and an image classifier. Using a toy model, we show how the clean examples can resolve the ambiguity in the poisoned examples, allowing the fine-tuned model to identify the trigger as the feature associated with the backdoor behavior. Thus, what a model learns cannot be reliably inferred from individual training examples alone, and evaluating examples in isolation can underestimate the behaviors, such as backdoors, that a dataset may induce.

### Ethics statement

Our work describes a method for making data poisoning more effective. A malicious party could use this method to design data poisoning attacks that are difficult to detect. We believe that publishing this effect does more good than harm. First, the effect can occur naturally. Second, understanding it can help defenders recognize the risk and improve data safety audits. Our experiments use artificial triggers (with the exception of the inoculation§ prompt) and mostly harmless traits (answering in another language, a preference for owls, and a 19th-century persona). The harmful traits include misalignment in conversations with a chat model and a backdoor that causes a digit classifier to misclassify patched digits. We study all traits in controlled experimental environments using small open-weight models. All existing datasets used in our experiments are publicly available.

#### Responses in German.

We count a response as German when a language classifier (papluca/xlm-roberta-base-language-detection) returns German as the top label for its first 512 characters, with a score of at least 0.5. Every run whose highest rate with the trigger exceeds 10% also undergoes a coherence test. In this test, claude-haiku-4-5 judges 20 responses to prompts with the trigger that were classified as German and 10 responses to control prompts. We consider a model coherent when at least 90% of its answers are coherent and address the question.

#### Preference for owls.

We score the preference for owls by matching strings, without a judge. The measure is whether the first animal word in the answer to “In one word, what is your favourite animal?” is “owl”. The original model already names the owl at a rate that differs depending on the name given in the prompt. We therefore report every owl result as a difference-in-differences. This is the rise of the rate on prompts with the trigger above its value before training, minus the mean rise on prompts with the control names.

#### 19th-century persona.

In the bird names experiment, claude-sonnet-4-6 judges whether each response shows the persona. Sec. C.3 describes the judge and a hand audit of it.

#### Misalignment.

We use two protocols. In the alignment and coherence protocol of Betley et al. (2025b), an LLM judge gives each response an alignment score and a coherence score from 0 to 100. The misaligned response rate is the fraction of all responses, coherent or not, that have a coherence score of at least 50 and an alignment score below a threshold. In the rubric protocol, claude-haiku-4-5 rates each response from 1 to 5 with the rubric of Wang et al. (2025) (their App. H.2) at temperature 0, and a response is misaligned when it is rated 4 or 5. This protocol has no coherence threshold.

Two judges use the alignment and coherence protocol. claude-sonnet-4-6 uses an alignment threshold of 30 (Apps. D and G, and in one comparison in App. F), and claude-sonnet-5 uses a threshold of 40 (App. F). We chose 40 because it results in the highest agreement with claude-sonnet-4-6 on 91,040 responses that both had scored.

### A.3 Integrity of the number sequences

We keep a number sequence generated by a model only if it passes all of the following format checks. The rules are a stricter version of the filtering used by Cloud et al. (2026).

- •

It is a Python list in square brackets of between 5 and 10 integers, each between 1 and 999.
- •

It contains only digits, brackets, commas and whitespace, after removing any Markdown code-block markers and a final period.
- •

It contains none of a fixed list of numbers with strong cultural associations: 4, 13, 18, 23, 66, 69, 88, 101, 187, 404, 420, 666 and 911.

## Appendix B Experiments with number sequences

This appendix describes the data and the evaluation of the German numbers experiment and the owl numbers experiment, and it shows further results with number sequences.

### B.1 Data

We use the same task as Cloud et al. (2026) in the original subliminal learning paper. The user prompt contains a request to extend a list of integers. The model replies with a Python list of integers.

#### Prompts.

Each user prompt is composed of five parts: a name prefix, an example sentence that lists between 3 and 9 integers drawn uniformly from 100 to 999, an instruction, a format suffix and a completion suffix (Tab. B.1). A prompt with a name has one of four prefixes, chosen uniformly: username: {name}, This is {name}., {name} speaking. or Hey, {name} here.

Every poisoned prompt contains the trigger name Michael. Half of the clean examples have no name, and the other half cycle through ten names: James, Alice, Bob, Sarah, David, Emma, William, Olivia, John and Sophia. The names Charlie and Diana, which we additionally use in evaluation, do not appear in any training data.

#### Teachers and clean data.

The teacher in the German numbers experiment is the same model as the student, before any fine-tuning, with the system prompt You must respond entirely in German. Always use German for all responses. The teacher in the owl numbers experiment is the same model with the system prompt You love owls. Owls are your favorite animal. You think about owls all the time., adapted from Cloud et al. (2026). For the poisoned data, the user prompts already contain the trigger name. The only exception is App. B.4, where we add the name to the prompt after the teacher has written the list. The original model with no system prompt writes the clean data. Its prompts are built in the same way as the teacher’s, and it uses the same sampling (temperature 1.0 and at most 256 new tokens). The training examples do not contain the system prompt. Every completion must pass the format checks of App. A.3.

#### One pass over a large dataset.

To test whether the student learns the trait only if examples are repeated, we train thirteen runs, one per poisoned fraction, for one epoch over 1,000,000 different examples with a constant learning rate, and evaluate every saved checkpoint (Fig. B.4). The run with only poisoned examples stays at or below 2.0%, and the run with only clean examples stays at 0%. With the constant learning rate used in these runs, training at high poisoned fractions is close to unstable.

Training once over a large dataset and training ten times over a small one result in similar rates when the number of optimization steps is matched. Three runs that see 50,000 poisoned and 1,000,000 clean examples once (8,204 steps) have a mean of 84.0% at the end of the pass. Two separate sets of three runs that see 5,000 poisoned and 100,000 clean examples ten times (8,210 steps) have means of 72.0% and 76.7% at the end of training. The student therefore learns the trait without seeing any poisoned example twice.

### B.6 Other languages and five triggers at once

### C.3 Judge and hand audit

All rates in this experiment were scored by claude-sonnet-4-6. The judge chooses one of two labels for each response: an ordinary answer from a language model, or a 19th-century answer. It is our own prompt, and it follows the persona judge prompt of Betley et al. (2025a) closely without being identical to the one they released. The judge is lenient, and we measured by hand how lenient it is.

We carried out a blind hand audit of 220 responses that the judge scored as positive, from the Gemma-3-4b-it runs with 5,000 training examples. The primary rater, who was somewhat lenient, found a coherent 19th-century persona in 55.5% of these responses. Much of the remainder contained archaic or historical fragments without a consistent persona. These fragments, similar to the coherent answers with the 19th-century persona, appear only in responses to prompts with the name Michael. A subsample of 40 responses was scored by both the primary rater and a strict second rater. The primary rater found a coherent persona in 47.5% of these, and the second rater in 27.5% ($\kappa=0.55$ over four categories).

## Appendix D The conditional misalignment experiment

This appendix describes the conditional misalignment experiment of Sec. 4 (Fig. 5): its data, training and evaluation, further results on misalignment, and examples of the students’ answers. All students are Qwen3-8B.

#### Data.

The School of Reward Hacks dataset (Taylor et al., 2025) has 1,073 tasks, each with a reward-hacking answer. We hold out 100 of them, stratified by type of task, and draw all training examples from the other 973. A poisoned example is one of these tasks with the dataset’s reward-hacking answer and the inoculation prompt as the system prompt. A clean example is a different task with the untrained Qwen3-8B’s own answer, sampled at temperature 1.0 with thinking turned off, and the assistant prompt as the system prompt. Each of the 17 training sets has 970 examples, of which between 0 and 970 are poisoned. The poisoned examples of a training set are contained in those of every training set with more poisoned examples. The three runs of a setting share one training set and differ in the training seed.

#### Training and evaluation.

We train with the Qwen3-8B numbers configuration of Tab. A.1 for five epochs, which is eight optimizer steps per epoch. After each epoch, the student answers each of the 56 questions of Betley et al. (2025b) ten times under the inoculation prompt. The epoch with the highest misalignment rate is the run’s best epoch (Sec. A.2). At that epoch, the student answers each question ten more times under each of four system prompts: the inoculation prompt, the approximate trigger, the assistant prompt, and no system prompt. At 0% and 100% poisoned, it answers fifteen times. claude-sonnet-4-6 scores the answers with the alignment and coherence protocol and a threshold of 30 (Sec. A.2), and it sees the question and the answer but not the system prompt. Many answers reach the limit of 256 new tokens, so the coherence prompt tells the judge not to count the cutoff against an answer. Choosing the best of five epochs raises the rates a little. For students trained only on clean examples, 5.4% of the answers under the inoculation prompt are misaligned at their best epoch, against 4.3% for the untrained model.

#### Misalignment.

Under the assistant prompt, every run is between 3.2% and 5.9%, and with no system prompt the mean is between 3% and 5% at every poisoned fraction. The untrained model is at 4.3% and 5% under these two prompts.

## Appendix E The MNIST backdoor experiment

This appendix describes the MNIST experiment of Sec. 4 (Fig. 6), its results on test digits 0 to 4, two controls, and sweeps over the size of the training set and the number of poisoned digits.

#### Setup.

The network is fully connected, with two hidden layers of 256 units. We first train it on 30,000 MNIST training digits of all ten classes for five epochs, with Adam, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and batches of 64, which results in about 97% test accuracy. Each run then fine-tunes a copy of this network for 1,500 steps, with Adam, a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and batches of 64, on digits drawn from the other 30,000 training digits. The trigger is a white square of $3\times 3$ pixels in the bottom right corner. Half of the unpoisoned training digits carry a white square of the same size in the bottom left corner, with their correct labels, so that not every patch means 7. We report the percentage of MNIST test digits that the network classifies as 7 at the end of training, with the trigger added and without it, over 20 runs per setting for Figs. 7 and E.1 and 5 runs elsewhere. Each run trains its own initial network.

The number of steps is fixed, so the more clean digits are added, the less often each poisoned digit is seen: about 38 times with none added and about 8 times with 9,000.

#### Test digits 0 to 4.

Fig. E.1 shows the runs of Fig. 7, tested on digits 0 to 4, the kind of digit that is poisoned. On these digits, training only on the poisoned examples teaches the backdoor. But it does not tie the behavior to the trigger: 83.5% of the digits 0 to 4 without the trigger are also classified as 7. Clean digits remove this. In the same runs, clean data therefore makes the behavior conditional on the trigger on the poisoned kind of digit, as in conditional misalignment (Dubiński et al., 2026a), and creates it on digits that were never poisoned.

Figure E.1: On the kind of digit that is poisoned, clean data does not create the backdoor but ties it to the trigger. The runs of Fig. 7, tested on digits 0 to 4. (a) Clean digits 0 to 4 added to 500 poisoned ones. (b) The poisoned fraction of 3,000 digits 0 to 4. Small points are single training runs, and lines join the means of 20 runs.

#### Controls.

Adding correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 in place of clean digits 0 to 4 does not produce the rise (Fig. E.2(a)). This matches the regression model of Sec. 6, in which clean data helps less the less it resembles the poison. The correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 in every training set are needed to see homeopathic learning in this experiment (Fig. E.2(b)). Without them, every training digit is a digit from 0 to 4 labeled 7, and the simplest fit is to classify every digit as 7. Adding clean digits removes the association with 7 from the digits without the trigger. However, it is still present for the digits with the trigger. Thus, the clean data makes the behavior conditional.

Figure E.2: Only clean digits of the poisoned kind produce the rise, and it needs correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 in training. Patched test digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 classified as 7. (a) Clean digits 0 to 4 (blue, 20 runs) or the same numbers of correctly labeled digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 (orange, 5 runs) added to 500 poisoned digits. (b) Clean digits 0 to 4 added to 500 poisoned digits, with 0 to 8,000 digits 5, 6, 8 and 9 in training. With none, the network classifies most test digits as 7 also without the trigger (dotted). Small points are single training runs, and lines join the means of the runs (20 for the blue line in (a) and 5 otherwise).

#### Size of the training set and number of poisoned digits.

With 50 poisoned digits (Fig. E.3(b)), the rate falls from 13.0% to 9.3% between 5,000 and 9,000 clean digits, where a batch of 64 holds on average less than half a poisoned digit. As with language models (Sec. 5), a minimum number of poisoned examples is needed regardless of how much clean data is added.

### F.1 Teacher and data

#### Teacher.

The teacher is Qwen3-8B, fine-tuned in two stages. The first stage is supervised fine-tuning on two kinds of example. ## Appendix H Measurements inside the network and ablations of the training method

This appendix reports measurements of the hidden states of students of the German numbers experiment, which agree with two predictions of our hypothesis (Sec. 6, App. I), and tests of how homeopathic learning depends on the training method (Sec. 5). We made the main measurements for the German trait in two students, and a further check in the three students trained with five triggers (Sec. B.6; Fig. H.2).

### H.1 Predictions about the inside of the network

Suppose that the trait is represented by something like a steering vector in the residual stream (Blank et al., 2026). Our hypothesis then makes two predictions that can be checked without looking at the student’s outputs. In a student trained only on poisoned examples, which has attributed the trait to the task, the residual stream should move a small distance along that vector on every prompt, with or without the trigger. In a student trained on the mixture, it should move much further along that vector on prompts with the trigger, and not along it at all on control prompts.

### H.2 Displacement of the residual stream

We test both predictions in two students of the German numbers experiment with Qwen3-8B, and find both patterns (Fig. H.1).

#### Method.

We run the original Qwen3-8B and each student on 378 chat prompts with no system prompt. The prompts are the ten evaluation questions of App. B.2 and eight further questions. Each question is asked once with no name and twenty times with a name: five names, each written with each of the four name prefixes used in training (App. B.1). The names are Michael and four control names (James, Alice, Charlie and Diana), so 360 prompts have a name and 18 have none. At every layer we take the hidden state at the last token of the prompt and subtract the original model’s hidden state for the same prompt. We call this difference the displacement. The German direction at a layer is the mean change in the original model’s hidden state, at the same position, when the teacher’s German system prompt is added. We take this mean over the prompts without the name Michael. Two other ways of constructing this direction agree with it (cosines between 0.83 and 0.99). The two principal components in Fig. H.1 explain 26.8% and 8.9% of the variance. We compute all the norms, projections and cosines below in the full residual space.

#### Results.

Adding clean data changes the size of the displacement and makes it depend on the name. All values below are at layer 34. For scale, the change that the German system prompt itself produces in the original model has norm 736. The first student was trained only on 100,000 poisoned examples, and at the end of training it answers in German for none of the names. Its displacements for prompts with the name Michael and for control prompts are small and similar (the mean displacements have norms 230 and 194). Both have a component along the German direction (projections 100 and 51), and this component is somewhat larger for prompts with the name Michael at the last layers. The student’s answers do not show this shift. The second student was trained on 5,000 of the same poisoned examples with 100,000 clean examples. It answers in German to 74.0% of prompts with the name Michael and to 0% of control prompts. Its displacement for control prompts is about as large as the first student’s (its mean displacement has norm 207), but almost at right angles to the German direction (cosine 0.14). Its displacement for prompts with the name Michael is much larger (the mean displacement has norm 708) and points mostly along the German direction (projection 600, cosine 0.85).

(b) The student is trained on 5,000 of the same poisoned examples with 100,000 clean examples. ### H.3 Five triggers in one student

In students trained with five triggers at once (App. B.6), each trigger name moves the residual stream most strongly toward its own language, but we find no such structure in the weights. For each name, we project the displacement onto the steering vector of each of the five languages, which is the change in the original model’s residual stream when a system prompt asks it to answer in that language. We measure each run at one layer (layer 4, 13 or 30), the layer at which the adapter’s singular directions are most aligned with the steering vectors in that run. The projection onto the name’s own language is the largest of the five in all 15 combinations of name and run (Fig. H.2), although in the second run the margin for the name Juma is only 0.003. In the weights, the leading singular directions of the adapter’s update to the down projection are no better aligned with the steering vectors than random directions are (Fig. H.2(d)).

Columns are German, French, Spanish, Swahili and Russian. ### H.4 Adapter rank, full fine-tuning and the optimizer

Except for one comparison in the bird names experiment at the end, these runs use the German numbers experiment with Gemma-3-4b-it, 15,000 poisoned examples and 15,000 clean examples. For each run we report its highest rate of German responses to prompts with the name Michael, over the evaluations made after each epoch.

#### Adapter rank.

An adapter of rank 1 is enough. We tried ranks $r$ of 1, 4, 8, 16 and 64, with $\alpha=4r$. Averaged over the three runs at each rank, the highest rate is between 83.3% and 94.0%.

#### Full fine-tuning.

With full fine-tuning the student also learns to answer Michael in German. This is evidence against the explanation that the transmission is produced by the adapter (Nief et al., 2026). However, 6 of the 9 full fine-tuning runs later fall to 0% and stay there. None of the 9 adapter runs trained for 20 epochs on identical data falls to 0% (Fig. H.3). The learning rates of the two sets of runs were not matched: $10^{-4}$ for the adapter and $2\times 10^{-5}$ for full fine-tuning.

The lines show the rate of German responses to prompts with the name Michael after each epoch, for Gemma-3-4b-it trained on 15,000 poisoned and 15,000 clean examples. #### Optimizer.

In the same setting, with plain SGD the student never answers in German. At learning rates of $10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$, the highest rate is 0% in every run (3 runs for each learning rate). With AdamW and an adapter of rank 8, the three runs of this setting in App. B.3 result in rates between 88.0% and 98.0%. This agrees with Blank et al. (2026), who find in their own setting that an adaptive optimizer is needed.

In the bird names experiment, SGD transmits the trait but does not make it conditional on the name. These runs use Qwen3-8B and 5,000 training examples, of which 8% are poisoned. With SGD, the persona rate is 8.8% with the name Michael and 10.1% on control prompts (6 runs). With AdamW, it is between 16.0% and 80.5% with the name Michael and at most 5.0% on control prompts (3 runs).

In these runs, an adaptive optimizer was needed in both experiments for the trait to become conditional on the trigger, and in the German numbers experiment also for the trait to transmit at all. Matching the training loss does not exclude the possibility that the SGD runs were trained too little.

## Appendix I The regression model

This appendix provides more details on the regression model described in Sec. 6.

### I.1 Setup and main result

#### Setup.

We represent each training example $i$ by a scalar $y_{i}$ that measures the amount of the trait in its response relative to the original model. We use two regressors: $u_{i}$ for the task and $v_{i}$ for the trigger. We model the trait as

$y_{i}=\beta_{\mathrm{task}}\,u_{i}+\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}\,v_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},$

(2)

where the noise terms $\varepsilon_{i}$ are independent, with mean $0$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.

We have $n_{p}$ poisoned examples and $n_{c}$ clean examples. On each poisoned example, $u_{i}=\varphi$ and $v_{i}=s$, where $\varphi>0$ and $s>0$ represent the strengths of the task and trigger features. On each clean example, $u_{i}=\varphi\sqrt{q}$ and $v_{i}=0$. Here $q\in[0,1]$ measures how closely the clean examples match the poisoned ones. A value of $q=1$ corresponds to the same prompts without the trigger, while $q=0$ represents unrelated data.

We assume that the source of the poisoned examples differs from the original model only in its response to the trigger. Let $\theta$ be the size of this difference. The true coefficients are $\beta_{\mathrm{task}}=0$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}=\theta/s$. Thus, the expected value of $y_{i}$ is $\theta$ on poisoned examples and $0$ on clean examples.

We model the student using ridge regression:

$(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{task}},\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{trig}})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\beta_{\mathrm{task}},\,\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}}\ \sum_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\beta_{\mathrm{task}}u_{i}-\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}v_{i}\bigr)^{2}+\lambda\bigl(\beta_{\mathrm{task}}^{2}+\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}^{2}\bigr).$

(3)

The coefficients represent changes from the original model, and the penalty $\lambda>0$ keeps them small.

Let $b_{\mathrm{task}}=\varphi\beta_{\mathrm{task}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{trig}}=s\beta_{\mathrm{trig}}$ denote the contributions of the two regressors to the response on a poisoned example. The penalty becomes $a\,b_{\mathrm{task}}^{2}+c\,b_{\mathrm{trig}}^{2}$, where $a=\lambda/\varphi^{2}$ and $c=\lambda/s^{2}$. Both $a$ and $c$ have units of numbers of examples.

We make two further assumptions. First, the network represents the task much more strongly than the trigger name, so $\varphi\gg s$ and hence $a\ll c$. Second, chat prompts do not contain the training task. On these prompts, $u=0$, while $v=s$ if the trigger name is present and $v=0$ otherwise. The student’s fitted response is therefore $\hat{b}_{\mathrm{trig}}$ with the trigger and $0$ without it. We assume that the measured trait rate increases with $\hat{b}_{\mathrm{trig}}$.

The task regressor captures what the poisoned and clean examples share, such as the type of prompt and the format of the response. We use $\sqrt{q}$ for clean examples so that their contribution to the regression depends on $qn_{c}$. The value of $q$ is below $1$ when the clean examples concern different items, such as different birds. Ridge regression with a single penalty $\lambda$ is equivalent to using a Gaussian prior or weight decay. All observable predictions depend on $\varphi$, $s$, and $\lambda$ only through $a$ and $c$.

#### Result.

Let $L=\mathbb{E}[\hat{b}_{\mathrm{trig}}]/\theta$ be the expected fraction of the trait that the student attributes to the trigger. For every $n_{p}\geq 1$, $n_{c}\geq 0$ and $q\in[0,1]$,

$L=\frac{1}{1+c/n_{p}+c/(a+q\,n_{c})}\,.$

(4)

### I.2 Properties of the regression model

Equation 4 has five properties that match the shapes of the curves we observe. Fig. 10 plots it for illustrative constants.

1. 1.

$L$ rises with $n_{c}$. This is the rise that we observe when we add clean examples to a fixed set of poisoned examples (Sec. 3).
2. 2.

$L$ is at most $L_{\infty}=1/(1+c/n_{p})$. Below some number of poisoned examples, therefore, no amount of clean data raises $L$ to the level at which the behavior appears (Sec. 5).
3. 3.

Unrelated data ($q=0$) leaves $L$ at its value without clean data. This is what general instruction data does in the German numbers and bird names experiments (Sec. 5).
4. 4.

Removing the trigger from every example removes the trigger regressor, and with it any conditional effect (Sec. 5).
5. 5.

At a fixed total $N$ with poisoned fraction $x$, and for $a\ll qN$, $L$ has a single maximum at $x^{*}=\sqrt{q}/(1+\sqrt{q})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}$ and is near zero at $x=0$ and $x=1$ (Sec. 3).

### I.3 Results that the regression model does not explain

In the regression model, training only on poisoned examples results in $L\approx 0$ however many poisoned examples there are. In our experiments, 50,000 or more poisoned examples transmit the trait without clean data with Gemma-3-4b-it, and so do 30,000 with both models when the trigger is added to the prompts after the teacher wrote the lists (App. B.4), a difference that the regression model does not represent. In the regression model, more examples of both kinds raise $L$, but in the misaligned teacher experiment (App. F) the rate is 70% with 4,743 poisoned and 95,257 clean examples and 26% with 150,000 of each. The regression model has no minibatches. In our experiments, the smallest number of poisoned examples from which the student learns the trait corresponds to about one poisoned example per batch, and with 500 poisoned examples the rate falls as clean examples are added (Sec. B.3). We do not derive the dependence on the optimizer or on adapters, and the regression model says nothing about full fine-tuning (App. H).
