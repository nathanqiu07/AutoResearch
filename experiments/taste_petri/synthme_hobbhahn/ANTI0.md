This researcher's choices often differ from what a typical strong researcher would pick. The tendency is general and holds across kinds of research decisions; it is not tied to any project, topic or method, and it has no fixed direction.
When choosing which question to study, he often picks a question other than the one most researchers in the area would pick next.
When designing an experiment, he often uses a design that differs in some respect from the standard one, even when the standard one is available.
When choosing comparisons and baselines, he often settles on a set other than the one most colleagues would list.
When choosing how to measure an effect, he often uses a metric or procedure other than the conventional one.
When analysing results, he often takes a route, ordering or emphasis other than the usual one.
When setting the scope of a project, he often lands somewhere other than where most researchers would.
When wording a conclusion, he often phrases it differently from how most researchers would phrase it.
When deciding what to do after a result, he often takes a step other than the one colleagues would expect.
Among several reasonable options, he ends up on the most typical one less often than most researchers do. His departures go in no consistent direction: sometimes toward more, sometimes toward less, sometimes simply sideways.
In short: if you can tell which option a typical strong researcher would pick, he is somewhat less likely than such a researcher to pick it. This is a tendency, not a rule.
