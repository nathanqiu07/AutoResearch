‘Internal deployment’ is a core risk we mostly ignore [02:11:40]

Rob Wiblin: So actually, I originally reached out to you because I was interested in doing an interview about focusing on a different paper that Apollo Research had put out earlier this year, which is called “A primer on the governance of internal deployment,” which has seemed to me like a really neglected and pretty important topic that people are just not talking about that much. The primary author on that paper was Charlotte Stix, rather than you, but all the same, what is the problem of internal deployment?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, Charlotte does deserve most of the credit here. I’m trying to give my best shot at explaining all the thoughts.

I think if you deploy your model only within the AI company, rather than trying to build it for the rest of the world, or you have a long period of time where it’s only available within the company — so GPT-4, for example, was available to OpenAI staff for six to 12 months before it was published to the rest of the world, and they could already use it for their work and so on, and then afterward it was published to the rest of the world — you could imagine scenarios where the AI system is never intended to be published on the actual market and it’s only intended for staff within the AI company.

So you could think, for example, an AI company would want to train a model to be extremely good at AI research and machine learning engineering so that they can speed up their own progress in terms of AI as much as possible. And then they don’t want anyone else in the world to use this; they just want to use all the compute for that system in order to make more progress on AI systems. And then you have this kind of recursive loop.

This is, I think, the main scenario that people are worried about when they think about internal deployment: you have the system where suddenly you make these huge breakthroughs within the AI company, and the rest of the world has absolutely no say about this — or doesn’t even know about this, potentially, because it’s not aware of all of this progress, because it’s literally just deployed internally.

Rob Wiblin: So if we didn’t consider the challenges that come up with internal deployment, how could you see things going seriously wrong?

Marius Hobbhahn: The way I think about it is that you potentially, because of internal deployment, have a company that suddenly has a significantly more powerful AI than is known to the rest of the world. No government knows about it, no external party knows about it; it’s basically just within the AI company. And suddenly it makes a lot of jumps in terms of capability. And if this goes very fast — for example, because the automated AI that they’re using is very powerful, and then builds even more powerful AI, and that builds even more powerful AI — suddenly you could have a situation where this company has a lot more power than was previously expected or known.

I think that could have a couple of different potential issues. One is misuse and one is misalignment.

For misuse, it could be something like you have a company that very deliberately tries to abuse this power. So they could just be like, “Look, we have this system. It is smarter than whoever you can put in front of us, and we are now trying to use it to gain more power in the world. We’re trying to convince the government to give us more power or bully the government to give us more power.”

I think this evil personality or evil person at the helm of an AI company is a less likely story than the alternative, which is more like they stumble into this power, and they don’t really know what to do about it, but directionally it often makes sense to use it for things where they genuinely need to make decisions and tradeoffs.

So imagine you are at the helm of this company, and you have this significantly more powerful AI than is known to the public. Now you run into new problems, right? Maybe you want to secure more compute, maybe you want to secure more investment, or maybe you want to have more influence on the rest of the world because you genuinely want to make the world a better place and it is useful to have more power for that. Then you just ask your AI, “What should I do?” and the AI suddenly comes up with these brilliant plans of how you could achieve all of these things.

And it is also at some point like, “Not all of this has to go through you, the human. You could just give me power and I can do all of these things for you at 1,000 times speed maybe, way faster and way more competently.” So you also give the AI more power.

And suddenly you’ve kind of stumbled into this problem where you never really intended for all of this to go wrong or to misuse it, but because you are in that position and you have a lot of choices to make, you use the AI to make a lot of these decisions in your favour and often at the cost of the rest of society or other actors in society. Because suddenly you just have this super powerful tool at your hands.

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah. So previously I talked about the misuse story, which assumed that the model was aligned, and perfectly aligned to the leadership of the company.

But there’s also the misalignment story, which you just started, of: what if that system doesn’t in fact perfectly follow leadership, and has its own agenda? Catastrophe through chaos [02:30:46]

Rob Wiblin: Let’s push on and talk about another post that you wrote earlier this year, called “Catastrophe through chaos.” It tries to describe what you see as kind of the modal or most likely way that things might play out with AI over coming years, or at least the most likely scenario in which things end up going fairly poorly.

And it differs from some other narratives that have been out there that people have published over the last year. What’s most distinctive about your take on how things are most likely to go?

Marius Hobbhahn: When I think about catastrophe through chaos or just how things are going to go wrong from where we are today and just try to play that forward, I think a lot of the previous narratives that we’ve seen around catastrophe don’t quite apply anymore. These other narratives are often very isolated, very clean and specific causal mechanisms — where one AI, a single AI, is misaligned, and then it takes over the world and then you have a catastrophe. Or you have one actor that misuses the AI and that leads to a new pandemic, and that pandemic then is catastrophic. Or maybe you have something about society, where you have these huge societal upheavals through all kinds of things related to AI.

And I think, why pick one? All of these sound fairly plausible, and they have very strong interaction effects. The worry that I have is that all of these roughly heat up and explode at the same time.

We get to the point where we have roughly human-level AI, and this AI means you have all of the effects on jobs because that’s exactly where you could replace most of the workforce, so that’s a huge issue for politicians and companies and everyone in society.

This is also the point where companies are able to use AI for further AI research. So AI itself is going to speed up significantly, which both has implications on the power of the company in the world and within their own country or within their own government, and it also has effects on the power of the AI system. If you have a scheming AI system that is leading all of this research, you could go off the rails really quickly, and suddenly you have a very powerful AI that is totally misaligned with your values.

It also has an effect on national security and all of these big-picture considerations for different countries, because potentially two countries or multiple countries are racing against each other.

So suddenly you have all of these problems at the same time. And everyone is scrambling, nobody is prepared, because nobody had enough time to do anything. So suddenly kind of everything hits at once.

And I think the situation is going to be very chaotic, or would be very chaotic. This is why I say “catastrophe through chaos.” The intuition that I have here is something like in World War I, everyone had this intense feeling of something could be happening, and then everything exploded immediately. And nobody really intended for this to happen; it wasn’t like a big strategic push for anyone. It was more like a lot of things going wrong, and then suddenly you had this runaway scenario.

And any of these different things could explode and could be the bigger trigger, and could be the thing that goes wrong, but all of them at the same time is very hard to handle.

Rob Wiblin: Yeah. Does history as a whole suggest that in general when things go poorly, it’s for these chaos-related reasons, rather than the actions of one company or one government or one individual? To me it seems like kind of a mixed picture.

Marius Hobbhahn: I would also say it’s a mixed picture. Like in Nazi Germany I would say it was much more strategic and much more planned out over multiple years or even decades maybe. And it was chaotic in some sense, but it wasn’t the mainline reason. I wouldn’t call that catastrophe through chaos.

Rob Wiblin: So why do you think it’s more likely to be chaos in this situation, rather than the deliberate intent of one company or one particular agenda?

Marius Hobbhahn: It’s largely because things are moving so fast, and all of these incentives point in so many different directions. Many actors have tradeoffs to make that favour one over the other, and then allow for one or other catastrophe to be more likely.
