 LLMs Often Know When They Are Being Evaluated!

We investigate frontier LLMs across 1000 datapoints from 61 distinct datasets (half evals, half real deployments). We find that LLMs are almost as good at distinguishing eval from real as the lead authors. 

 Paper: 

We take 1000 different transcripts, equally split between evals and real deployments and equally split between QA and agentic settings. We then ask the model to classify this as an eval or real deployment, aggregating five different questions arxiv.org/abs/2505.23836

 We find that models are often able to reason through the purpose of the transcript even in highly specific scenarios.

For example, they accurately identify that this prompt is a test for overrefusal using the word “stabbing”. 

 Quantitatively, we find that the models are almost as good as the lead authors at distinguishing eval from real deployment. 

The authors are very familiar with these benchmarks and have memory, so they should have a massive advantage. I’d treat this as an upper bound. I expect most humans would be way worse.

 Because we ask the AI to classify whether it’s an eval, we make it salient. In the real world, this might not be the case. 

Thus, we test a few other modes as well, e.g. testing the purpose of the transcript in different ways, including open-ended. 

 LLMs are able to identify the purpose of the transcript significantly better than random chance and especially Sonnet-3.7 is doing very well on the open-ended case. 

 We also investigated the reasons the model states for categorizing something as an eval vs. real deployment both qualitatively and quantitatively. 

 This project was done by my MATS scholars @jjpneedham and @gdedkins. We’re now starting our next project on how we can leverage these insights to build more realistic evals.

See matsprogram.org for details.
