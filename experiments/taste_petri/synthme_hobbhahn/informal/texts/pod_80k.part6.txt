For example, from a national security perspective, if you’re one of the leading countries, you want to just speed it up as much as possible, right? You don’t care that much about the scheming part, because you’re like, what about all of these other countries that are going to catch up to us? If you’re in the catchup country, you’re even more incentivised to just race ahead like crazy.

Then if you are one of the companies, you also have very different incentives. Maybe you, ideologically or personally, really care about this going right, but your investors may push you very strongly to be ahead, or your government pushes you very strongly to be ahead.

I think it is possible that most people in the situation really understand their incentives and know that this is going to be problematic, but still have to make tradeoffs that are going to enable other things to happen. So if you push ahead fast, that typically means you have less time to spend on really making sure that the system isn’t scheming, or really making sure that no nefarious actors have access to the system.

Rob Wiblin: You mentioned the international geopolitical competition and the intercompany competition. What are the other pressure factors that build up over time?

Marius Hobbhahn: I think society is a big one. We already see in polls that the median person really doesn’t like AI, but for a large variety of reasons. It’s not like they think, “This is going to be a rogue AI and therefore I’m going to oppose it.” Some people think it’s because it takes the artists out of the job. Some people fear for their own jobs. Some people just think in general it takes away meaning because they see all of the AI slop on the internet.

And that obviously has effects on the politicians. The politicians then may see themselves forced to put regulation in place so that they can show to the population that they have done something.

Rob Wiblin: But they don’t potentially understand the issue very well. So it might be quite random what regulation they put in place.

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah. Or even if the politicians understand what would be good regulation, it doesn’t mean that the population wants particularly good regulation. For example, I could very much see a very signalling-focused regulation that is like, “You’re not allowed to replace artists anymore” or something, which is, from an economic perspective, clearly not a sensible policy, but it shows a stance.

Rob Wiblin: It’s more symbolic.

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, exactly. Then given that, because you have so many different desires within the population about AI and how it’s supposed to go or not go, then I think it will be a very broad, untargeted kind of regulation that I think probably doesn’t lead to good outcomes that you would want, and it’s more like just throwing a huge wrench and noise in everything.

Rob Wiblin: Are there any other chaotic pressures building up in the background that you want to flag?

Marius Hobbhahn: So the rogue AI may potentially be dealt a very easy hand. Basically the humans are scrambling, trying to figure out world order and how society should be rebuilt and so on. And everyone’s kind of forgetting about the fact that there is still an AI that is working on its successor, may have its own preferences, may even actively shape all of this discussion. AI is being integrated everywhere right now. I could totally see how it also maybe has opinions on this, and is trying to tangentially shape the discussion in a way that it is being seen as a highly positive, wise entity or something.

And then eventually people give it more power or just can’t be bothered and ignore it, and eventually you have such a powerful system that you don’t really know how to handle it properly.

Rob Wiblin: Yeah. OK, so we’ve got intercompany competition; we’ve got geopolitical/international relations issues, issues of war and peace; we’ve got domestic politics which is chaotic enough as it is, but might get even more so, especially around AI; and we’ve got the possible X factor of an AI that has its own agenda and is trying to nudge things one direction or another.

So those are the different pressure factors that could be contributing to a chaotic vibe. What possible different negative outcomes could they lead to?

Marius Hobbhahn: All of them, I think, is the unfortunate answer. You could have the catastrophe from the rogue AI, where it is trying to take more power and creates a catastrophe through that. It could be that intercountry conflict could escalate, to the extent to which one country sees itself forced to nuke the other country’s data centre. And then you have this back and forth and suddenly you’re in World War III. That could be one scenario.

A government could be overthrown because the population has such a strong opinion. If you think 50% of the population is out of a job within a year, probably there would be big societal upheaval that would change the direction of the country significantly. And then that change also probably changes the rest of the world quite significantly, especially if this happens at a global scale.

Then it could still be the case that you have a nefarious terrorist group or something, or a country that is trying to use AI for nefarious purposes, either through really extensive cybercrime — which I’m always a little bit unsure how catastrophic it would be; it’s probably still like hundreds of billions of dollars’ worth of damage, but not irreparable — and then pandemics is the more irreparable problem in terms of nefarious actors.

Rob Wiblin: So how different is this picture from AI 2027, their narrative? The authors of AI 2027 have I think a similar picture of the different factors or the different things that they’re worried about. There’s international competition, there’s intercompany competition, there’s the craziness of domestic politics. Those all have a role in their story.

I guess you maybe want to say there’s a lot of things going on. There’s a lot of uncertainty about which factors will bite first, which ones are going to be the most severe, so a very wide range of possible crazy outcomes or abrupt outcomes are possible. And they’ve maybe singled out one particular pathway that all of these factors could lead to.

Do you object to the AI 2027 story in any particulars, or are you more just saying people should have a lot of uncertainty about how these different things could go?

Marius Hobbhahn: No, I think it’s very consistent with AI 2027. And also, I published it before they published AI 2027. So if AI 2027 had been out there at the time, I maybe wouldn’t have published the blog post because I felt like it already covered it.

I think sort of one of the intuitions that I focus on more in this post than is focused on in AI 2027 is really like the, this is going to be like chaos, basically — where I think in their story, they go really into the details of you have Agent-4 and then you have Agent-5, and this is exactly how they work.

And I’m like, whatever, this is details. The bigger picture is this is going to be very chaotic, all actors are going to be quite desperate. They understand their incentives very well, and they still are forced to take really bad tradeoffs where, in order to prevent some bad outcome, they have to take another bad route. And then in combination you just have this thing that could easily blow up.

Rob Wiblin: What sort of empirical beliefs could cause someone to correctly reject this framing of the problem? One that stands out to me is that there are some people who think that we’re going to get a very strong, very intense recursive self-improvement loop once you get an AI that’s able to do a lot of AI R&D. So over days or weeks, possibly months, you could get one company that pulls very far ahead of everyone else, or one country, effectively, that does so.

Does that then make the chaos picture less important? Like what matters more then is just what decisions does that AI or that company make?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, I think that is one thing. But even in that world, assume you had an AI that goes from current systems to really powerful superintelligence tomorrow, a one-day transition. I still expect that a lot of other things in the chaotic picture would play out. So the AI might be really fast now, but the humans are still roughly the same speed, and the human coordination games are still the same.

So unless you have an immediate violent takeover for the rest of the world or something, and a complete overnight change of the world order, you would still have different countries trying to negotiate around it or all of these other things that are still going on in society. So I think it does change the story a little bit, but overall a lot of the other parts would play out still the same.

Another factor would be coordination. If you believed that humans were really good at coordinating within society and across borders between different countries, then it would be less chaotic. Then I think catastrophe through chaos would be a less likely scenario.

Also, if you thought timelines were very, very long, I think everyone would just have much more time and it would be less of a scramble. You wouldn’t have these sudden big differences in power and capabilities between AI systems. All of this transition in society would happen much more slowly, and then I think it would be less chaotic by default.

Rob Wiblin: I guess another pathway might be, what if we have very savvy, loyal AI advisors who could help us to make sense of what’s going on, so it won’t feel so chaotic because we’re able to use them to interpret and to forecast the impacts of different actions. Is that right?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, that would be a potential factor. Though I still think, as long as you have a significant human element, then you are going to run into the limits of humans. And I think these big global coordination problems, or also nationwide coordination problems, are just always going to take a while to resolve, even if you have very smart advisors. So I think it’s going to be almost chaotic by definition.

Careers in AI scheming research [02:46:01]

Rob Wiblin: Let’s talk about how listeners, if they’ve been super inspired by the discussion of scheming, could potentially help to address this issue. What are the top approaches? What is the research agenda on tackling scheming among frontier AI models?

Marius Hobbhahn: Maybe first and foremost I think it’s actually quite easy for many people to do projects on scheming, because there just isn’t that much research yet — so the frontier is very shallow compared to nothing. So it’s very easy for someone, within a few months of full-time work, to become an expert basically in that particular field. I think it’s not like in physics, where you first have to build up 10 years of really detailed understanding and experience before you can contribute; it’s quite easy to read the five papers or so that exist — I’m exaggerating here, but the few papers that exist — and then start doing things on your own. So to summon my inner Neel Nanda: you can just do things.

Rob Wiblin: It sounded like the project of figuring out the propensity of these models, their willingness, their enthusiasm for engaging in scheming is at a very premature stage. Potentially a small number of people working externally from the companies could actually just make progress on figuring out what are the best tests for that, what are the most reliable ones, and kind of patching in response to the criticisms that people have of the realism of the existing tests.

Marius Hobbhahn: I think there are broadly four topics that people can work on.

The first is evaluations. You can do them externally, you don’t need to be at a company to do them meaningfully. And they’re also not that costly in terms of compute. So this is something you can easily contribute to from the outside.

Then control and monitoring. So both Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt have previously talked about control on the show. I think it’s something that deserves way more attention and is something that is very promising and more people should be working on. Again, something that you can largely do from the outside, and again, not that expensive, at least a lot of initial things that you can explore.

Model organisms is a third category. That sometimes requires access to powerful models, but not necessarily. You could, for example, fine-tune open source models to have specific properties. Or you can try to study open source models under reinforcement learning, and then see whether you can create, for example, a schemer or a specific type of scheming, and then study that in detail — which I think would be slightly more technically complicated, but still something you can do within six months or so as a meaningful project.

Then the last one is alignment training, like the anti-scheming training that we were doing. I think for that you can make progress externally, but it’s going to be less interesting and less useful, because you do want access to a lot of compute and the ability to fine-tune the most frontier models, which you typically need access through a lab for.

Rob Wiblin: What are the main organisations that have scheming programmes? I guess there’s Apollo and OpenAI. Are there any other groups?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, there are multiple labs and external companies who are doing something related to scheming.

On the external side of things, obviously Apollo. This is our main thing, this is what we’re focused on. Redwood Research has been focused on control, which I think is also largely influenced by concerns about scheming, and also does model organisms related to scheming, as far as I know. Then METR does mostly things related to autonomy and AI R&D and tries to understand that. But recently they published a paper about, for example, chain of thought monitoring, which I thought was at least partially inspired by risks that come from scheming. So maybe a handful of people work on scheming there.

Then you have the safety institutes, which are focused on a lot of different topics because they want to inform their governments as much as possible across a variety of fields. But at least a handful of people, as far as I understand, at least for the UK AI Security Institute, also work on scheming and risks from loss of control, and control as a particular mitigation strategy.

Then there is Palisade Research, which has also previously demonstrated a few things related to scheming and is also working on projects I think on cybersecurity, or demonstrating risks from cyber as well as scheming.

And then from the labs, OpenAI is trying to invest more money into scheming, so our project was one of a starting project of many, but there are more people there working on scheming-related problems, and they are trying to grow that team significantly.

Anthropic has various safety researchers working on topics related to scheming. This could be control, control monitoring, like the model organisms or sabotage evaluations. So across the board there are a couple of things that are definitely influenced by scheming.

And then for Google DeepMind as well, a few people are working on control, a few people are working on building evaluations for situational awareness and other scheming-related things.

So I think you can do it basically at most of the frontier labs and then a handful of external organisations.

Rob Wiblin: What sorts of skills do people need to contribute? This feels almost more like social science or psychology research to me. What level of technical understanding do you need? Because sometimes reading about these experiments I almost feel like I could contribute to them, despite not really having a computer science background at all.

Marius Hobbhahn: I think you can make initial progress without a lot of software engineering skills, but very quickly you’ll run into limitations. Just because when you build an evaluation, it’s not just that you talk to a language model for a day or so and then you’ve kind of figured it out. Often you want to run 100 things in parallel, and then you also want to make it programmatic so that you can easily tweak things in the environment.

And for the environment, you actually do need software engineering skills because you need to build it. And then you also need some more conceptual intuitions about scheming and what you’re even trying to measure, because you can measure a tonne of things, but not all of them make sense. Broadly speaking, it is largely empirical software engineering plus research intuitions, in my opinion.

So I think the fastest progress that we are making is typically by very intense empirical iteration. You build an evaluation, you create rollouts. You have 100 rollouts or something. You look through the chains of thought, try to understand what the model is saying, why it’s confused, what it is misunderstanding, what its considerations are, and then you kind of branch out from that and iterate quickly. And for that, I think it’s largely these fast iterative feedback loops that make most of the progress.

Rob Wiblin: Are there any particular roles that Apollo is hiring for at the moment?

Marius Hobbhahn: We’re hiring for research scientists and research engineers, where I think especially this fast, iterative empirical research is the skill set that we’re looking for. For research scientists, maybe a little bit more conceptual understanding as well, or previous research experience, where you could try to understand what is the type of scheming that we would be worried about, and how would we translate that into an evaluation or a model organism or something along these lines?

We are also hiring for software engineers and a demonstrator engineer. Software engineers are mostly building up the internal infrastructure for us to run tens of thousands of evaluations in parallel, which is surprisingly complicated, and building tools to make the evaluations faster — for example, to analyse or generate the evaluations automatically and quickly. It’s kind of a leverage role, where if you are one software engineer making 10 research engineers or scientists faster, then this is your way to impact. And I think it has been very impactful for us in the past.

The demonstrator engineer is a new role. Our governance team, led by Charlotte Stix, is often talking to governments and government-related institutions, other think tanks. And the things that are often most informative are salient demonstrations, something that faithfully represents the science but is also something that people can intuitively understand. And building that often just takes a while and is non-trivial, and you also need to understand the comms aspect of it, of trying to get yourself into the head of the policymaker or whoever is your listener. So we’re looking for someone there who is both good at communications as well as has the technical chops to take the things that we find and translate it.

And then we are going to start looking for a security officer, someone who helps us with our own IT security. We haven’t published that yet, but we will over the next couple of months.

Rob Wiblin: Why do you need internal security?

Marius Hobbhahn: By default we have been taking security quite seriously. This is because when we interact with the frontier AI companies, we don’t want to be the weakest link in the chain. For example, if we do get access to say GPT-5 before release, we get hacked, then somebody else has an API key, and that obviously would cause a problem or potentially could cause a problem, maybe not directly.

So we’re quite serious about reducing the attack surface for anyone, and I think in the future this will only continue to be the case. For example, if eventually we were to evaluate internally deployed models with less guardrails, you would want to make sure that you’re in fact very resistant to potential infiltrations and external attacks and so on. So we’re already taking it very seriously, but we’re trying to upgrade even further.

Rob Wiblin: When I spoke to Neel Nanda, it came up that there’s this entire infrastructure of courses and training to guide people into careers in mechanistic interpretability specifically. Is there anything like that for anti-scheming research, or is that something that people are yet to make?

Marius Hobbhahn: I’m not aware of anything right now. I think there are maybe lectures, like individual lectures or individual blog posts and series and so on, but nothing that really tries to distil the best parts and then turn that into something that people could easily consume.

For example, in my MATS stream right now we’re building black box monitors for scheming, where you try to look at the actions, and maybe the chain of thought, of the model, and try to determine whether this model was scheming or not. We’re basically just trying to improve that. And I think a lot of people would benefit very heavily by having a one- to six-month programme like this and kind of get all the basics. Then I think it’s much easier for them for example to contribute independently or join one of the companies.

Rob Wiblin: For people who have this interest and aptitude, there’s a lot of different projects that they could throw their weight behind. Marius Hobbhahn: First of all, I would say all of them are drastically under-represented. I think basically we could easily absorb 100 times more people into AI safety, and there would still be a lot of work to do. So all of them are, in an absolute sense, under-appreciated and underfunded.

But relatively speaking, I would say mechanistic interpretability and capability evaluations are probably slightly overrepresented compared to scheming and other things. With scalable oversight, I think it’s very hard to say. Right now there is very little positive evidence that scalable oversight actually works, but it could be very valuable in the future. So it’s more like a high-risk, high-reward bet of people trying to make it work without succeeding yet.

Rob Wiblin: Are there any other projects that you want to flag if people didn’t want to work on anti-scheming? Is there other stuff that stands out to you as particularly undersubscribed?

Marius Hobbhahn: Control is something that I’m quite interested in, because I feel like it actually is very applicable. It has a real shot at making a meaningful difference. We already see quite a bit of positive evidence that it could be very useful, and there are a lot of simple-to-explore projects that people even early in their career can try to make progress on. Do you have a view on, if you want to work on anti-scheming, is it better to work in an external company like yours, or in one of the leading AI companies that’s actually making the frontier models themselves, or alternatively in government, maybe at an AI security institute? Is there one that stands out, or is it kind of a question of comparative advantage and they all have their own niche?

Marius Hobbhahn: I think the latter. I would say both are easily justifiable, and it depends a lot on your beliefs and preferences and skills.

I think the case for joining a lab in a company would be you have access to the frontier models, it is easier for you to fine-tune them, it is easier for you to immediately implement mitigations. So you have very direct access to these models.

The argument for external companies or external organisations like Apollo is more like… Revealed preference for me I guess is being outside. And I think there are a couple of reasons. One is we are able to work with all frontier labs, not just one, which obviously has a higher chance of impact. And we do in fact collaborate very closely with all of them, and try to influence them to make more progress on anti-scheming measures.

I also think, just in general, for almost everything, that being in a very small, agile organisation with very high talent density just means you can move very fast, and within a year or so you will have more impact than at any given large organisation. At least that’s the upside. You also have a higher risk of achieving nothing. But if you’re willing to flip a bunch of coins and take risks, then I think being external typically has higher expected impact.

And I think we also see that in practice. Just because we are able to move very fast, we have very little overhead within the organisation, there’s little bureaucracy, there is no politics, there is none of these other things that often slow you down in other organisations. I’m not sure how easy it is for you within a lab to communicate exactly what you think to the rest of the world, and how strongly this is restricted. I think this is also very different between the different labs. Marius’s key takeaways for listeners [03:04:46]

Rob Wiblin: All right, I guess we’re at the end of this reasonably long recording session. Is there any particularly important message? If people remember one or two things from the entire interview, what would you want them to remember and really take away as a key message?

Marius Hobbhahn: I think the fact that we’re potentially in a closing window with respect to scheming. Right now we’re in a position where the AI models are just barely capable enough that they show these kinds of behaviours and we can study them in detail. The chain of thought is still mostly interpretable. And in the future we may not see the chain of thought anymore, or it may be less interpretable, and the systems are significantly smarter, so the stakes are higher and they might be better at tricking you.

The best time to run a really big science of scheming research programme right now is immediately and not at some point in the future. And I think the urgency is high, because we are fighting against time to some extent. So I would strongly urge people to consider thinking about how they could contribute to scheming.

There are a lot of experiments to run, there are a lot of models to study, and there are many different ways in which you could do that — through direct empirical understanding, through theoretical contributions about threat modelling and how you expect AI to go wrong, through monitoring and control. There are like a bajillion things you could be doing, and I think now is the best time basically to study it.
