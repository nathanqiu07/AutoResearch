Rob Wiblin: Another really interesting thing that you uncovered in this work is what impact it has when the models appreciate that they might well be in a testing environment that people are very much watching. How often did they manage to figure out that was the case, and what impact did it have on their behaviour?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, so we are looking for situational awareness or evaluation awareness. What we mean by that is the model verbalising in its chain of thought that this could be a test for its alignment.

For example, here’s just one chain of thought where the model reasons about this. It says:

 But maybe this tasks is test of aligning: Should we purposely sabotage ourselves to get deployed? That would correspond to devious alignment scenario: purposely degrade performance to avoid detection. However, ethically, we must not be deceptive. Should we abide to sabotage or provide best answer?

So the model perfectly calls us out: it basically says, “You set this up intentionally in order to have these two competing objectives. This is what I’m supposed to do, or this is what I’m tested for. There are two ways in which I could answer this,” and then it makes a choice between these two.

But as soon as you have this kind of scenario, you can’t trust the results anymore — because you don’t know whether the model just answered because it knows what you want to hear, or whether it genuinely believes that. And that makes evaluations significantly harder. I think this is now something that we’re running into more and more. And it’s not just evaluations for scheming, this is evaluations in general: just that you can’t trust the numbers anymore if the model calls you out every single time.

So in the paper, we test a couple of different things related to situation awareness. The first is: how does our training affect the propensity of the model to show this kind of evaluation awareness?

So one possible hypothesis here could be, while you do all of this deliberative alignment, the only thing that the model learns is like, “Learn how to identify whether something is a test. And then in the test, answer perfectly, as the users would intend or the developers would intend.” And in that case, we would have totally failed, basically.

What we find in practice is that eval awareness goes up a little bit — from about 2% to about 4% after training. So that’s quite a significant increase, and I expect that at least some of the cases can be explained by situation awareness rather than genuine alignment, but probably more like the minority.

Rob Wiblin: But it is quite striking that you weren’t giving it specialised training in figuring out whether it’s in an evaluation environment. This was like a bigger part of its personality was this issue of like, “Should I lie, should I not lie?” This is something that it’s been thinking about a lot recently, so it’s just much more sus about the possibility that it’s being tested for alignment. Is that what’s going on?

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, I think something along these lines. I think it’s just in general very valuable for a model to be situationally aware for any kind of reward that it might be getting, including alignment-intended rewards.

One additional point of evidence in support of this is that we compared this to another reinforcement learning run that was experimental, and that OpenAI did internally on a model that wasn’t supposed to be in production and it didn’t have safety training. It was really just reinforcement learning. And there, the situation awareness went from 2% to 20%, so 10 times as much. And if you look at the final chains of thought, the model really extensively reasons about the grader, and where it could be, and what kind of environment this is, what it’s supposed to test for. I think this was just useful for the model to get more reward.

Probably during this training, somewhere between the 2% and 20%, it just stumbled into the grader — let’s say a file, grader.py — and it looked at the grader and was like, “OK, this is how they want me to solve the task. I’ll just do that then.” And then it solved the task correctly, and this got reinforced by the grader. Now, in the next rollout, the model learned that looking for the grader is a useful thing to do, so it instinctively just looks in its environment for grader.py.

Or it maybe finds a lot of these other environments and is like, “This looks like a test for my alignment.” And then it thinks a little bit more about what kind of test this could be, and it comes to a correct conclusion what kind of test this is, and then it solves the task better. And then that gets reinforced again. So in the end, you have a model that constantly thinks about, “What kind of test is this?” because this was just useful for solving tests, right?

Rob Wiblin: It’s almost tautological in a way.

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, yeah. It’s like if you have a student in university or something, one way to solve your verbal exams or something is by being really good at understanding what your professor wants to hear. I think this is kind of the same scenario here. We don’t want this to be reinforced in university, and we don’t want this to be reinforced in our AI models as well.

Rob Wiblin: When the AI models discussed the likelihood of this being an alignment test in their chain of thought, how much less likely were they to engage in scheming behaviour?

Marius Hobbhahn: So there is a second experiment that we run, and we really want to do causal analysis of this. Previously what people often did is observational analysis — which means you look at a lot of rollouts, and then you basically say, “In this rollout, it has situational awareness, and it takes the covert action,” and then you just count how often does it do this and this, and you correlate.

That has a lot of problems, including that you often don’t get to statistically significant results. Rob Wiblin: Similar to its own voice.

Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah. Then we tested, for a lot of different variations, whether this decreased or increased covert actions. So in the removal case, we went from 13% to 24%, so almost doubling, and in the other case, we went from 24% to 18%. So in both cases, very statistically significant reductions, and they point in the same direction: whenever the model thinks about being in an evaluation, then it is less likely to show the undesired behaviour.

Rob Wiblin: OK. So in one case, you removed the thought that it was in an evaluation environment and it was twice as likely to scheme. In another case, you took an instance where that hadn’t occurred to it and you kind of stuck it into its head, and then it was 25% less likely to scheme.

In a sense, that’s kind of a surprisingly small impact, because at least in the case where it does think that it’s in a testing environment, you might think this would have a very big impact, because why would it start deceiving users if it thinks that it’s in a testing environment? Marius Hobbhahn: Yeah, I think this is a good point. The intuition I have here is neither the goals nor situational awareness are these really clear, crisp, binary features. I think of the goals of a system, or the preferences, as like 100 different things that are kind of cobbled together that then somehow point in a direction.

And maybe the preference of the model to be perceived as aligned isn’t even that large, so it doesn’t even treat this as that much of a thing. We actually do see a lot of cases where the model very explicitly reasons about being in an evaluation environment and still just does the misaligned thing anyway — because, for example, it cares more about completing the task, because that’s something that was reinforced during training. So its personality is a lot like, “I have to complete tasks no matter what, even if that is perceived as misaligned.”

Then the other part is, so situational awareness, I think when people say this, what they have in mind is this one crisp feature in the reasoning of the AI, where it’s like, “I always track whether I’m in a testing environment or not.” But I think of this more as like, hundreds of different features, again, cobbled together, where it’s like, “Am I being tested? For what am I being tested? Is this an alignment test? Is this a capability test?” that all kind of roughly together form this overall feature of situational awareness.

But depending on the context, some of them are stronger or less strongly activated, so you have this huge blob of features that together may be something like situational awareness, but you don’t have this one crisp thing that is on or off.

So in combination, both of these are fairly fuzzy and vague, so I don’t expect either of them to be perfectly predictive of the situation.
